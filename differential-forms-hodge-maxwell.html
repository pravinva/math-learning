Step 01 · Ground floor

What a derivative already is

You know: if \(f:\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}\), then \(f'(x)\) is the best linear approximation to how \(f\) changes near \(x\). In several variables,

\[ df_x(v) \;=\; \nabla f\cdot v \]

That is: the differential \(df\) takes a vector \(v\) (a little arrow: “which way am I probing?”) and returns a number (the directional rate of change).

Analogy
A vector is a finger pointing. A differential is a ruler held against that finger — it answers “how much change do I see along this direction?” Vectors push; forms measure.
Key shift
Calculus textbooks treat \(\nabla f\) as a vector. Differential geometry treats \(df\) as the more primitive object — a 1-form. The gradient vector is what you get after using a metric (dot product) to convert form ↔ vector.
Step 02 · Forms

Differential forms: measuring, not pointing

In \(n\) dimensions, a \(k\)-form is an object that eats \(k\) vectors and returns a number — alternating and linear in each slot. Think: “oriented infinitesimal \(k\)-volume measurement.”

0-form
A function \(f\). No vectors needed — already a number at each point.
scalar field
1-form
\(\alpha = P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz\). Measures directed length / circulation density.
like a covector field
2-form
\(\beta = A\,dy{\wedge}dz + B\,dz{\wedge}dx + C\,dx{\wedge}dy\). Measures flux through a tiny parallelogram.
like an area density
3-form
\(\gamma = \rho\,dx{\wedge}dy{\wedge}dz\). Measures volume density / charge density.
like a density
Kitchen analogy
  • 0-form: the temperature written on the oven display.
  • 1-form: a wind gauge — “how much air flows along this wire?”
  • 2-form: a window screen — “how much air passes through this pane?”
  • 3-form: a room humidity meter — “how much moisture is packed in this volume?”

In 3D, a \(k\)-form has \(\binom{3}{k}\) independent components — same counts as scalars / vectors / vectors / scalars. That coincidence is why vector calculus can hide the form language. In 4D spacetime the coincidence breaks — and forms become essential.

Step 03 · Algebra

Wedge product: stacking measurements

The wedge \(\wedge\) glues a \(p\)-form and a \(q\)-form into a \((p+q)\)-form. It is anticommutative on 1-forms:

\[ dx\wedge dy \;=\; -\,dy\wedge dx,\qquad dx\wedge dx \;=\; 0 \]

Geometrically: \(dx\wedge dy\) measures the signed area of the parallelogram spanned by two vectors, projected onto the \(xy\)-plane. Orientation matters — flip the order, flip the sign.

Step 04 · One derivative

Exterior derivative \(d\)

There is a single operator \(d\) that raises form degree by 1, generalizes the derivative, and satisfies \(d^{2}=0\). On a function (0-form):

\[ df \;=\; \partial_x f\,dx + \partial_y f\,dy + \partial_z f\,dz \]

On a 1-form \(\alpha = P\,dx + Q\,dy + R\,dz\):

\[ d\alpha \;=\; (\partial_y R - \partial_z Q)\,dy{\wedge}dz + (\partial_z P - \partial_x R)\,dz{\wedge}dx + (\partial_x Q - \partial_y P)\,dx{\wedge}dy \]

On a 2-form \(\beta = A\,dy{\wedge}dz + B\,dz{\wedge}dx + C\,dx{\wedge}dy\):

\[ d\beta \;=\; (\partial_x A + \partial_y B + \partial_z C)\,dx{\wedge}dy{\wedge}dz \]
Already recognizable
Those component formulas are exactly curl and div — written as forms. And \(d^{2}=0\) is the mother of \(\curl(\grad f)=0\) and \(\div(\curl V)=0\).
Stokes, unified
The fundamental theorem of calculus, Green, Stokes, and the divergence theorem are all one sentence: \[ \int_M d\omega \;=\; \int_{\partial M}\omega \] “The total infinitesimal change inside equals the measured boundary.”
Step 05 · Duality

Hodge star \(\star\): flipping dimensions

The exterior derivative alone is not enough for Maxwell or for “div of a vector field written as a 1-form.” You also need a way to turn \(k\)-forms into complementary \((n-k)\)-forms. That is the Hodge star, which uses the metric and orientation of space.

In \(\mathbb{R}^3\) with the usual orientation:

\[ \begin{aligned} \star\,dx &= dy{\wedge}dz, & \star\,dy &= dz{\wedge}dx, & \star\,dz &= dx{\wedge}dy,\\[4pt] \star\,1 &= dx{\wedge}dy{\wedge}dz, & \star(dx{\wedge}dy{\wedge}dz) &= 1. \end{aligned} \]

Intuition: \(\star\) answers “what complementary measurement completes this one to a full oriented volume?” A line direction dualizes to the perpendicular plane; a plane dualizes to its normal line.

Glove analogy
Think of filling an \(n\)-dimensional box. If you already occupy \(k\) dimensions of measurement, \(\star\) hands you the complementary \((n-k)\)-dimensional “glove” that completes the box. Same information, different packaging — line vs face, function vs volume density.
Step 06 · Vector calculus recovered

Unifying grad, curl, div

Identify vector fields with 1-forms via the metric: \(V^\flat = V_x\,dx + V_y\,dy + V_z\,dz\). Then the classical operators are compositions of \(d\) and \(\star\):

Classical In forms What it means
\(\nabla f\) \((\mathrm{d}f)^\sharp\) \(d\) on 0-forms; then raise index to a vector
\(\nabla\times V\) \((\star\,\mathrm{d}\,V^\flat)^\sharp\) \(d\) of 1-form → 2-form; \(\star\) → 1-form; raise
\(\nabla\cdot V\) \(\star\,\mathrm{d}\,\star\,V^\flat\) \(\star\) → 2-form; \(d\) → 3-form; \(\star\) → 0-form
Why this matters
Vector calculus identities become automatic: \(d^{2}=0\) implies curl∘grad = 0 and div∘curl = 0. You stop memorizing; you see a single algebraic machine.
Step 07 · Spacetime

Electric and magnetic as one 2-form \(F\)

In spacetime \((t,x,y,z)\), the natural home for electromagnetism is a single 2-form \(F\) — the Faraday tensor / electromagnetic field strength. Its six independent components are exactly the three of \(\mathbf{E}\) and the three of \(\mathbf{B}\):

\[ \begin{aligned} F \;=\; &\; E_x\,dt{\wedge}dx + E_y\,dt{\wedge}dy + E_z\,dt{\wedge}dz \\ &\;+ B_x\,dy{\wedge}dz + B_y\,dz{\wedge}dx + B_z\,dx{\wedge}dy \end{aligned} \]

(Units where \(c=1\); sign conventions vary by metric signature — the idea is what matters.)

Shadow analogy
Hold a bent wire in sunlight: from one angle the shadow looks like an “E-shape,” from another like a “B-shape.” Boost to a moving frame and the shadows remix — electric becomes partly magnetic — but the wire \(F\) is unchanged. That is why \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) mix under Lorentz transformations: they were never separate objects.

The Hodge dual \(\star F\) (now in 4D Minkowski spacetime) swaps the roles of \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) (up to signs): roughly \(\star F\) packages \((-\mathbf{B},\mathbf{E})\) the way \(F\) packages \((\mathbf{E},\mathbf{B})\). Duality is the geometric reason electric and magnetic equations look so similar.

Step 08 · The punchline

Maxwell: two equations instead of four

In vacuum (no charges, no currents), the four Maxwell equations

\[ \begin{aligned} \nabla\cdot\mathbf{B} &= 0, & \nabla\times\mathbf{E} + \partial_t\mathbf{B} &= 0,\\[4pt] \nabla\cdot\mathbf{E} &= 0, & \nabla\times\mathbf{B} - \partial_t\mathbf{E} &= 0 \end{aligned} \]

are exactly the component expansions of

\[ \boxed{\;dF \;=\; 0\;} \qquad\text{and}\qquad \boxed{\;d{\star}F \;=\; 0\;} \]
Form equation Unpacks to Classical name
\(dF = 0\) \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{B}=0\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf{E}=-\partial_t\mathbf{B}\) no magnetic monopoles + Faraday
\(d{\star}F = 0\) \(\nabla\cdot\mathbf{E}=0\) and \(\nabla\times\mathbf{B}=\partial_t\mathbf{E}\) Gauss (vacuum) + Ampère–Maxwell (vacuum)
With sources
If charge-current is a 3-form \(J\) (or equivalently a 1-form current), the inhomogeneous pair becomes \(d{\star}F = J\) (up to constants), while \(dF=0\) stays homogeneous — still the geometric statement that \(F\) is “closed,” often \(F=dA\) for a potential 1-form \(A\).
Why it feels inevitable
Once you accept that the field is a spacetime 2-form, Maxwell’s vacuum laws are almost the simplest nontrivial PDEs you can write with \(d\) and \(\star\): “\(F\) is closed, and so is its dual.” Symmetry between electricity and magnetism is Hodge duality. Unification of \(\mathbf{E}\) and \(\mathbf{B}\) is the 2-form. Unification of grad/curl/div was the warm-up.

If I get stuck, ask me which step failed and re-explain only that step with a new analogy.
