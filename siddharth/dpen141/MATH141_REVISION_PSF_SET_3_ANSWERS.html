Parametric + Special Functions Set 3 — Worked Answers

Advanced solutions: restrictions, singular cases, and proof steps are made explicit.
  1. Because $x^{2/3}=\cos^2t$ and $y^{2/3}=\sin^2t$, $$x^{2/3}+y^{2/3}=1,\qquad x\ge0,\ y\ge0.$$ Also $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{3\sin^2t\cos t}{-3\cos^2t\sin t}=-\tan t,$$ so at $t=\pi/4$ the gradient is $-1$. This is the first-quadrant arc of an astroid, with gradient $-1$ at $(2^{-3/2},2^{-3/2})$.
  2. $$x^2+y^2=\frac{(1-t^2)^2+4t^2}{(1+t^2)^2} =\frac{(1+t^2)^2}{(1+t^2)^2}=1.$$ The point $(-1,0)$ would require $2t/(1+t^2)=0$, hence $t=0$, but that gives $(1,0)$; no finite $t$ attains $(-1,0)$. As $t$ rises from $-\infty$ to $0$ to $\infty$, the point moves from just below $(-1,0)$ through $(1,0)$ and back toward $(-1,0)$ from above. The circle minus $(-1,0)$ is traced counterclockwise.
  3. Since $x^2=t^6$ and $y^3=t^6$, the Cartesian equation is $y^3=x^2$ with $y\ge0$. For $t\ne0$, $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2t}{3t^2}=\frac{2}{3t}.$$ Its magnitude tends to infinity as $t\to0^\pm$, so the limiting tangent is vertical: $x=0$. An upper-left and upper-right branch meet in a cusp at the origin with common vertical tangent.
  4. Since $x=t^2$, $$y=t^4-2t^2=x^2-2x,\qquad 0\le x\le4.$$ Each $x>0$ corresponds to $t=\pm\sqrt x$, and $y$ depends only on $t^2$, so both parameters give the same point. From $t=-2$ to $0$, the curve moves from $(4,8)$ to $(0,0)$; from $0$ to $2$, it retraces from $(0,0)$ to $(4,8)$. Every point except $(0,0)$ is attained twice on the stated interval.
  5. At $x=0$, continuity gives $c=0$, and matching derivatives gives $b=1$. At $x=1$, continuity gives $$a+b+c=2-3+2=1,$$ so $a=0$. The middle derivative at $1$ is $2a+b=1$, matching the right derivative $4(1)-3=1$. $a=0,\ b=1,\ c=0$.
  6. For $x<0$, $f(x)=(-1+A)x+B$, so the left derivative at $0$ is $A-1$. For $x>0$, $f(x)=(1+A)x+B$, so the right derivative is $A+1$. Equality would require $A-1=A+1$, or $-1=1$. No constants $A,B$ can make the function differentiable at $0$.
  7. Break at $x=\pm1$. If $x\le-1$ or $x\ge1$, solve $x^2-1=x+1$, giving $(x-2)(x+1)=0$, so the eligible roots are $-1,2$. If $-1\le x\le1$, solve $1-x^2=x+1$, giving $-x(x+1)=0$, so $x=-1,0$. Combining without duplication, $x=-1,0,2$.
  8. The left side is nonnegative, so we first need $x+2>0$. For a positive right side, $$-(x+2)<2x-1<x+2.$$ The left inequality gives $x>-1/3$; the right gives $x<3$. These already imply $x>-2$. $-\frac13<x<3$.
  9. The product $(x-1)(x+2)$ is positive outside its roots and negative between them. Therefore $$q(x)=\begin{cases}1,&x<-2,\\0,&x=-2,\\-1,&-2<x<1,\\0,&x=1,\\1,&x>1.\end{cases}$$ $q(x)=-1$ exactly when $x\in(-2,1)$.
  10. $$\begin{aligned} f'(x)&=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{(x+h)^3-2(x+h)-(x^3-2x)}h\\ &=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{3x^2h+3xh^2+h^3-2h}{h}\\ &=\lim_{h\to0}(3x^2+3xh+h^2-2)=3x^2-2. \end{aligned}$$ $f'(x)=3x^2-2$.
  11. At $0$, $$f'(0)=\lim_{h\to0}\frac{h|h|-0}{h}=\lim_{h\to0}|h|=0.$$ For $x>0$, $f(x)=x^2$, so $f'(x)=2x$. For $x<0$, $f(x)=-x^2$, so $f'(x)=-2x$. Both equal $2|x|$, including the value $0$ at the origin. $f'(x)=2|x|$ for all $x\in\mathbb R$.
  12. At $t=0$, $(x,y)=(1,0)$. Now $$\frac{dx}{dt}=e^t(\cos t-\sin t),\qquad \frac{dy}{dt}=e^t(\sin t+\cos t),$$ so $dy/dx=1$ at $0$. The tangent is $y=x-1$ and the normal is $y=-x+1$. Let $$m(t)=\frac{\sin t+\cos t}{\cos t-\sin t}.$$ At $0$, $dm/dt=2$ and $dx/dt=1$, hence $$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=\frac{dm/dt}{dx/dt}=2.$$ Tangent $y=x-1$; normal $y=-x+1$; $d^2y/dx^2=2$.
