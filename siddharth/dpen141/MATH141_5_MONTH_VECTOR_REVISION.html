How to use a five-month course

The course assumes coordinate arithmetic, algebraic rearrangement, Pythagoras, and basic sine, cosine and inverse trigonometry.

Starting vocabulary

A scalar is a number with magnitude. A vector has magnitude and direction. Its components are its signed amounts along chosen coordinate directions, and its norm \(\|\mathbf v\|\) is its length. A unit vector has length \(1\). Parallel vectors share a line direction. Perpendicular vectors meet at \(90^\circ\), written \(\mathbf u\perp\mathbf v\). A normal is perpendicular to a line, plane, or surface. Orientation records an ordered direction such as clockwise versus counterclockwise.

Spend one month revisiting each set. Read that month's concept primer before its questions, then begin each problem with a sketch and the expected output type.

Open a solution only after committing to a picture. When an answer surprises you, close it and explain the result aloud without symbols. Calculation is useful here, but explanation is the part that should survive five months.

Month 1

Vectors before coordinates

Arrows, bases, units, displacement and physical meaning

An arrow is the object; coordinates are its description

A vector represents a directed change. A column of numbers records its components in a chosen coordinate system. A free vector keeps its length and direction when its drawn arrow is translated. Position vectors remain attached to the chosen origin, and applied forces also carry a point or line of application.

Basis vectors are measuring directions

A basis is a collection of independent directions from which every vector in the space can be built in exactly one way. A plane needs two non-parallel basis vectors. Three-dimensional space needs three vectors that do not all lie in one plane.

The standard basis is \[ \mathbf i=(1,0,0),\qquad \mathbf j=(0,1,0),\qquad \mathbf k=(0,0,1). \] Thus \(3\mathbf i-2\mathbf j+\mathbf k\) means three steps along the first axis, two steps opposite the second axis, and one step along the third axis.

Symbols such as \(\mathbf e_1,\mathbf e_2\) name a different chosen basis. For example, \(\mathbf e_1=(1,1)\) and \(\mathbf e_2=(1,-1)\) point along diagonal directions. Writing \(\mathbf w=a\mathbf e_1+b\mathbf e_2\) says that \((a,b)\) are the coordinates of \(\mathbf w\) measured using those two diagonal directions. Basis vectors may have different lengths and need not meet at a right angle.

As a small example, let \(\mathbf f_1=(1,0)\) and \(\mathbf f_2=(1,1)\). Then \[ (3,2)=1\mathbf f_1+2\mathbf f_2. \] The geometric arrow \((3,2)\) therefore has coordinates \((1,2)\) in the \(\mathbf f_1,\mathbf f_2\) basis.

Components, length and unit direction

The vector from \(A\) to \(B\) is found by subtracting coordinates: \[ \overrightarrow{AB}=B-A. \] For \(\mathbf v=(v_1,v_2,v_3)\), its length is \[ \|\mathbf v\|=\sqrt{v_1^2+v_2^2+v_3^2}. \] When \(\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0\), division by its length gives the unit vector \(\widehat{\mathbf v}=\mathbf v/\|\mathbf v\|\). Multiplying a unit vector by a physical magnitude produces a vector with the required size and direction.

Addition, position, displacement and span

Vector addition joins successive changes tip to tail. Component addition gives the same final arrow. A scalar records a magnitude, while a vector records magnitude and direction. Distance is a scalar total along a path. Displacement is the vector from the starting point to the finishing point.

A position vector runs from the chosen origin to a point. A displacement vector runs from one point to another, so shifting the origin leaves the displacement unchanged. If the new origin has old-frame position \(\mathbf o\), then \[ \mathbf p_{\rm new}=\mathbf p_{\rm old}-\mathbf o. \]

Relative velocities use the same tip-to-tail rule: \[ \mathbf v_{A/C}=\mathbf v_{A/B}+\mathbf v_{B/C}. \] The subscripts state who moves relative to whom.

The span of \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) is the set \[ \operatorname{span}\{\mathbf u,\mathbf v\} =\{a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v:a,b\in\mathbb R\}. \] Two non-parallel vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) span a plane through the origin. Parallel vectors span one line. For example, \((2,-1,0)=2(1,0,0)-(0,1,0)\) belongs to the span of the first two coordinate directions, while \((0,0,1)\) lies outside that plane.

Every physical vector also carries units. Metres, metres per second and newtons describe different quantities, and the units remain attached to every component. Vector addition requires compatible physical dimensions. For constant velocity, \(\Delta\mathbf x=\mathbf v\,\Delta t\) converts velocity into displacement before the displacement is added to another displacement.

Set 1

Ten questions about geometry, meaning and calculation

1Two arrows are drawn: one from \(A=(1,2)\) to \(B=(4,6)\), and another from \(C=(-5,3)\) to \(D=(-2,7)\). Are they the same vector? Explain what information a vector remembers and what information it forgets.
Worked explanation

\(\overrightarrow{AB}=B-A=(3,4)\), while \(\overrightarrow{CD}=D-C=(3,4)\). They are the same vector.

A vector remembers a change: \(3\) units in the \(x\)-direction and \(4\) in the \(y\)-direction. It forgets where the arrow was drawn. A translated arrow can therefore represent the same velocity or the same free vector.

An applied force needs one extra piece of physical information: its point or line of application. Moving the arrow may leave the force vector unchanged while changing its turning effect on the body.

2A drone has velocity \(\mathbf v=6\mathbf i-2\mathbf j+3\mathbf k\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}\). What are \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\), and what physical story do the coefficients tell?
Worked explanation

The symbols are unit vectors along the chosen coordinate axes: \(\mathbf i=(1,0,0)\), \(\mathbf j=(0,1,0)\), and \(\mathbf k=(0,0,1)\). They are the three measuring directions of the coordinate frame.

Thus \(\mathbf v=(6,-2,3)\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}\): \(6\) metres per second in the positive \(x\)-direction, \(2\) in the negative \(y\)-direction, and \(3\) upward if \(+\mathbf k\) was chosen as up. The numbers mean nothing physically until the axes and units are declared.

3In a different basis, \(\mathbf e_1=(1,1)\) and \(\mathbf e_2=(1,-1)\). Express \(\mathbf w=(4,2)\) as \(a\mathbf e_1+b\mathbf e_2\). Explain how one geometric arrow can have two coordinate descriptions.
Worked explanation

We need \(a(1,1)+b(1,-1)=(a+b,a-b)=(4,2)\). Hence \(a+b=4\) and \(a-b=2\), giving \(a=3,\ b=1\).

So \(\mathbf w=3\mathbf e_1+\mathbf e_2\). Coordinates are instructions relative to a chosen set of measuring arrows. A new basis gives new instructions for the same geometric vector. It is like describing the same location using north/east directions or two diagonal streets.

4A walker goes \(3\) km east, \(4\) km north, then \(3\) km west. Find the total distance and the displacement. Why are these different kinds of quantities?
Worked explanation

The distance is the length of the travelled path: \(3+4+3=10\) km. The displacement is the net vector: \((3,0)+(0,4)+(-3,0)=(0,4)\) km, whose magnitude is \(4\) km.

Distance records how much ground was covered, so it is a scalar. Displacement records the single arrow from start to finish. Opposite pieces cancel because vectors retain direction.

5A cable's tension has magnitude \(50\) N and direction \((3,4,0)\). Find the force vector. Explain why multiplying by \((3,4,0)\) directly would give the wrong magnitude.
Worked explanation

The direction vector has length \(5\), so its unit vector is \(\widehat{\mathbf d}=(3/5,4/5,0)\). The force is \[ \mathbf F=50\widehat{\mathbf d}=(30,40,0)\ {\rm N}. \]

The vector \(50(3,4,0)\) would have magnitude \(250\) N because \((3,4,0)\) already has length \(5\). A direction vector tells us a direction, but only a unit direction lets the coefficient equal the physical magnitude.

6A boat moves through water with velocity \((5,1)\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}\). The current moves at \((-2,3)\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}\). Find the velocity seen from the shore and explain the addition geometrically.
Worked explanation

The shore velocity is \[ \mathbf v_{\rm shore}=\mathbf v_{\rm boat/water}+ \mathbf v_{\rm water/shore}=(5,1)+(-2,3)=(3,4)\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}. \]

During one second the boat moves by the first arrow relative to its patch of water, while the patch itself moves by the second arrow. Placing those arrows tip to tail gives the actual start-to-finish arrow. Vector addition is the geometry of successive changes.

7Can \(\mathbf e_1=(1,0)\) and \(\mathbf e_2=(2,0)\) form a basis for the plane? Test whether \((0,1)\) can be written as \(a\mathbf e_1+b\mathbf e_2\), and interpret the result geometrically.
Worked explanation

Every linear combination has the form \[ a(1,0)+b(2,0)=(a+2b,0). \] Its second component is always zero, so it can never equal \((0,1)\). Therefore these two vectors do not form a basis for the plane.

The two proposed directions lie on the same line. They can reach every point on the \(x\)-axis, but they cannot sweep across the plane. A planar basis needs two non-parallel directions.

8A map's new origin is placed at the old-frame coordinate \((10,-4)\). A point has old position vector \((13,2)\). Find its coordinates in the new frame. Does a displacement vector between two points change?
Worked explanation

The new origin has old coordinate \((10,-4)\), so the point is \((13,2)-(10,-4)=(3,6)\) relative to it.

The displacement remains unchanged. Subtracting the same translation from both endpoints cancels in \(Q-P\). Position vectors depend on the chosen origin, while a displacement records the difference between two points.

9Let \(\mathbf u=(1,0,1)\) and \(\mathbf v=(0,1,1)\). Decide whether \(\mathbf w=(2,3,5)\) and \(\mathbf z=(2,3,4)\) lie in the span of \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\). Interpret the span geometrically.
Worked explanation

A combination \(a\mathbf u+b\mathbf v=(a,b,a+b)\). For \(\mathbf w\), choosing \(a=2,b=3\) gives \(a+b=5\), so \(\mathbf w=2\mathbf u+3\mathbf v\).

For \(\mathbf z\), the first two components force \(a=2,b=3\), but then the third would have to be \(5\). Therefore \(\mathbf z=(2,3,4)\) lies outside the span. Two non-parallel vectors in \(\mathbb R^3\) sweep out a plane through the origin; membership in their span means lying in that plane.

10A calculation adds a displacement \((4,1)\) metres to a velocity \((2,-3)\) metres per second. The component arithmetic is possible. Why is the physical calculation meaningless, and how can units catch the mistake?
Worked explanation

The two lists have the same shape, but they measure different kinds of quantity. Metres cannot be added to metres per second, just as three seconds cannot be added to five kilograms.

Components retain physical units. A velocity could first be multiplied by a time interval to produce a displacement; only then could the vectors be added. Dimensional consistency checks physical meaning alongside arithmetic.

Month 2

Dot product as alignment

Angles, work, shadows, projections and closest points

The dot product is an alignment meter

For vectors with matching numbers of components, \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=u_1v_1+u_2v_2+\cdots+u_nv_n. \] The geometric form is \[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta \] when both vectors are nonzero. A positive value gives an acute angle, zero gives a right angle, and a negative value gives an obtuse angle. Dividing by both lengths produces the normalised alignment \(\cos\theta\), which lies from \(-1\) to \(1\). The endpoints mean opposite and matching directions respectively.

Scaling follows \[ (c\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf v=c(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v), \qquad \|c\mathbf u\|=|c|\,\|\mathbf u\|. \] For a unit direction \(\widehat{\mathbf t}\), the scalar component simplifies to \(\operatorname{comp}_{\widehat{\mathbf t}}\mathbf F =\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf t}\).

Scalar and vector projection

The scalar component of \(\mathbf u\) along a nonzero vector \(\mathbf v\) is the signed length \[ \operatorname{comp}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u =\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf v\|}. \] The vector projection turns that signed length into an arrow along \(\mathbf v\): \[ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u =\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\,\mathbf v. \] The remainder \(\mathbf u-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf v\). This remainder is called the residual. The decomposition writes one vector as an along-part plus a perpendicular part.

For example, projecting \(\mathbf a=(3,2)\) onto \(\mathbf b=(1,0)\) gives \[ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf b}\mathbf a=(3,0), \qquad \mathbf a-\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf b}\mathbf a=(0,2). \] The dot product \((3,0)\cdot(0,2)=0\) confirms the right angle.

Why projection gives the closest point

The perpendicular segment from a point to a line is the shortest route to that line. Therefore \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u\) is the closest point to \(\mathbf u\) on the line spanned by \(\mathbf v\). Pythagoras compares every other choice on the line with this perpendicular route. The algebraic identity \[ \|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2 =\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2-2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v \] connects dot products with Pythagoras.

Work uses the along-component of force

For a constant force \(\mathbf F\) and displacement \(\mathbf d\), \[ W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d=\|\mathbf F\|\,\|\mathbf d\|\cos\theta. \] Positive work assists the displacement, negative work opposes it, and a perpendicular force component contributes zero to the work. Force in newtons multiplied by displacement in metres gives joules: \(1\ {\rm J}=1\ {\rm N\,m}\).

Set 2

Ten questions about geometry, meaning and calculation

1Without finding any angles, classify each pair as acute, perpendicular, or obtuse: (a) \((2,1)\) and \((3,-1)\); (b) \((2,1)\) and \((1,-2)\); (c) \((2,1)\) and \((-3,1)\).
Worked explanation

The signs of the dot products settle the question: \[ (a)\ 2(3)+1(-1)=5>0,\quad (b)\ 2(1)+1(-2)=0,\quad (c)\ 2(-3)+1(1)=-5<0. \]

Because \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\cos\theta\), a positive dot product means acute, zero means \(90^\circ\), and negative means obtuse. The dot product is a signed test of directional agreement.

2For \(\mathbf u=(3,4)\) and \(\mathbf v=(4,0)\), compute the normalised alignment \(\dfrac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|}\). Interpret the value, then find the scalar component of \(\mathbf u\) along \(\mathbf v\).
Worked explanation

The dot product is \(12\), and the lengths are \(5\) and \(4\), so \[ \frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|} =\frac{12}{20}=0.6=\cos\theta. \]

This is a dimensionless alignment score: \(1\) means same direction, \(0\) means perpendicular, and \(-1\) means opposite direction. The score \(0.6\) is the cosine of the angle. The scalar component of \(\mathbf u\) along \(\mathbf v\) has length \(\|\mathbf u\|\cos\theta=3\).

3Let \(\mathbf u=(3,4)\) and \(\mathbf v=(4,0)\). Replace \(\mathbf u\) by \(10\mathbf u\). What happens to the dot product, the angle, and the normalised alignment? Explain before calculating.
Worked explanation

The dot product becomes ten times larger because \((10\mathbf u)\cdot\mathbf v=10(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)\). The length of \(\mathbf u\) also becomes ten times larger, so the factor cancels in the normalised ratio. The angle and alignment remain unchanged.

A raw dot product mixes two ideas: lengths and alignment. Dividing by both lengths removes scale and leaves only the geometry of direction.

4A constant force \(\mathbf F=(10,6)\) N moves an object through displacement \(\mathbf d=(3,-1)\) m. Find the work done and explain which part of the force contributes nothing.
Worked explanation

\[ W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d=10(3)+6(-1)=24\ {\rm J}. \] Work counts force in the direction of motion. Any component perpendicular to the total displacement \(\mathbf d\) has dot product zero with \(\mathbf d\), so it contributes zero to this net work calculation. What that perpendicular component does during the motion cannot be inferred from the endpoints alone.

The negative contribution \(6(-1)\) says the vertical component of force opposes the vertical part of the motion.

5A ramp's uphill unit vector is \(\widehat{\mathbf t}=(3/5,4/5)\). Gravity on a mass is \(\mathbf F=(0,-100)\) N. Find the signed scalar component of gravity uphill and interpret its sign.
Worked explanation

The scalar projection is \[ \mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf t} =0(3/5)-100(4/5)=-80\ {\rm N}. \]

The negative sign says the component points opposite the declared uphill direction, so gravity pulls \(80\) N downhill. Keeping the sign is more useful than reporting only a magnitude: it carries the directional decision.

6Decompose \(\mathbf u=(6,2)\) into a component parallel to \(\mathbf v=(1,1)\) and a component perpendicular to \(\mathbf v\). Explain the geometry of the two pieces.
Worked explanation

\[ \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u =\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\mathbf v =\frac{8}{2}(1,1)=(4,4). \] The leftover is \((6,2)-(4,4)=(2,-2)\), and \((2,-2)\cdot(1,1)=0\).

Thus \(\mathbf u=(4,4)+(2,-2)\). The first piece is the shadow along the line of \(\mathbf v\); the second is the shortest correction from that line to \(\mathbf u\).

7Two sensor readings are \(\mathbf a=(1,2,2)\) and \(\mathbf b=(2,4,4)\). Their raw dot product is large. What does their normalised alignment reveal that the raw number hides?
Worked explanation

Since \(\mathbf b=2\mathbf a\), the vectors have exactly the same direction. Their normalised alignment is \[ \frac{\mathbf a\cdot\mathbf b}{\|\mathbf a\|\|\mathbf b\|}=1. \]

The raw dot product grows when either reading is scaled, even if the directional pattern is unchanged. Normalisation separates “same pattern” from “large readings”, which is why the same geometry appears as cosine similarity in data analysis.

8Suppose nonzero vectors \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v\) satisfy \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0\). Derive the right angle from \(\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2\) and Pythagoras.
Worked explanation

Expand: \[ \|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2 =(\mathbf u-\mathbf v)\cdot(\mathbf u-\mathbf v) =\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2-2\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v. \] If the dot product is zero, this becomes \(\|\mathbf u-\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf u\|^2+\|\mathbf v\|^2\).

That is Pythagoras for the triangle whose sides are \(\mathbf u,\mathbf v,\mathbf u-\mathbf v\). The zero dot product is the algebraic fingerprint of a right angle. The nonzero condition matters because the zero vector has no direction and therefore no angle with another vector.

9A force of fixed magnitude \(F\) acts through a displacement of fixed length \(d\). Compare the work when the angle between them is \(0^\circ,60^\circ,90^\circ,120^\circ,180^\circ\). What does the sequence reveal about the dot product?
Worked explanation

Since \(W=Fd\cos\theta\), the five values are \[ Fd,\quad \frac12Fd,\quad 0,\quad-\frac12Fd,\quad-Fd. \] The sign and size record whether the force assists the displacement, has no component along it, or opposes it.

This sequence shows why work uses a dot product: work measures the signed component of force along the displacement.

10Why is \(\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u\) the point on the line spanned by a nonzero vector \(\mathbf v\) closest to \(\mathbf u\)? Give a geometric argument.
Worked explanation

Write \[ \mathbf u=\operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u+\mathbf r, \qquad \mathbf r\perp\mathbf v. \] Any other point on the line differs from the projection by some \(c\mathbf v\). Its error from \(\mathbf u\) is \(\mathbf r-c\mathbf v\).

Those two pieces are perpendicular, so Pythagoras gives \(\|\mathbf r-c\mathbf v\|^2=\|\mathbf r\|^2+c^2\|\mathbf v\|^2\). This is smallest at \(c=0\). Projection is the nearest-shadow construction used in closest-fit calculations. The condition \(\mathbf v\ne\mathbf0\) is required because the projection formula divides by \(\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v\).

Month 3

Cross product as oriented turning

Area, normals, torque, magnetic force and rotational velocity

The cross product is an oriented area vector

The vector-valued cross product used in this course takes two vectors in three dimensions. The result \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\) points perpendicular to the plane they span. Its magnitude is \[ \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\| =\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta, \] the area of their parallelogram. Parallel vectors produce zero area.

The right-hand rule fixes the direction

Curl the fingers of your right hand from the first vector toward the second through the smaller angle. Your thumb gives the cross-product direction. For the standard right-handed basis, \[ \mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k,\qquad \mathbf j\times\mathbf k=\mathbf i,\qquad \mathbf k\times\mathbf i=\mathbf j. \] Reversing the order reverses the vector: \[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u). \] In diagrams, a circle with a central dot shows a vector pointing out of the page, toward the viewer; a circle with a cross shows a vector pointing into the page. The cross product is distributive over addition, and \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf u=\mathbf0\).

For component calculations, \[ \begin{aligned} (\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)_1&=u_2v_3-u_3v_2,\\ (\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)_2&=u_3v_1-u_1v_3,\\ (\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)_3&=u_1v_2-u_2v_1. \end{aligned} \] Month 4 explains how the determinant produces this pattern.

Normals and physical meanings

Two non-parallel directions in a plane produce a normal vector through their cross product. Every nonzero scalar multiple of that result lies on the same normal line.

Three physical formulas use this geometry:

  • Torque: \(\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F\), where \(\mathbf r\) runs from the pivot to the point of application and is called the lever-arm vector. The result gives the rotation axis and turning sense, with units \({\rm N\,m}\). Its magnitude is \(\tau=rF\sin\theta\).
  • Magnetic force: \(\mathbf F=q\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\) for charge \(q\), velocity \(\mathbf v\), and magnetic field \(\mathbf B\). Positive and negative charges experience opposite force directions. Magnetic field strength is measured in teslas, written \({\rm T}\), and charge is measured in coulombs, written \({\rm C}\).
  • Rotational velocity: \(\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\), where \(\boldsymbol\omega\) points along the rotation axis and its magnitude is the angular speed in radians per second. Its direction follows the right-hand rule and records the rotation sense.

The last formula gives an instantaneous tangent velocity. A finite rotation through a specified angle uses a transformation containing the sine and cosine of that angle.

Set 3

Ten questions about geometry, meaning and calculation

1Complete the oriented cycle \(\mathbf i\times\mathbf j,\ \mathbf j\times\mathbf k,\ \mathbf k\times\mathbf i\), then reverse each product. What single rule explains all six signs?
Worked explanation

In a right-handed frame, \[ \mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k,\qquad \mathbf j\times\mathbf k=\mathbf i,\qquad \mathbf k\times\mathbf i=\mathbf j. \] Reversing the order reverses the sign: \[ \mathbf j\times\mathbf i=-\mathbf k,\qquad \mathbf k\times\mathbf j=-\mathbf i,\qquad \mathbf i\times\mathbf k=-\mathbf j. \]

The right-hand rule fixes the orientation. Algebraically this is anti-commutativity: \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=-(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u)\).

2Let \(\mathbf u=(2,0,0)\) and \(\mathbf v=(0,3,0)\). Find \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\). Interpret both its direction and its magnitude.
Worked explanation

\[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(0,0,6)=6\mathbf k. \] Its direction is perpendicular to the \(xy\)-plane, with the positive choice fixed by curling from \(\mathbf u\) toward \(\mathbf v\).

Its magnitude \(6\) is the area of the \(2\)-by-\(3\) parallelogram spanned by the vectors. The cross product packages an oriented plane and its area into one vector.

3Why is the cross product zero for parallel vectors? Explain using both the area picture and \(\|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\| =\|\mathbf u\|\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta\).
Worked explanation

Parallel vectors span a flattened parallelogram, so its area is zero. In the magnitude formula their angle is \(0\) or \(\pi\), and \(\sin0=\sin\pi=0\).

A zero cross product therefore signals that the two directions fail to determine a plane. There is no unique normal direction to a collapsed parallelogram.

4A force \(\mathbf F=(0,50,0)\) N is applied at position \(\mathbf r=(0.4,0,0)\) m from a pivot. Find the torque \(\boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F\) and interpret the result.
Worked explanation

\[ \boldsymbol\tau=(0.4,0,0)\times(0,50,0)=(0,0,20)\ {\rm N\,m}. \] The magnitude \(20\ {\rm N\,m}\) measures turning effectiveness. The \(+\mathbf k\) direction records the axis and sense of the attempted rotation: counterclockwise in the \(xy\)-plane when viewed from \(+\mathbf k\).

Torque is a cross product because the force component perpendicular to the lever arm determines the turning effect.

5The same \(30\) N force is applied at a point \(0.8\) m from a door hinge in two ways: perpendicular to the lever arm in the plane of the door, and directly toward the hinge. Compare the torques without component calculations.
Worked explanation

Perpendicular application gives \(\tau=rF\sin90^\circ=0.8(30)=24\ {\rm N\,m}\). A force aimed directly toward the hinge is parallel to the lever arm, so \(\tau=rF\sin180^\circ=0\).

This is the cross product's sine geometry in ordinary experience. A large force can be useless for rotation if its line of action passes through the pivot.

6A positive charge \(q\) moves with velocity \(\mathbf v=(2,0,0)\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}\) through a magnetic field \(\mathbf B=(0,0,3)\ {\rm T}\). Find the direction of \(\mathbf F=q\,\mathbf v\times\mathbf B\). What changes for a negative charge?
Worked explanation

\[ \mathbf v\times\mathbf B=(2\mathbf i)\times(3\mathbf k) =6(\mathbf i\times\mathbf k)=-6\mathbf j. \] For \(q>0\), the force points in the negative \(y\)-direction.

If \(q<0\), multiplying by a negative scalar reverses the direction. The magnetic force is perpendicular to the velocity, which is why it bends a path without directly increasing the particle's speed.

7A rigid body has angular velocity \(\boldsymbol\omega=(0,0,2)\ {\rm rad\,s^{-1}}\). Find the instantaneous velocity of the point \(\mathbf r=(3,0,0)\ {\rm m}\) using \(\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\). Explain the picture.
Worked explanation

\[ \mathbf v=(0,0,2)\times(3,0,0)=(0,6,0)\ {\rm m\,s^{-1}}. \] The velocity is tangent to the circle about the \(z\)-axis and perpendicular to the radius. Its speed \(6\) agrees with \(v=\omega r=2(3)\).

The angular-velocity vector stores the rotation axis in its direction and the rotation rate in its length. Crossing it with position turns a radial arrow into the correct tangent arrow.

8Does \(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r\) rotate \(\mathbf r\) through a finite angle? State precisely what it describes, and why confusing the two ideas causes trouble.
Worked explanation

No. The cross product gives the instantaneous velocity, or equivalently the first-order change \(d\mathbf r\approx(\boldsymbol\omega\times\mathbf r)\,dt\) over a tiny time.

A finite rotation through a specified angle uses a transformation containing the sine and cosine of that angle. Repeated tiny tangent changes build the curved motion.

9Two directions in a plane are \(\mathbf a=(1,2,0)\) and \(\mathbf b=(3,-1,0)\). Find a normal vector and explain why every nonzero scalar multiple of it describes the same normal line.
Worked explanation

\[ \mathbf a\times\mathbf b =(0,0,1(-1)-2(3))=(0,0,-7). \] This is perpendicular to both directions, so it is normal to their plane.

Any nonzero multiple, such as \((0,0,-1)\) or \((0,0,14)\), points along the same normal line. Normal length is arbitrary. The sign selects one of the two orientations.

10A student finds \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(2,-1,4)\) and \(\mathbf v\times\mathbf u=(2,-1,4)\). Without recomputing components, diagnose the error and state a quick check.
Worked explanation

The two answers cannot both be correct unless the cross product is zero. Reversing the inputs must reverse the output: \[ \mathbf v\times\mathbf u=-(2,-1,4)=(-2,1,-4). \]

A quick check is to add the two claimed products; the sum must be zero. Dotting a candidate with both inputs can confirm perpendicularity, but cannot distinguish the two opposite normal directions: both signs are perpendicular. The order must be checked with anti-commutativity or the right-hand rule.

Month 4

Why the determinant computes a cross product

The roles of i, j, k, orientation, cofactors and volume

The determinant encodes oriented area

For a \(2\times2\) matrix, \[ \det\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad-bc. \] Its absolute value gives the area scale factor, while its sign records orientation. The columns are the two vertical component lists. Swapping them reverses the sign. Parallel columns give determinant zero.

The \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\) array

The cross-product array is a cofactor mnemonic: \[ \mathbf u\times\mathbf v= \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\ u_1&u_2&u_3\\ v_1&v_2&v_3 \end{vmatrix}. \] For an entry of a matrix, its minor is the smaller determinant left after deleting that entry's row and column. A cofactor is the minor with its position sign attached. Across the first row, those signs are \(+,-,+\). Expanding along the first row uses the sign pattern \(+,-,+\): \[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf u\times\mathbf v ={}&(u_2v_3-u_3v_2)\mathbf i\\ &-(u_1v_3-u_3v_1)\mathbf j\\ &+(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)\mathbf k. \end{aligned} \] The entries \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\) turn the three scalar cofactors into the three components of one vector.

A visual route from elimination to oriented area

1. Begin with the object produced by the cross product

The two vectors \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\) span a parallelogram. Their cross product \(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\) packages two measurements into one arrow: its length is the parallelogram's area, and its direction records the oriented normal chosen by the right-hand rule.

A third vector creates a volume calculation: \[ V=\left|\mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)\right|. \] For non-parallel \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\), let \(\mathbf A=\mathbf a\times\mathbf b\) and \(\widehat{\mathbf n}=\mathbf A/\|\mathbf A\|\). The signed perpendicular height is \(h=\mathbf c\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\), so \[ \mathbf c\cdot\mathbf A=\|\mathbf A\|h. \] The scalar triple product therefore multiplies base area by signed height. Parallel \(\mathbf a,\mathbf b\) give \(\mathbf A=\mathbf0\), a collapsed base and zero volume.

2. Read the components as three shadows

Picture the parallelogram as a tilted sheet inside a glass room. Its shadow on the \(yz\)-wall gives the signed \(x\)-component: \[ (\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)_x =\det\begin{pmatrix}a_2&a_3\\b_2&b_3\end{pmatrix}. \] Its shadow on the \(zx\)-wall gives the signed \(y\)-component: \[ (\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)_y =\det\begin{pmatrix}a_3&a_1\\b_3&b_1\end{pmatrix}. \] Its shadow on the \(xy\)-wall gives the signed \(z\)-component: \[ (\mathbf a\times\mathbf b)_z =\det\begin{pmatrix}a_1&a_2\\b_1&b_2\end{pmatrix}. \] These three signed shadow areas are exactly the \(x,y,z\) components of the area vector. A component measures how strongly the area vector points toward the normal of that coordinate wall.

3. The middle sign preserves the cyclic orientation

The positively oriented coordinate walls follow the cycle \[ yz,\qquad zx,\qquad xy, \] because \(\mathbf j\times\mathbf k=\mathbf i\), \(\mathbf k\times\mathbf i=\mathbf j\), and \(\mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k\). The \(y\)-component therefore uses \(zx\) order: \[ a_3b_1-a_1b_3. \] Cofactor expansion deletes the middle column and displays the surviving columns in \(xz\) order, giving \(a_1b_3-a_3b_1\). Reversing \(xz\) to \(zx\) changes the sign. This is the source of the minus sign attached to the \(\mathbf j\)-minor.

4. The algebraic pattern appeared before modern vector notation

Determinant methods grew from elimination. Seki Takakazu used determinant-like arrays in 1683 while eliminating unknowns from polynomial equations, and Leibniz used related coefficient expressions for simultaneous linear equations in 1693. The modern matrix viewpoint arrived much later.

The \(2\times2\) expression can already be seen in \[ ax+by=p,\qquad cx+dy=q. \] Multiply the first equation by \(d\), the second by \(b\), and subtract: \[ (ad-bc)x=dp-bq. \] The quantity \(ad-bc\) emerges as the coefficient that survives elimination. Its zero or nonzero value controls whether this elimination can isolate \(x\).

In 1773, Lagrange used component expressions now recognised as dot- and cross-product formulas while studying tetrahedra and rotation. Hamilton's quaternion algebra in 1843 later combined scalar and vector parts in one product. Gibbs and Heaviside separated those parts into the dot and cross products used in modern vector analysis during the late nineteenth century.

5. Permutation parity controls every sign

A determinant pairs entries using different column orders. Each swap in an order reverses the sign. An even number of swaps gives a positive term; an odd number gives a negative term. Along the first row of a \(3\times3\) determinant, moving columns \(1,2,3\) into the first position takes \(0,1,2\) swaps. Their signs are therefore \[ +,\quad-,\quad+. \] This is the cofactor checkerboard rule \((-1)^{i+j}\) viewed through permutation parity.

Algebra records orientation through the order of columns. Geometry records orientation through the order of axes. The determinant and cross product share their sign laws because both encode alternating, oriented area.

Historical reading: MacTutor: Matrices and determinants and Lagrange's identity and its 1773 setting.

Multilinear and alternating

Multilinear means scaling or adding an input scales or adds the result in that input. Alternating means swapping two inputs reverses the sign, while equal inputs give zero. These determinant properties match oriented area.

The scalar triple product

Three non-coplanar edge vectors form a six-faced box-like solid called a parallelepiped. Its signed volume is \[ \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c). \] Let \(\mathbf A=\mathbf b\times\mathbf c\) be the oriented base-area vector and \(\widehat{\mathbf n}=\mathbf A/\|\mathbf A\|\) its unit normal. The signed height is \(h=\mathbf a\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}\), which gives \[ \mathbf a\cdot\mathbf A=\|\mathbf A\|h. \] Its absolute value gives ordinary volume. A zero triple product means the three vectors are coplanar: they lie in one plane through the origin.

After calculating a cross product, check \(\mathbf u\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=0\) and \(\mathbf v\cdot(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v)=0\).

Set 4

Ten questions about geometry, meaning and calculation

1In the cross-product determinant, why may the first row contain \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\) while the lower rows contain numbers? Explain what the expansion is constructing.
Worked explanation

The first-row entries are vectors: the standard unit basis \(\mathbf i=(1,0,0)\), \(\mathbf j=(0,1,0)\), \(\mathbf k=(0,0,1)\). Cofactor expansion produces three scalar minors, then uses them as coefficients of these basis vectors.

The result is therefore a vector assembled as \(c_1\mathbf i+c_2\mathbf j+c_3\mathbf k\). Read the array as a compact, vector-valued cofactor mnemonic for obtaining the three components. An ordinary determinant has scalar entries.

2Expand \[ \begin{vmatrix} \mathbf i&\mathbf j&\mathbf k\\ u_1&u_2&u_3\\ v_1&v_2&v_3 \end{vmatrix}. \] Match the three components with signed area projections on the \(yz,zx,xy\) coordinate planes. Why does writing the middle minor in \(xz\) order produce an outside minus sign?
Worked explanation

Cofactor expansion along the first row gives \[ (u_2v_3-u_3v_2)\mathbf i -(u_1v_3-u_3v_1)\mathbf j +(u_1v_2-u_2v_1)\mathbf k. \]

The \(\mathbf i\)-component is the \(yz\) signed area \(\det\!\begin{pmatrix}u_2&u_3\\v_2&v_3\end{pmatrix}\). The \(\mathbf j\)-component is the \(zx\) signed area \[ \det\!\begin{pmatrix}u_3&u_1\\v_3&v_1\end{pmatrix} =u_3v_1-u_1v_3. \] The displayed cofactor minor uses \(xz\) order, whose determinant has the opposite sign, so the outside minus restores \(zx\) orientation. The \(\mathbf k\)-component is the \(xy\) signed area. The cyclic order \(yz,zx,xy\) matches \(\mathbf j\times\mathbf k=\mathbf i\), \(\mathbf k\times\mathbf i=\mathbf j\), and \(\mathbf i\times\mathbf j=\mathbf k\).

3Use the determinant pattern to calculate \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\) for \(\mathbf u=(1,2,3)\), \(\mathbf v=(4,-1,2)\).
Worked explanation

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathbf u\times\mathbf v &=(2\cdot2-3(-1),\ 3\cdot4-1\cdot2,\ 1(-1)-2\cdot4)\\ &=(7,10,-9). \end{aligned} \]

The middle component was written in its already sign-correct form \(u_3v_1-u_1v_3\). This is equivalent to placing the cofactor minus outside the corresponding \(2\times2\) determinant.

4For \(\mathbf u=(1,2,3)\) and \(\mathbf v=(4,-1,2)\), verify that \((7,10,-9)\) is perpendicular to both inputs. Why is this check stronger than checking only one dot product?
Worked explanation

\[ (1,2,3)\cdot(7,10,-9)=7+20-27=0, \] \[ (4,-1,2)\cdot(7,10,-9)=28-10-18=0. \]

A vector perpendicular to only one input could lie anywhere in a whole plane. Being perpendicular to both non-parallel inputs pins it to their common normal line. Both checks are needed to test the defining geometry.

5The \(\mathbf i,\mathbf j,\mathbf k\) array is often called a determinant, but its first row contains vectors. In what sense is it a useful mnemonic, and what deeper determinant properties make it work?
Worked explanation

Read the array as a mnemonic for cofactor expansion. Expanding produces the three scalar components multiplying the basis vectors.

It works because determinants are multilinear and alternating. Linearity explains how scaling and addition pass through the construction; alternation explains why swapping the inputs reverses the sign and why parallel inputs give zero. Those are exactly the structural properties an oriented area vector must have.

6Take planar vectors \(\mathbf a=(1,3)\) and \(\mathbf b=(2,4)\) as the columns of a \(2\times2\) matrix. Compute the determinant and interpret its magnitude and sign.
Worked explanation

\[ \det\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\3&4\end{pmatrix}=1(4)-2(3)=-2. \] The magnitude \(2\) is the area of the parallelogram spanned by the columns.

The negative sign says the ordered pair \((\mathbf a,\mathbf b)\) has the opposite orientation to the standard \((\mathbf i,\mathbf j)\) ordering. Determinants measure signed area; cross products extend that idea to an oriented area vector in three dimensions.

7Find the volume of the parallelepiped generated by \(\mathbf a=(1,0,0)\), \(\mathbf b=(0,2,0)\), \(\mathbf c=(1,1,3)\) using a scalar triple product. Explain the geometry of “base area times height” inside the formula.
Worked explanation

\[ \mathbf b\times\mathbf c=(6,0,-2),\qquad \mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)=6. \] Hence the volume is \(|6|=6\).

Here \(\|\mathbf b\times\mathbf c\|=2\sqrt{10}\) is the base area. Its unit normal is \(\widehat{\mathbf n}=(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)/(2\sqrt{10})\), so the signed height is \[ \mathbf a\cdot\widehat{\mathbf n}=\frac{3}{\sqrt{10}}. \] Therefore base area times signed height is \(2\sqrt{10}(3/\sqrt{10})=6\), matching the scalar triple product.

8What happens to \(\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\) when two vectors are swapped? State the effect on orientation and ordinary volume.
Worked explanation

Swapping any two vectors reverses the sign of the scalar triple product. For example, \(\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf c\times\mathbf b) =-\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\).

The absolute value, and hence ordinary volume, remains unchanged. The sign reverses because the ordering has switched handedness. The sign records orientation while the absolute value records ordinary volume.

9Show that \(\mathbf a=(1,0,1)\), \(\mathbf b=(0,1,1)\), and \(\mathbf c=(2,3,5)\) are coplanar using a scalar triple product. Connect the zero to a linear-combination statement.
Worked explanation

First compute the normal: \[ \mathbf a\times\mathbf b =(1,0,1)\times(0,1,1)=(-1,-1,1). \] Then \[ \mathbf c\cdot(\mathbf a\times\mathbf b) =(2,3,5)\cdot(-1,-1,1)=-2-3+5=0. \]

The zero says \(\mathbf c\) has no component along the plane's normal. Indeed, \(\mathbf c=2\mathbf a+3\mathbf b\). The zero volume and the linear-combination statement describe the same coplanarity.

10Show that \(\mathbf u\times(\mathbf v+c\mathbf u)=\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\) for every scalar \(c\). Explain this identity by sliding one side of a parallelogram.
Worked explanation

Using distributivity and \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf u=\mathbf0\), \[ \mathbf u\times(\mathbf v+c\mathbf u) =\mathbf u\times\mathbf v+c(\mathbf u\times\mathbf u) =\mathbf u\times\mathbf v. \]

Geometrically, adding a multiple of the base vector \(\mathbf u\) slides the tip of the other side parallel to the base. The parallelogram is sheared, but its height, area, and oriented normal remain unchanged. Row replacement leaves a determinant unchanged for exactly the same reason.

Month 5

Choosing the right operation

Integrated geometry and physics: what to use, when, and why

Choose an operation by the question it answers

Choose the formula from the required output. A scalar measuring along-ness suggests dot. A vector normal to a plane suggests cross. An along-component suggests projection. Successive motions suggest addition.

Physical problems often need several of these in sequence. A cross product may construct a plane normal, then a dot product may find an angle to that normal. A dot product may locate a target ahead of a robot, then a cross product may decide whether the target lies left or right.

Formula recap

\[ \widehat{\mathbf u}=\frac{\mathbf u}{\|\mathbf u\|},\qquad \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|},\qquad \operatorname{proj}_{\mathbf v}\mathbf u =\frac{\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v}{\mathbf v\cdot\mathbf v}\mathbf v. \] \[ \|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\| =\|\mathbf u\|\,\|\mathbf v\|\sin\theta,\qquad W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d,\qquad \boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F. \] \[ V=\left|\mathbf a\cdot(\mathbf b\times\mathbf c)\right|. \] These formulas require nonzero reference vectors in every denominator.

Relative motion adds vectors tip to tail. For example, \[ \mathbf v_{\rm ground} =\mathbf v_{\rm aircraft/air}+\mathbf v_{\rm air/ground}. \] The ground speed is \(\|\mathbf v_{\rm ground}\|\), and the drift angle is measured from the declared reference direction.

Two derived formulas used in this set

If a line has direction \(\mathbf d\) and a plane has normal \(\mathbf n\), the acute angle \(\beta\) between the line and plane satisfies \[ \sin\beta= \frac{|\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n|}{\|\mathbf d\|\,\|\mathbf n\|}. \] This angle is complementary to the angle between the line and the normal.

For a line through \(A\) with direction \(\mathbf d\), the distance from \(P\) to the line is \[ \operatorname{dist}(P,L) =\frac{\|\overrightarrow{AP}\times\mathbf d\|}{\|\mathbf d\|}. \] The numerator is parallelogram area \(=\) base times perpendicular height. Division by the base length \(\|\mathbf d\|\) leaves the height.

Reading physical questions

Work selects the force component along a displacement. Torque selects the force component perpendicular to a lever arm. A force applied perpendicular to a lever arm therefore achieves a chosen torque with the smallest force magnitude. For left-right orientation in the \(xy\)-plane, the sign of the \(\mathbf k\)-component of a cross product records the turn direction. For example, \(\mathbf i\times(-\mathbf j)=-\mathbf k\), so the turn from \(+\mathbf i\) toward \(-\mathbf j\) is clockwise when viewed from \(+\mathbf k\).

Set 5

Ten questions about geometry, meaning and calculation

1For each request, choose the most direct operation: (a) directional agreement, (b) a component along a line, (c) an oriented normal to a plane, (d) work, (e) torque, (f) volume from three edge vectors.
Worked explanation

(a) Use a normalised dot product. (b) Use projection, which is built from a dot product. (c) Use a cross product. (d) Use \(\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d\). (e) Use \(\mathbf r\times\mathbf F\). (f) Use the absolute scalar triple product.

The organising question is: what kind of output and geometry are required? Dot produces a scalar measuring along-ness; cross produces a perpendicular vector whose magnitude is oriented area; projection returns the along-part itself.

2A force \(\mathbf F=(3,4,0)\) N acts at \(\mathbf r=(2,0,0)\) m from a pivot while the point moves through \(\mathbf d=(5,0,0)\) m. Find the work and torque. Explain why the same force is inspected in two different ways.
Worked explanation

\[ W=\mathbf F\cdot\mathbf d=15\ {\rm J},\qquad \boldsymbol\tau=\mathbf r\times\mathbf F=(0,0,8)\ {\rm N\,m}. \]

Work asks how much force lies along the motion, so it uses cosine geometry. Torque asks how much force lies perpendicular to the lever arm, so it uses sine geometry. Neither number is “the effect of the force” in general; each answers a different physical question.

3An actuator applies \(\mathbf F=(70,10)\) N beside a rail whose unit direction is \(\widehat{\mathbf t}=(3/5,4/5)\). Decompose the force into components parallel and perpendicular to the rail.
Worked explanation

The tangent component is \[ \mathbf F_\parallel=(\mathbf F\cdot\widehat{\mathbf t}) \widehat{\mathbf t} =50(3/5,4/5)=(30,40)\ {\rm N}. \] The perpendicular component is \[ \mathbf F_\perp=\mathbf F-\mathbf F_\parallel=(40,-30)\ {\rm N}. \]

The check \((30,40)+(40,-30)=(70,10)\) reconstructs the force, while \((40,-30)\cdot(3/5,4/5)=0\) confirms the geometry.

4An aircraft points with air velocity \((180,0)\) km/h while wind velocity is \((0,60)\) km/h. Find its ground velocity, speed, and drift angle. Which vector operation creates the ground velocity, and which extracts the angle?
Worked explanation

Vector addition gives \(\mathbf v_g=(180,60)\) km/h. Its speed is \(\sqrt{180^2+60^2}=60\sqrt{10}\approx189.7\) km/h, and its drift angle north of east is \(\tan^{-1}(60/180)=\tan^{-1}(1/3)\approx18.4^\circ\).

Addition is required because both velocities contribute during the same interval. The drift angle is then the orientation of the resulting velocity relative to east, found by inverse trigonometry or a normalised dot product with the east unit vector.

5A plane contains directions \(\mathbf a=(1,1,0)\) and \(\mathbf b=(0,1,1)\). Find a normal and then find the acute angle between the plane and \(\mathbf d=(1,0,0)\). Explain why both cross and dot appear.
Worked explanation

A normal is \[ \mathbf n=\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=(1,-1,1). \] If \(\alpha\) is the angle between \(\mathbf d\) and the normal, \(\cos\alpha=1/\sqrt3\). The angle \(\beta\) between the line and plane is complementary, so \[ \sin\beta=\frac{|\mathbf d\cdot\mathbf n|} {\|\mathbf d\|\|\mathbf n\|}=\frac1{\sqrt3}, \qquad \boxed{\beta=\sin^{-1}\!\left(\frac1{\sqrt3}\right)\approx35.3^\circ}. \]

Cross first constructs the normal; dot then measures alignment with it. Synthesis problems often require operations in sequence.

6A line passes through the origin in direction \(\mathbf d=(2,1,0)\). Find the shortest distance from \(P=(1,3,0)\) to the line using a cross product. Explain why the formula measures the perpendicular part.
Worked explanation

\[ \operatorname{dist}(P,L) =\frac{\|\overrightarrow{OP}\times\mathbf d\|}{\|\mathbf d\|} =\frac{\|(1,3,0)\times(2,1,0)\|}{\sqrt5} =\frac{5}{\sqrt5}=\sqrt5. \]

The cross-product magnitude is \(\|OP\|\|d\|\sin\theta\). Dividing by \(\|d\|\) leaves \(\|OP\|\sin\theta\), precisely the height of the parallelogram and therefore the perpendicular distance to the line.

7A \(0.3\) m wrench lies along \(+\mathbf i\). You need torque \(\boldsymbol\tau=(0,0,12)\ {\rm N\,m}\). Find the smallest-magnitude force that produces this torque, and explain why adding force along the wrench is wasted effort.
Worked explanation

Write \(\mathbf r=(0.3,0,0)\) m and \(\mathbf F=(F_x,F_y,F_z)\) N. Then \[ \mathbf r\times\mathbf F=(0,-0.3F_z,0.3F_y)=(0,0,12). \] Hence \(F_z=0\) and \(F_y=40\) N, while \(F_x\) is unrestricted by the torque.

The smallest magnitude occurs at \(F_x=0\), giving \(\boxed{\mathbf F=(0,40,0)\ {\rm N}}\). Any \(F_x\) component is parallel to the wrench, so its cross product with \(\mathbf r\) is zero; it increases the applied force without increasing the torque.

8For \(\mathbf u=(3,0,0)\) and \(\mathbf v=(4,4,0)\), compute \(\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v\) and \(\|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|\). Use the results to explain \[ (\mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v)^2+\|\mathbf u\times\mathbf v\|^2 =\|\mathbf u\|^2\|\mathbf v\|^2. \]
Worked explanation

The dot product is \(12\), and \(\mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(0,0,12)\), so the cross magnitude is also \(12\). The left side is \(144+144=288\). The right side is \(3^2(4\sqrt2)^2=9(32)=288\).

The identity comes directly from \(\cos^2\theta+\sin^2\theta=1\) multiplied by \(\|\mathbf u\|^2\|\mathbf v\|^2\). Dot and cross are complementary measurements of the same pair: one uses the cosine of the angle and the other uses its sine.

9Let \(\mathbf u=(1,0,0)\) and \(\mathbf v=(0,-1,0)\). The dot product says the angle is \(90^\circ\). What extra information does the cross product provide?
Worked explanation

\[ \mathbf u\cdot\mathbf v=0,\qquad \mathbf u\times\mathbf v=(0,0,-1)=-\mathbf k. \] The dot product gives the unsigned angle. The negative \(z\)-direction of the cross product says the turn from \(\mathbf u\) to \(\mathbf v\) is clockwise when viewed from \(+\mathbf k\).

Together, dot and cross distinguish “how far around” from “which way around”. This is useful in navigation, robotics, and orientation tests.

10A robot faces \(\widehat{\mathbf f}=(1,1,0)/\sqrt2\). A target lies at displacement \(\mathbf d=(4,0,0)\) m. Find (i) how far ahead the target is, (ii) the sideways correction vector, and (iii) whether the target lies to the robot's left or right when viewed from \(+\mathbf k\).
Worked explanation

The signed forward distance is \[ \mathbf d\cdot\widehat{\mathbf f} =\frac4{\sqrt2}=2\sqrt2\ {\rm m}. \] The forward projection is \(2\sqrt2\,\widehat{\mathbf f}=(2,2,0)\) m, so the sideways correction is \(\mathbf d-(2,2,0)=(2,-2,0)\) m.

Finally, \[ \widehat{\mathbf f}\times\mathbf d=(0,0,-2\sqrt2). \] The negative \(z\)-sign says the turn from facing direction to target is clockwise, so the target is to the robot's right. Dot measures aheadness, projection constructs the ahead-part, and cross supplies left-versus-right.
