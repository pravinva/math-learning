Section 5A
Coordinates in Three Dimensions
What changes when we add a z-axis to the plane?
You already know how to find the distance between two points in 2D. The question for this whole chapter is: what changes — if anything — when we add a third dimension? The answer, as you'll see, is almost nothing — you just apply the same trick twice.
Pattern Build it: two Pythagoras steps chained together ▶

Step 1 — recall the 2D case, rebuilt, not quoted. Take $A(1,2)$ and $B(4,6)$. Draw the right-angled triangle: horizontal leg $4-1=3$, vertical leg $6-2=4$.

\[ AB = \sqrt{3^2+4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5 \]

The "formula" $d=\sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2+(y_2-y_1)^2}$ is just that one Pythagoras step, written down so you don't redraw the triangle each time.

Step 2 — what's new about a $z$-axis? Picture a room: $x$ along the floor to your right, $y$ along the floor ahead, $z$ straight up. To reach $(3,4,12)$ from the origin, first walk to $(3,4,0)$ on the floor — that floor-distance is $\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$, from Step 1. Then climb $12$ more, straight up — perpendicular to the floor. That's a second right triangle: legs $5$ and $12$.

\[ OP = \sqrt{5^2+12^2} = \sqrt{169} = 13 \]

Step 3 — spot the pattern. The floor-distance squared was $3^2+4^2$. The full distance squared was $(\sqrt{3^2+4^2})^2+12^2 = 3^2+4^2+12^2$ — the square root just cancels cleanly. Pythagoras applied twice in a row is one Pythagoras with three legs instead of two.

1
Walk the floor distance between the $x$ and $y$ coordinates first.
2
Treat that floor-distance as one leg of a new right triangle.
3
The $z$-difference is the other leg — apply Pythagoras once more.
Trigger for this idea: any time you add a dimension perpendicular to the ones you already have, distance just gets one more squared term under the root — never a new rule.
Definition Distance formula in 3D, and the coordinate planes ▶

For $A(x_1,y_1,z_1)$ and $B(x_2,y_2,z_2)$:

\[ d = \sqrt{(x_2-x_1)^2 + (y_2-y_1)^2 + (z_2-z_1)^2} \]

The three coordinate planes — $xy$ (where $z=0$), $xz$ (where $y=0$), $yz$ (where $x=0$) — cut space into eight octants, the direct 3D analogue of the four 2D quadrants.

Example Two worked examples ▶
Example 1 — straight application

Find the distance between $A(1,-2,3)$ and $B(4,0,-1)$.

\[ AB = \sqrt{(4-1)^2+(0+2)^2+(-1-3)^2} = \sqrt{9+4+16} = \sqrt{29} \]
ANSWER $\sqrt{29}$
Example 2 — proving a right angle, not assuming one

Show that the triangle with vertices $O(0,0,0)$, $A(1,2,2)$, $B(2,-2,1)$ is right-angled at $O$.

1
$OA = \sqrt{1+4+4}=3$
2
$OB = \sqrt{4+4+1}=3$
3
$AB = \sqrt{1+16+1}=\sqrt{18}$
4
Check: $OA^2+OB^2 = 9+9=18=AB^2$ — converse of Pythagoras confirms $90^\circ$ at $O$.
ANSWER Right-angled at $O$, proved algebraically
Note Uni bridge & things to watch ▶
Uni Bridge
At university this two-Pythagoras trick becomes the Euclidean norm of a difference vector: $d(A,B)=\lVert \mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}\rVert$. The same idea generalises to $\mathbb{R}^n$ for any number of dimensions — the proof is literally induction on the number of perpendicular steps you take. Lecturers write vectors in bold ($\mathbf{a}$) instead of with a squiggle underneath ($a_\sim$) — same object, different convention.
Watch out: don't try to memorise the distance formula as a separate fact from the 2D version — it's the same Pythagoras idea with one extra term. If you forget the formula, rebuild it from the two-triangle argument.
Section 5B
Vectors in Three Dimensions
A displacement with one more instruction: how far up.
A vector is a displacement — "3 across, 2 up" in 2D. What does a displacement look like once you can also move up off the floor?
Pattern Build it: extend components, basis vectors, magnitude, unit vectors ▶

Step 1 — extend the displacement idea. Walking from $A(1,0,2)$ to $B(4,3,-1)$: subtract coordinates exactly as in 2D, just with one more slot.

\[ \overrightarrow{AB} = \begin{pmatrix}4-1\\3-0\\-1-2\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\-3\end{pmatrix} \]

Step 2 — name the three "pure" directions. $\mathbf{i}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{j}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}$, and now a third: $\mathbf{k}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ for straight up. So $\begin{pmatrix}3\\3\\-3\end{pmatrix}=3\mathbf{i}+3\mathbf{j}-3\mathbf{k}$.

Step 3 — magnitude reuses 5A. The length of a vector is the distance from tail to head — already solved.

\[ |\mathbf{a}| = \sqrt{3^2+3^2+(-3)^2} = \sqrt{27}=3\sqrt3 \]

Step 4 — unit vectors: direction, ignoring how far. Shrink a vector by its own length. For $\mathbf{u}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\0\\-4\end{pmatrix}$, $|\mathbf{u}|=5$, so dividing every component by $5$ gives $\begin{pmatrix}0.6\\0\\-0.8\end{pmatrix}$ — and checking, $\sqrt{0.36+0+0.64}=1$. That check is what guarantees the recipe always works.

Definition Component form, magnitude, unit vector ▶
\[ \mathbf{a} = x\mathbf{i}+y\mathbf{j}+z\mathbf{k} = \begin{pmatrix}x\\y\\z\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |\mathbf{a}|^2 = x^2+y^2+z^2, \qquad \hat{\mathbf{a}} = \frac{\mathbf{a}}{|\mathbf{a}|} \]

Addition, subtraction, and scalar multiplication all work component-by-component — no special 3D rule, just one more component to carry along.

Example Two worked examples ▶
Example 1 — combine and find magnitude

Given $\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}-1\\4\\0\end{pmatrix}$, find $2\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$ and its magnitude.

\[ 2\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b} = \begin{pmatrix}4\\-2\\6\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}-1\\4\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}5\\-6\\6\end{pmatrix}, \qquad |2\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}| = \sqrt{25+36+36}=\sqrt{97} \]
ANSWER $\begin{pmatrix}5\\-6\\6\end{pmatrix}$, magnitude $\sqrt{97}$
Example 2 — scale to a target length

A vector $\mathbf{v}$ has length $10$ and points the same way as $\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}$. Find $\mathbf{v}$.

1
$\left|\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}\right|=\sqrt{1+4+4}=3$
2
Unit vector: $\tfrac13\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}$
3
Scale to length 10: $\mathbf{v}=\tfrac{10}{3}\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix}$
ANSWER $\mathbf{v}=\begin{pmatrix}10/3\\20/3\\20/3\end{pmatrix}$
Note Uni bridge ▶
Uni Bridge
$\mathbb{R}^3$ is your first concrete example of a vector space — a set closed under addition and scalar multiplication, satisfying axioms like a zero vector and additive inverses. Everything you just rebuilt by hand ("vectors add tip-to-tail," "$\mathbf{i},\mathbf{j},\mathbf{k}$ build everything else") gets restated as axioms that any vector space must satisfy — even ones whose "vectors" are polynomials or functions.
Section 5C
The Dot Product
An operation that turns two vectors into one number — discovered, then proved.
So far every operation on vectors gives back another vector. Is there an operation that combines two vectors into a single number — and what could that number tell us?
Pattern Build it: a numerical experiment, then a proof ▶

Step 1 — invent an operation and test it. Multiply matching components, add the results.

1
$\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\end{pmatrix}$ (perpendicular) $=0$
2
$\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}3\\0\end{pmatrix}$ (same direction) $=3=|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|$
3
$\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}-3\\0\end{pmatrix}$ (opposite) $=-3=-|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|$
4
$\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}\cdot\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ ($45^\circ$ apart) $=1=\sqrt2\cos45^\circ$
Pattern: zero when perpendicular, $+|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|$ when parallel same way, $-|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|$ when opposite, and in between it seems to track $\cos\theta$.

Step 2 — prove the pattern. Take the triangle formed by $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}$. The law of cosines gives $|\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}|^2=|\mathbf{a}|^2+|\mathbf{b}|^2-2|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta$. Expanding the left side by components and comparing:

\[ |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta = a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3 \]

Not a guess anymore — proved for every pair of vectors. That's why the dot product has two equivalent definitions.

Definition Two equivalent definitions, and the properties that fall out for free ▶
\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = a_1b_1+a_2b_2+a_3b_3 = |\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta \]
PropertyStatementWhy
Commutative$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=\mathbf{b}\cdot\mathbf{a}$real multiplication commutes
Distributive$\mathbf{a}\cdot(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c})=\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{c}$real mult. distributes
Self dot product$\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{a}=|\mathbf{a}|^2$same sum as magnitude
Perpendicularity test$\mathbf{a}\perp\mathbf{b}\iff\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=0$$\cos90^\circ=0$
Example Worked examples ▶
Example 1 — perpendicularity test

Are $\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-3\\1\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}$ perpendicular?

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b} = 2-3+1 = 0 \]
ANSWER Yes — dot product is zero
Example 2 — verify both definitions agree

Check the algebraic and geometric definitions agree for $\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$, $\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}$.

1
Algebraic: $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=1+0+0=1$
2
Geometric: $|\mathbf{a}|=1$, $|\mathbf{b}|=\sqrt2$, angle $=45^\circ$
3
$1\times\sqrt2\times\cos45^\circ = \sqrt2\times\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}=1$ — matches
ANSWER Both give 1 ✓
Note Uni bridge & a teaser ▶
Uni Bridge
MATH142 renames this the inner product, $\langle\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b}\rangle$, defined on much stranger spaces (function spaces, complex vector spaces) as long as it satisfies the same properties you just derived for free. The perpendicularity test becomes orthogonality — the backbone of Fourier series and least-squares fitting.
Teaser: in 3D there's a second vector product, the cross product $\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}$, returning a vector perpendicular to both — not in the HSC syllabus, but it appears almost immediately in MATH141.
Section 5D
Angles & Projections
Turning the dot product formula around to extract information.
Now that the dot product connects algebra to angles, can we turn the relationship around — use components to find an angle we couldn't otherwise measure?
Pattern Build it: rearrange for angle, then derive the projection formula ▶

Angle: from 5C, $\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\cos\theta$. Everything except $\theta$ is computable, so just divide:

\[ \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|} \]

Projection: picture $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ tail-to-tail with angle $\theta$. Drop a perpendicular from the head of $\mathbf{a}$ onto the line through $\mathbf{b}$ — the "shadow" length is $|\mathbf{a}|\cos\theta$. Substitute the formula for $\cos\theta$:

\[ |\mathbf{a}|\cos\theta = |\mathbf{a}|\cdot\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{b}|} \]

That's the shadow's length. For the shadow vector, multiply by the unit vector $\hat{\mathbf{b}}=\mathbf{b}/|\mathbf{b}|$:

\[ \left(\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{b}|}\right)\cdot\frac{\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{b}|} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{b}|^2}\right)\mathbf{b} \]
Definition Angle formula and vector projection ▶
\[ \cos\theta = \frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|}, \qquad \text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a} = \left(\frac{\mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{b}|^2}\right)\mathbf{b} \]
Example Worked examples ▶
Example 1 — find an angle

Find the angle between $\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{pmatrix}$ and $\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{pmatrix}$.

\[ \mathbf{a}\cdot\mathbf{b}=1,\ |\mathbf{a}|=|\mathbf{b}|=\sqrt2 \implies \cos\theta=\frac12 \implies \theta=60^\circ \]
ANSWER $60^\circ$
Example 2 — sanity-checked projection

Project $\mathbf{a}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\4\\0\end{pmatrix}$ onto $\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$.

\[ \text{proj}_{\mathbf{b}}\mathbf{a} = \frac{3}{1}\begin{pmatrix}1\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}3\\0\\0\end{pmatrix} \]

Matches intuition: the shadow of $\mathbf{a}$ onto the $x$-axis is just its $x$-component. ✓

ANSWER $\begin{pmatrix}3\\0\\0\end{pmatrix}$
Note Uni bridge ▶
Uni Bridge
This exact formula reappears when MATH142 covers orthogonal projection onto a subspace — the key step in least-squares regression (fitting the "best" line or plane through noisy data). Knowing how it was derived (drop a perpendicular, use trig, rescale) means you'll recognise the geometry when it's dressed up in matrix notation.
Section 5E
Vector Proofs in Geometry
Proving geometric facts with pure algebra — no protractor required.
Could you prove a fact like "the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other" using only vector algebra — without measuring an angle or drawing an accurate diagram?
Pattern Build it: the four-step recipe, demonstrated live ▶

Step 1 — set up coordinates that cost nothing. Pick a convenient origin $O$, a vertex of the parallelogram $OACB$. Let $\overrightarrow{OA}=\mathbf{a}$, $\overrightarrow{OB}=\mathbf{b}$ — arbitrary vectors, which is what makes the proof general.

Step 2 — translate "parallelogram" into an equation. $\overrightarrow{OA}=\overrightarrow{BC}$, so $\overrightarrow{OC}=\overrightarrow{OB}+\overrightarrow{BC}=\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{a}$.

Step 3 — find both midpoints and compare. Midpoint of any segment $PQ$ is $\tfrac12(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q})$ — just averaging.

\[ \text{mid}(OC) = \tfrac12(\mathbf{0}+\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}) = \tfrac12(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}), \qquad \text{mid}(AB) = \tfrac12(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}) \]

Step 4 — the expressions match — not approximately, literally, for any $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$ whatsoever.

1
Name a convenient origin and a small set of vectors.
2
Translate the geometric hypothesis into a vector equation.
3
Express what you're comparing in terms of the same vectors.
4
Show the expressions match.
Definition The result, and the midpoint formula it rests on ▶
\[ \text{Midpoint of } PQ = \tfrac12(\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{q}) \]

Result: the diagonals of a parallelogram bisect each other — proved for every parallelogram at once. $\blacksquare$

Example Worked examples using the recipe ▶
Example 1 — midpoint from any point $P$

$M$ is the midpoint of $AB$. Show $\overrightarrow{PM}=\tfrac12(\overrightarrow{PA}+\overrightarrow{PB})$ for any point $P$.

Let $P$ be the origin: $\overrightarrow{PA}=\mathbf{a}$, $\overrightarrow{PB}=\mathbf{b}$, and by the midpoint formula $\overrightarrow{PM}=\tfrac12(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})=\tfrac12(\overrightarrow{PA}+\overrightarrow{PB})$ directly.

ANSWER Proved directly by choosing $P$ as origin
Example 2 — Varignon's theorem

Show the midpoints of the sides of any quadrilateral form a parallelogram.

Vertices $\mathbf{a},\mathbf{b},\mathbf{c},\mathbf{d}$; midpoints $P=\tfrac12(\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b})$, $Q=\tfrac12(\mathbf{b}+\mathbf{c})$, $R=\tfrac12(\mathbf{c}+\mathbf{d})$, $S=\tfrac12(\mathbf{d}+\mathbf{a})$.

\[ \overrightarrow{PQ}=\tfrac12(\mathbf{c}-\mathbf{a}) = \overrightarrow{SR} \]
ANSWER $PQ \parallel SR$ and equal — $PQRS$ is a parallelogram
Note Uni bridge ▶
Uni Bridge
This "express everything in a small set of vectors, then manipulate algebraically" approach is exactly the mindset MATH141 wants for proofs about linear independence and span. Statements like "three vectors in $\mathbb{R}^3$ are linearly dependent" use the same four-step recipe — just with the geometric picture stripped away.
Section 5F
The Vector Equation of a Line
One point, one direction, one parameter.
A Cartesian equation like $y=2x+1$ describes a line in 2D. Is there a vector version — and what would it mean for a vector "equation" to describe an entire infinite line?
Pattern Build it: point plus direction, swept by a parameter ▶

Step 1 — minimum information for a line. Standing at a known point, walking in a fixed direction, traces exactly one line. One point plus one direction — nothing more, nothing less.

Step 2 — turn it into algebra. Start at $\mathbf{a}$, direction $\mathbf{b}$. "One $\mathbf{b}$'s worth" $\to\mathbf{a}+\mathbf{b}$. "Two $\mathbf{b}$'s worth" $\to\mathbf{a}+2\mathbf{b}$. In general, "$\lambda$ lots of $\mathbf{b}$":

\[ \mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b} \]

As $\lambda$ ranges over every real number, this visits every point on the line and nothing else.

Step 3 — parallel/perpendicular lines. A line's direction lives entirely in $\mathbf{b}$ — the point $\mathbf{a}$ only tells you where it sits. So parallel means $\mathbf{b}_1=k\mathbf{b}_2$; perpendicular reuses the 5C test: $\mathbf{b}_1\cdot\mathbf{b}_2=0$.

Definition Vector equation of a line ▶
\[ \mathbf{r} = \mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}, \qquad \lambda\in\mathbb{R} \]
RelationshipTest on $\mathbf{b}_1,\mathbf{b}_2$
Parallel$\mathbf{b}_1=k\mathbf{b}_2$
Perpendicular$\mathbf{b}_1\cdot\mathbf{b}_2=0$
Example Worked examples ▶
Example 1 — write the equation, find a point

Line through $A(1,2,-1)$, direction $\mathbf{b}=\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}$. Find the point at $\lambda=2$.

\[ \mathbf{r}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\-1\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}, \qquad \lambda=2 \implies \begin{pmatrix}5\\0\\5\end{pmatrix} \]
ANSWER Point $(5,0,5)$
Example 2 — build a line from two points

A line passes through $(2,-1,3)$ and $(5,0,1)$. Build its vector equation.

1
Direction: $\begin{pmatrix}5\\0\\1\end{pmatrix}-\begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix}$
2
Use first point as $\mathbf{a}$
\[ \mathbf{r} = \begin{pmatrix}2\\-1\\3\end{pmatrix}+\lambda\begin{pmatrix}3\\1\\-2\end{pmatrix} \]
ANSWER As above
Note Uni bridge & notation shift ▶
Uni Bridge
$\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}$ is your first parametrisation. MATH142 generalises this to curves $\mathbf{r}(t)$ that bend, and surfaces $\mathbf{r}(u,v)$ with two parameters — the same "point plus direction, swept by a parameter" reasoning.
Notation shift to expect: $\mathbf{r}=(1-\lambda)\mathbf{a}+\lambda\mathbf{b}$ is the same line through $A$ and $B$, phrased so $\lambda=0$ gives $A$, $\lambda=1$ gives $B$. Expand it: $\mathbf{a}+\lambda(\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a})$ — identical to what you derived, direction vector $\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}$.
Section 5G
Vector Equations of Circles, Spheres & Planes
Turning a plain-English definition directly into an equation.
A circle is "every point a fixed distance from a centre." Could that sentence be turned directly into a vector equation, without ever writing $(x-h)^2+(y-k)^2=r^2$ by hand?
Pattern Build it: translate the defining sentence, literally ▶

Circles/spheres. "Every point $\mathbf{v}$ a fixed distance $r$ from centre $\mathbf{c}$" — "distance from $\mathbf{c}$ to $\mathbf{v}$" is $|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{c}|$. So the whole definition is the sentence with symbols swapped in: $|\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{c}|=r$. Works identically in 2D or 3D.

Planes need a different sentence. "The set of points $\mathbf{v}$ such that the displacement from a known point $A$ to $\mathbf{v}$ is perpendicular to a fixed normal $\mathbf{n}$." If $\mathbf{n}$ sticks straight out of a flat sheet, any in-plane displacement is perpendicular to it. Reuse the 5C perpendicularity test:

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{a}) = 0 \]

Expand using the distributive property (free from 5C):

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v} - \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a} = 0 \implies \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a} \]

The right side is just a number once $\mathbf{n}$, $\mathbf{a}$ are known — a single linear equation in the components of $\mathbf{v}$.

Definition Sphere and plane equations ▶
\[ |\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{c}| = r \qquad\qquad \mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{v}-\mathbf{a}) = 0 \iff \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a} \]
Example Worked examples ▶
Example 1 — read off a sphere

Describe $\left|\mathbf{v}-\begin{pmatrix}1\\-2\\0\end{pmatrix}\right|=3$.

ANSWER Sphere, radius 3, centred at $(1,-2,0)$
Example 2 — build a plane equation

Plane through $A(2,0,-1)$, normal $\mathbf{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\3\\-2\end{pmatrix}$.

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a} = 2+0+2 = 4 \implies x+3y-2z=4 \]
ANSWER $x+3y-2z=4$
Note Uni bridge ▶
Uni Bridge
$x+3y-2z=4$ is a single linear equation in three unknowns — stack more and you get a system of linear equations, solved by row reduction in MATH141. A plane is the solution set of one equation; two planes generally meet in a line; three generally meet at a point. The normal vector is literally the coefficient vector — not a coincidence, you derived that connection yourself.

Find the shortest distance from the point $(1,2,3)$ to the plane $x+2y+2z=6$. (Hint: reuse the projection idea from 5D.)
5D + 5G — Challenge
▶
Solution

The shortest distance from a point to a plane is the length of the projection of the displacement (from any point on the plane) onto the unit normal. This gives the point-to-plane distance formula:

\[ \text{distance} = \frac{|\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{p} - d|}{|\mathbf{n}|}, \qquad \mathbf{n}=\begin{pmatrix}1\\2\\2\end{pmatrix},\ d=6 \]
\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{p} = 1+4+6=11, \qquad |\mathbf{n}|=3 \implies \text{distance} = \frac{|11-6|}{3}=\frac{5}{3} \]
ANSWER $\tfrac53$
