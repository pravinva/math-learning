0 Vocabulary first — read this before the formulas

This chapter uses a few vector words constantly. They are short ideas; learn them once and the line/plane formulas become readable.

Four words you will see everywhere

1. Position vector. If a point \(A\) has coordinates \((x_0,y_0,z_0)\), its position vector is the arrow from the origin \(O\) to \(A\):

\[ \mathbf{a}=\overrightarrow{OA}=(x_0,y_0,z_0). \]

So “position vector of \(A\)” just means “the coordinates of \(A\), written as a vector.” We often write \(\mathbf{a}\) for point \(A\), \(\mathbf{r}\) for a general point \(P\), and so on.

2. Direction vector. A direction vector \(\mathbf{d}=(a,b,c)\) of a line tells you which way the line points. It is not a distance and not a point. Any nonzero multiple of \(\mathbf{d}\) (for example \(2\mathbf{d}\) or \(-\mathbf{d}\)) is an equally valid direction for the same line. Think: “travel instructions,” not “a place.”

3. Vector from one point to another. From point \(A\) to point \(B\):

\[ \overrightarrow{AB}=\mathbf{b}-\mathbf{a}=B-A \]

(subtract coordinates). This is the displacement you need to go from \(A\) to \(B\). On a line through \(A\) with direction \(\mathbf{d}\), every such displacement along the line is a multiple of \(\mathbf{d}\).

4. Parameter \(t\). A real number that says how many copies of the direction vector you walk from the known point. \(t=0\) means “still at \(A\)”; positive/negative \(t\) means forward/backward along \(\mathbf{d}\). \(t\) controls distance along the line; \(\mathbf{d}\) itself is the direction.

Quick check. In \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+t\mathbf{d}\): \(\mathbf{a}\) and \(\mathbf{r}\) are position vectors (they locate points). \(\mathbf{d}\) is a direction vector (it does not locate a point by itself). \(t\) is a scalar (how far along that direction).
A 3D lines: forms, checks, and classification

In the plane (\(xy\)), a line is easy to picture: one equation like \(y=mx+c\) is enough. In 3D space there is an extra dimension, and a single equation \(ax+by+cz=d\) actually describes a plane, not a line. So a line in 3D needs a different description. The cleanest idea is geometric:

Pick a point you know is on the line, and pick a direction the line points. Every other point on the line is reached by walking along that direction from the known point — forwards or backwards, any distance.

1. What “parametric” means

A parameter \(t\) is the real number from the vocabulary section: it labels how far you have walked along the direction.

  • When \(t=0\), you are exactly at the known point \(A\) (position vector \(\mathbf{a}\)).
  • When \(t=1\), you have walked exactly one copy of the direction vector \(\mathbf{d}\) from \(A\).
  • When \(t=2\), two copies; when \(t=-1\), one copy in the opposite direction.

So the position vector \(\mathbf{r}\) of a general point \(P\) on the line is

\[ \mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+t\mathbf{d},\qquad t\in\mathbb{R}. \]

That is the vector equation of the line. It is not a different object from the parametric equations — it is the same statement written with vectors instead of three separate coordinates.

Why introduce a parameter at all?

Because a line is one-dimensional inside three-dimensional space. You need exactly one free number to specify where you are on it. The parameter \(t\) is that free number. Without it, you would be stuck trying to force three coordinates \(x,y,z\) to satisfy somehow “being on a line” with no organised way to generate those points.

2. From vector form to parametric equations

Write \(\mathbf{a}=(x_0,y_0,z_0)\) and \(\mathbf{d}=(a,b,c)\). Then \(\mathbf{r}=(x,y,z)\) expands component by component:

\[ x=x_0+at,\qquad y=y_0+bt,\qquad z=z_0+ct. \]

These three are the parametric equations. They are not three independent stories — they are locked together by the same \(t\). If you change \(t\), all three coordinates update in lockstep, which is exactly what keeps you on the line.

Exam tip: given a vector equation, reading off parametric form is immediate. Going the other way, the constants are a point on the line and the coefficients of \(t\) are the direction.

3. Symmetric form — and why it exists

If none of \(a,b,c\) is zero, solve each parametric equation for \(t\):

\[ t=\frac{x-x_0}{a}=\frac{y-y_0}{b}=\frac{z-z_0}{c}. \]

That common value of \(t\) is the symmetric form (sometimes called two-point / Cartesian form of a line). It says: the fractional “how far along each axis, relative to the direction components” must match.

Why bother with symmetric form?

It hides the parameter. Sometimes exam questions give a line that way and ask for a direction vector — you just read the denominators \((a,b,c)\) and a point from the numerators. It is also a compact way to write “these three ratios are equal,” which is useful when checking whether a given point lies on the line (plug in \(x,y,z\) and see if the three fractions agree).

If a direction component is zero — say \(a=0\) — that coordinate never changes: \(x=x_0\) always. You cannot divide by zero, so you write the constant equation separately and keep the other two fractions equal. That is not a special case of geometry; it is just honesty about division.

The three forms side by side

Vector: \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+t\mathbf{d}\)

Parametric: \(x=x_0+at,\ y=y_0+bt,\ z=z_0+ct\)

Symmetric (if \(a,b,c\neq0\)): \(\dfrac{x-x_0}{a}=\dfrac{y-y_0}{b}=\dfrac{z-z_0}{c}\)

They describe the same set of points. Fluency is being able to convert in either direction without thinking twice.

4. Classifying two lines in 3D

In 2D, two distinct lines either intersect or are parallel. In 3D there is a third possibility: skew lines — not parallel, yet never meet, because they live in different “layers” of space.

Checklist:

  1. Parallel? Directions \(\mathbf{d}_1,\mathbf{d}_2\) are scalar multiples. If yes, either they are the same line (any point of one lies on the other) or distinct parallel lines.
  2. Perpendicular directions? \(\mathbf{d}_1\cdot\mathbf{d}_2=0\) (optional side check; lines can be skew and still have perpendicular directions).
  3. Intersect? If not parallel, set \(\mathbf{a}_1+t\mathbf{d}_1=\mathbf{a}_2+s\mathbf{d}_2\) and solve the three component equations for \(t,s\). One consistent pair \(\Rightarrow\) they meet at that point. Inconsistent \(\Rightarrow\) skew.
Worked example

Line \(L_1:\mathbf{r}=(1,2,-1)+t(2,-1,3)\), line \(L_2:\mathbf{r}=(5,0,5)+s(-4,2,-6)\).

\((-4,2,-6)=-2(2,-1,3)\), so directions are parallel.

Check if same line: substitute point \((5,0,5)\) into \(L_1\). From \(1+2t=5\Rightarrow t=2\), then \(2-2=0\), and \(-1+6=5\), consistent. So they are coincident lines.

Reading the skew panel. The gap in the red line is deliberate: it shows the black line passing in front of it. On paper the two lines appear to cross, but in space they are at different depths and never meet. That gap is the only thing separating “skew” from “intersecting” in a picture — which is exactly why you must settle it with algebra by solving for \(t\) and \(s\).

Convert \(\mathbf{r}=(2,-1,4)+t(3,2,-5)\) into parametric equations.

Solution

\(x=2+3t,\ y=-1+2t,\ z=4-5t\).

Write symmetric form of line through \((1,3,-2)\) with direction \((4,-1,2)\).

Solution

\(\dfrac{x-1}{4}=\dfrac{y-3}{-1}=\dfrac{z+2}{2}\).

A3

Find a direction vector for \(\dfrac{x-5}{2}=\dfrac{y+1}{-3}=\dfrac{z-4}{7}\).

Solution

Direction vector is \((2,-3,7)\).

A4

Classify the line pair: \((1,0,2)+t(2,1,-1)\) and \((3,1,1)+s(4,2,-2)\).

Solution

\((4,2,-2)=2(2,1,-1)\), so lines are parallel or coincident. Test point \((3,1,1)\) in first line: \(1+2t=3\Rightarrow t=1\), \(0+1=1\), \(2-1=1\), yes. Coincident.

A5

Classify: \((0,1,2)+t(1,2,0)\) and \((2,2,4)+s(-2,1,1)\).

Solution

Directions not parallel. Solve \(0+t=2-2s,\ 1+2t=2+s,\ 2=4+s\Rightarrow s=-2\). Then \(t=6\), second equation gives \(13\neq0\). No intersection. Skew.

A6

Find intersection (if any): \((1,2,3)+t(1,-1,2)\) and \((4,-1,1)+s(-1,2,1)\).

Solution

From \(x\): \(1+t=4-s\Rightarrow t+s=3\). From \(y\): \(2-t=-1+2s\Rightarrow t+2s=3\). So \(s=0,\ t=3\). Check \(z\): \(3+2(3)=9\), \(1+0=1\), inconsistent. Skew.

A7

Find a line through \((2,1,0)\) parallel to \((3,-2,4)\).

Solution

\(\mathbf{r}=(2,1,0)+t(3,-2,4)\).

A8

Find a line through \((1,-1,2)\) perpendicular to direction \((2,1,2)\) and using direction \((1,-2,0)\).

Solution

Check orthogonality: \((1,-2,0)\cdot(2,1,2)=2-2+0=0\). So line is \(\mathbf{r}=(1,-1,2)+t(1,-2,0)\).

A9

Point \(P(5,3,-1)\) on line \((1,-1,2)+t(2,4,-3)\)?

Solution

From \(x\): \(1+2t=5\Rightarrow t=2\). Then \(y=-1+8=7\neq3\). Not on the line.

A10

For line \(\mathbf{r}=(3,0,-2)+t(-2,1,2)\), find point at \(t=-3\).

Solution

\((x,y,z)=(3+6,\ 0-3,\ -2-6)=(9,-3,-8)\).

A11

Are \((1,2,3)+t(0,2,-1)\) and \((4,8,0)+s(0,4,-2)\) parallel, coincident, or distinct parallel?

Solution

Directions are multiples: \((0,4,-2)=2(0,2,-1)\). Check if second point on first: \(x=1\) always on first, but second has \(x=4\). So distinct parallel.

A12

Find direction cosines for direction vector \((2,-3,6)\).

Solution

\(|\mathbf{d}|=\sqrt{4+9+36}=7\). Direction cosines \((\cos\alpha,\cos\beta,\cos\gamma)=\left(\frac{2}{7},-\frac{3}{7},\frac{6}{7}\right)\).

B Planes: forms, normals, and angle relations

A plane is a flat 2D sheet sitting in 3D space. You could describe it with a point plus two independent directions lying in the sheet (parametric form \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+s\mathbf{u}+t\mathbf{v}\)). That works, but exams almost always use a different packaging: a point plus a normal vector.

1. Why a normal is the right descriptor

Recall: a position vector locates a point from the origin. For planes we need one more word.

Normal vector \(\mathbf{n}\). A vector that sticks straight out of the plane (perpendicular to every direction that lies in the plane). It is not a point on the plane and not a direction along the plane — it is the “outward spike” that defines the tilt of the sheet.

There are infinitely many directions inside a plane. Listing them all is hopeless. Instead, notice that every in-plane direction is perpendicular to one fixed nonzero vector \(\mathbf{n}\) — that normal.

So fix a point \(A\) on the plane with position vector \(\mathbf{a}\). A point \(P\) with position vector \(\mathbf{r}\) lies on the plane if and only if the connecting vector \(\overrightarrow{AP}=\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{a}\) is perpendicular to \(\mathbf{n}\):

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{r}-\mathbf{a})=0. \]

That single scalar equation is one linear constraint on \((x,y,z)\). One constraint cuts space from 3D down to 2D — exactly a plane. That is why the normal description is so apt: it encodes the whole flatness condition in one clean equation.

2. Cartesian form

With \(\mathbf{n}=(a,b,c)\) and \(A=(x_0,y_0,z_0)\), expand the dot product:

\[ a(x-x_0)+b(y-y_0)+c(z-z_0)=0 \quad\Rightarrow\quad ax+by+cz=d, \]

where \(d=\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{a}\). The coefficients of \(x,y,z\) are the normal. So from \(2x-y+3z=8\) you immediately read \(\mathbf{n}=(2,-1,3)\).

3. Building a plane from three points

Given non-collinear \(A,B,C\), both \(\overrightarrow{AB}\) and \(\overrightarrow{AC}\) lie in the plane. Their cross product

\[ \mathbf{n}=\overrightarrow{AB}\times\overrightarrow{AC} \]

is automatically perpendicular to both, hence a normal. Then use \(A\) and \(\mathbf{n}\) in the point-normal equation. (If the cross product is \(\mathbf{0}\), the points are collinear and do not determine a unique plane.)

4. Angles and parallelism via normals

The angle between two planes equals the angle between their normals (tilting a plane tilts its normal by the same amount). By convention we take the acute angle:

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{|\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2|}{|\mathbf{n}_1||\mathbf{n}_2|}. \]

Parallel planes \(\Leftrightarrow\) parallel normals. Perpendicular planes \(\Leftrightarrow\) \(\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2=0\).

A line with direction \(\mathbf{d}\) is parallel to a plane (never meets it, or lies in it) precisely when \(\mathbf{d}\) is perpendicular to the normal: \(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\). The line is perpendicular to the plane when \(\mathbf{d}\) is parallel to \(\mathbf{n}\).

Core toolkit

General plane: \(ax+by+cz=d\), normal \(\mathbf{n}=(a,b,c)\). Through point \(P_0\): \(a(x-x_0)+b(y-y_0)+c(z-z_0)=0\).

Angle between planes from normals: \(\cos\theta=\dfrac{|\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2|}{|\mathbf{n}_1||\mathbf{n}_2|}\). Line parallel to plane iff \(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\).

Worked example

Find plane through \(A(1,0,2)\) with normal \((2,-1,3)\).

Use point-normal form: \(2(x-1)-1(y-0)+3(z-2)=0\).

Simplify: \(2x-y+3z=8\).

Plane through points \(A(1,0,0),B(0,1,0),C(0,0,1)\).

Solution

Intercept form is \(x+y+z=1\).

B4

Are planes \(2x-y+z=3\) and \(4x-2y+2z=9\) parallel, identical, or intersecting?

Solution

Normals are parallel (second is 2 times first). Constants not same ratio (\(3\times2=6\neq9\)). Distinct parallel planes.

B5

Angle between planes \(x+y+z=0\) and \(x-y+2z=0\).

Solution

\(\mathbf{n}_1=(1,1,1),\mathbf{n}_2=(1,-1,2)\). Dot \(=2\). Magnitudes \(\sqrt3,\sqrt6\). \(\cos\theta=\frac{2}{\sqrt{18}}=\frac{2}{3\sqrt2}\). \(\theta\approx61.9^\circ\).

B6

Line direction \((2,-1,3)\) and plane \(2x+4y-2z=7\): line parallel/perpendicular/neither to plane?

Solution

Plane normal \((2,4,-2)\). Dot with direction \(=4-4-6=-6\neq0\), so not parallel. Direction not scalar multiple of normal, so not perpendicular. Neither.

B7

Find plane parallel to \(3x-y+4z=10\) through \((2,1,-1)\).

Solution

Same normal: \(3x-y+4z=d\). Substitute point: \(6-1-4=1\). Plane: \(3x-y+4z=1\).

B8

Find plane perpendicular to \(x-2y+2z=5\) with normal parallel to line direction \((1,2,2)\), through origin.

Solution

Required normal \((1,2,2)\). Through origin gives \(x+2y+2z=0\).

B9

Check if point \(P(1,-2,3)\) lies on \(2x+y-z=-3\).

Solution

\(2(1)+(-2)-3=-3\). Yes, lies on the plane.

B10

Find intercepts of plane \(2x+3y+6z=12\).

Solution

\(x\)-intercept \(=6\), \(y\)-intercept \(=4\), \(z\)-intercept \(=2\). Points: \((6,0,0),(0,4,0),(0,0,2)\).

B11

If two planes are perpendicular, what relation must normals satisfy?

Solution

\(\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2=0\).

B12

Find \(k\) so planes \(x+2y+kz=1\) and \(2x-y+3z=4\) are perpendicular.

Solution

Normals \((1,2,k)\), \((2,-1,3)\). Dot \(=2-2+3k=3k\). Set 0 gives \(k=0\).

C Intersections and systems in 3D

Once lines and planes have equations, asking “where do they meet?” is just algebra. The geometry tells you what kind of answer to expect; the algebra finds the actual points.

1. Line meeting a plane

A generic line hits a plane in exactly one point. Why? The line has one free parameter \(t\); the plane imposes one equation. One unknown, one constraint — typically one solution.

Method: write the line parametrically, substitute \(x(t),y(t),z(t)\) into the plane equation, solve for \(t\), then plug back to get the point.

Special cases (worth recognising before you grind algebra):

  • If \(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\) and a point of the line satisfies the plane, the whole line lies in the plane (infinitely many intersections).
  • If \(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\) but no point of the line satisfies the plane, the line is parallel to the plane and never meets it.
Picture: \(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\) means the line’s direction is flat relative to the plane (orthogonal to the normal), so the line never “dives into” the sheet. Whether it already sits on the sheet decides “contained” vs “misses forever.”
2. Two planes meeting

Two distinct non-parallel planes intersect in a line. Both normals are perpendicular to that intersection line, so the line’s direction must be perpendicular to both normals:

\[ \mathbf{d}=\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2. \]

Then find any one shared point (set one free variable, solve the two plane equations) and write \(\mathbf{r}=\mathbf{a}+t\mathbf{d}\).

If the normals are parallel, the planes are parallel: either identical (same equation up to scale) or distinct with no intersection.

3. Three planes / linear systems

Three plane equations are a \(3\times3\) linear system. Geometrically you may get a unique point (three sheets meet at a corner), a whole line (two of the planes coincide or are dependent), nothing (inconsistent), or a whole plane (all three are the same). The algebraic symptoms — unique solution, free variable, or contradiction — match those pictures.

Workflow summary

Line with plane: substitute parametric line into plane and solve one parameter.

Plane with plane: direction \(\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2\), then find one shared point.

Always pause to check parallelism (\(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\) or parallel normals) before declaring “one intersection point.”

C1

Intersect line \((1,0,2)+t(2,-1,1)\) with plane \(x+y+z=5\).

Solution

\((1+2t)+(0-t)+(2+t)=5\Rightarrow3+2t=5\Rightarrow t=1\). Intersection point \((3,-1,3)\).

C2

Determine if line \((2,1,-1)+t(1,2,3)\) is parallel to plane \(x-2y+z=4\).

Solution

Normal \((1,-2,1)\). Dot with direction \(1-4+3=0\). Direction parallel to plane. Check one point: \(2-2-1=-1\neq4\). So line is parallel and distinct (no intersection).

C3

Find intersection line of planes \(x+y+z=6\) and \(x-y+z=2\).

Solution

Subtract equations: \(2y=4\Rightarrow y=2\). Then \(x+z=4\Rightarrow x=4-z\). Let \(z=t\). Line: \((x,y,z)=(4-t,2,t)\) or \(\mathbf{r}=(4,2,0)+t(-1,0,1)\).

C4

Find intersection line of \(2x-y+z=1\) and \(x+3y-z=7\).

Solution

Add equations: \(3x+2y=8\Rightarrow x=\frac{8-2y}{3}\). Let \(y=t\). From first: \(z=1-2x+y=1-\frac{16-4t}{3}+t=\frac{-13+7t}{3}\). Line: \(\left(\frac{8}{3},0,-\frac{13}{3}\right)+t\left(-\frac{2}{3},1,\frac{7}{3}\right)\).

C5

Do planes \(x+2y-z=3\), \(2x+4y-2z=6\), \(3x-y+z=5\) intersect in one point?

Solution

First two are same plane (second is double first). Solve first and third jointly gives a line. So not a unique point; infinite points on their intersection line.

C6

Line through \(A(0,1,2)\) and \(B(3,-2,5)\): find line-plane intersection with \(x+y+z=4\).

Solution

Direction \(B-A=(3,-3,3)\). Line: \((0,1,2)+t(3,-3,3)\). Substitute: \(3t+(1-3t)+(2+3t)=4\Rightarrow3+3t=4\Rightarrow t=\frac13\). Point \((1,0,3)\).

C7

Find parameter \(k\) so line \((1,2,0)+t(1,k,2)\) lies in plane \(2x-y+z=0\).

Solution

All points must satisfy plane. Base point gives \(2(1)-2+0=0\), good. Direction must be orthogonal to normal \((2,-1,1)\): \(2(1)-k+2=0\Rightarrow k=4\).

C8

Do lines \((1,0,1)+t(2,1,-1)\) and \((3,1,0)+s(1,0,2)\) intersect?

Solution

From \(y\): \(t=1\). Then \(x=3\), so \(3=3+s\Rightarrow s=0\). Check \(z\): first \(=0\), second \(=0\). Yes, intersect at \((3,1,0)\).

C9

For planes \(x+y+z=1\) and \(2x-y+z=3\), find direction vector of their intersection line.

Solution

\(\mathbf{n}_1=(1,1,1)\), \(\mathbf{n}_2=(2,-1,1)\). Cross product \(\mathbf{d}=\mathbf{n}_1\times\mathbf{n}_2=(2,1,-3)\) (or any scalar multiple).

C10

Solve system: \(x+y+z=4,\ x-y+z=2,\ 2x+z=5\).

Solution

Subtract first two: \(2y=2\Rightarrow y=1\). First gives \(x+z=3\). Third gives \(2x+z=5\). Subtract: \(x=2\), hence \(z=1\). Point \((2,1,1)\).

C11

Line \((0,0,0)+t(1,2,3)\) with plane \(2x-4y+2z=8\): intersect, parallel, or in-plane?

Solution

Direction dot normal \((2,-4,2)\cdot(1,2,3)=2-8+6=0\), so line parallel to plane. Origin gives 0 not 8, so not in plane. Distinct parallel, no intersection.

C12

Find line through intersection of planes \(x+y=2\), \(y+z=1\), and passing through point with \(z=0\).

Solution

Let \(y=t\). Then \(x=2-t,\ z=1-t\). For \(z=0\), \(t=1\), point \((1,1,0)\). Direction from parametric form is \((-1,1,-1)\). Line: \((1,1,0)+s(-1,1,-1)\).

D Distances and angles in full 3D

Every distance formula in this section is the same idea in different clothing: find the unique direction that is perpendicular to the object, then project a connecting vector onto that direction. We will derive each formula from that principle, not just quote it. Keep a pencil handy — the algebra is short once the geometry is clear.

Shared engine. Point–plane uses the plane’s normal. Point–line uses the cross product with the line’s direction (parallelogram height). Skew lines use the common perpendicular \(\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2\). Angles come from the ordinary dot-product formula, sometimes rearranged.
1. Distance from a point to a plane — derivation

Setup. Plane \(\Pi\) with normal \(\mathbf{n}=(a,b,c)\), written \(ax+by+cz+d=0\). External point \(P=(x_0,y_0,z_0)\). Let \(Q\) be any point already on the plane (so \(aq_1+bq_2+cq_3+d=0\)).

Geometry. The shortest path from \(P\) to \(\Pi\) must be perpendicular to every direction in \(\Pi\), hence parallel to \(\mathbf{n}\). So the distance \(h\) is the absolute value of the component of \(\overrightarrow{QP}\) along the unit normal \(\hat{\mathbf{n}}=\mathbf{n}/|\mathbf{n}|\):

\[ h=\bigl|\overrightarrow{QP}\cdot\hat{\mathbf{n}}\bigr| =\frac{\bigl|\mathbf{n}\cdot\overrightarrow{QP}\bigr|}{|\mathbf{n}|}. \]

Algebra. Expand the numerator:

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot\overrightarrow{QP} =\mathbf{n}\cdot(\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{q}) =\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{q}. \]

But \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{q}=-d\) (because \(Q\) lies on the plane), and \(\mathbf{n}\cdot\mathbf{p}=ax_0+by_0+cz_0\), so

\[ \mathbf{n}\cdot\overrightarrow{QP}=ax_0+by_0+cz_0+d. \]

Therefore

\[ \boxed{\mathrm{dist}(P,\Pi)=\dfrac{|ax_0+by_0+cz_0+d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}}. \]

Notice \(Q\) cancelled out: any point on the plane works. Also watch the form of the plane equation — if your book writes \(ax+by+cz=k\), rewrite as \(ax+by+cz-k=0\) before using the formula.

Quick check of the derivation

Plane \(x+2y+2z-5=0\), point \(P(2,-1,3)\). Numerator \(|2-2+6-5|=1\). Denominator \(\sqrt{1+4+4}=3\). Distance \(=1/3\).

Bonus: parallel planes. Two planes \(ax+by+cz+d_1=0\) and \(ax+by+cz+d_2=0\) share the same normal. Pick any point on the second plane: its distance to the first is \(|d_1-d_2|/|\mathbf{n}|\) (the \(ax+by+cz\) terms cancel). So

\[ \mathrm{dist}=\frac{|d_1-d_2|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}. \]
2. Distance from a point to a line — derivation

Setup. Line through \(A\) with direction \(\mathbf{d}\). External point \(P\). Let \(h\) be the perpendicular distance from \(P\) to the line.

Geometry. Vectors \(\overrightarrow{AP}\) and \(\mathbf{d}\) span a parallelogram. The base of that parallelogram can be taken as \(\mathbf{d}\) (length \(|\mathbf{d}|\)). The corresponding height is exactly \(h\) — the distance we want.

Area fact. The magnitude of a cross product is the parallelogram area:

\[ \bigl|\overrightarrow{AP}\times\mathbf{d}\bigr| =(\text{base})\times(\text{height}) =|\mathbf{d}|\cdot h. \]

Solve for \(h\):

\[ \boxed{\mathrm{dist}(P,\text{line})=\dfrac{\bigl|\overrightarrow{AP}\times\mathbf{d}\bigr|}{|\mathbf{d}|}}. \]
Same result via \(\sin\theta\)

If \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\overrightarrow{AP}\) and \(\mathbf{d}\), then \(h=|\overrightarrow{AP}|\,|\sin\theta|\). But \(|\overrightarrow{AP}\times\mathbf{d}|=|\overrightarrow{AP}|\,|\mathbf{d}|\,|\sin\theta|\), so dividing by \(|\mathbf{d}|\) recovers \(h\). The cross-product form is usually faster for computation because you never need to find \(\theta\).

Quick check

\(A=(1,0,2)\), \(\mathbf{d}=(2,1,-1)\), \(P=(3,2,1)\). \(\overrightarrow{AP}=(2,2,-1)\). Cross product \((-1,0,-2)\), magnitude \(\sqrt5\). \(|\mathbf{d}|=\sqrt6\). Distance \(\sqrt5/\sqrt6=\sqrt{30}/6\).

3. Distance between skew lines — derivation

Setup. Line \(\mathcal{L}_1\) through \(A_1\) with direction \(\mathbf{d}_1\). Line \(\mathcal{L}_2\) through \(A_2\) with direction \(\mathbf{d}_2\). Assume the lines are not parallel, so \(\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2\neq\mathbf{0}\).

Step 1 — common perpendicular. Any vector perpendicular to both lines must be parallel to

\[ \mathbf{n}=\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2. \]

The shortest gap between the lines is measured along this common perpendicular.

Step 2 — connecting vector. The vector \(\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}\) generally has pieces along \(\mathbf{d}_1\), along \(\mathbf{d}_2\), and along \(\mathbf{n}\). Only the \(\mathbf{n}\)-piece contributes to separation.

Step 3 — volume over area. The three vectors \(\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}\), \(\mathbf{d}_1\), \(\mathbf{d}_2\) span a parallelepiped whose volume is the absolute value of the scalar triple product:

\[ V=\bigl|\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}\cdot(\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2)\bigr|. \]

The base of that parallelepiped (spanned by \(\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(\mathbf{d}_2\)) has area \(|\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2|\). Volume \(=\) base area \(\times\) height, and the height is precisely the distance \(h\) between the two lines:

\[ V=|\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2|\cdot h. \]

Step 4 — solve:

\[ \boxed{\mathrm{dist}=\dfrac{\big|\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}\cdot(\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2)\big|}{|\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2|}}. \]
Sanity checks built into the formula

If the lines intersect, you can choose \(A_1=A_2\) at the intersection (or more generally \(\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}\) lies in the plane of \(\mathbf{d}_1,\mathbf{d}_2\)), so the triple product is \(0\) and distance is \(0\).

If the lines are parallel, \(\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2=\mathbf{0}\) and this formula is undefined — correctly so. Use the point-to-line formula instead: pick any point on one line and measure its distance to the other.

How to read the diagram. The blue dashed line is \(\mathcal{L}_1\) and the red dashed line is \(\mathcal{L}_2\). Slide a copy of \(\mathbf{d}_2\) to \(A_1\): now \(\mathbf{d}_1\) and \(\mathbf{d}_2\) span the shaded blue base (area \(|\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2|\)), and \(\mathcal{L}_1\) lies inside that blue plane. The red plane above is the parallel copy containing \(\mathcal{L}_2\). The purple edge \(\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}\) links the two, so the solid is a parallelepiped of volume \(V=|\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}\cdot(\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2)|\). Its green height \(h\) is the gap between the two parallel planes — which is exactly the shortest distance between the skew lines.
4. Angles — derivations

(a) Angle between two lines. Directions \(\mathbf{d}_1,\mathbf{d}_2\). By the ordinary angle formula for vectors,

\[ \cos\theta=\frac{\mathbf{d}_1\cdot\mathbf{d}_2}{|\mathbf{d}_1||\mathbf{d}_2|}. \]

Lines have no preferred “forward,” so \(\mathbf{d}\) and \(-\mathbf{d}\) describe the same line. By convention we report the acute angle, which means inserting absolute value:

\[ \boxed{\cos\theta=\dfrac{|\mathbf{d}_1\cdot\mathbf{d}_2|}{|\mathbf{d}_1||\mathbf{d}_2|}}. \]

(b) Angle between a line and a plane. Let \(\alpha\) be the angle between the line and the plane (the angle between the line and its projection onto the plane). Let \(\phi\) be the angle between the line direction \(\mathbf{d}\) and the plane normal \(\mathbf{n}\). In a plane cut perpendicular to the line of intersection of the given plane with a plane containing \(\mathbf{d}\) and \(\mathbf{n}\), you see a right triangle: \(\phi\) and \(\alpha\) are complementary,

\[ \alpha=90^\circ-\phi\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\sin\alpha=\cos\phi=\frac{|\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}|}{|\mathbf{d}||\mathbf{n}|}. \]

(Absolute value again, so \(\alpha\) is taken acute / between \(0^\circ\) and \(90^\circ\).)

\[ \boxed{\sin\alpha=\dfrac{|\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}|}{|\mathbf{d}||\mathbf{n}|}}. \]

Special cases fall out immediately: \(\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}=0\Rightarrow\alpha=0^\circ\) (line parallel to plane); \(\mathbf{d}\parallel\mathbf{n}\Rightarrow\alpha=90^\circ\) (line perpendicular to plane).

(c) Angle between two planes. As in Section B: tilting a plane tilts its normal by the same amount, so the dihedral angle equals the angle between normals. With absolute value for the acute angle:

\[ \boxed{\cos\theta=\dfrac{|\mathbf{n}_1\cdot\mathbf{n}_2|}{|\mathbf{n}_1||\mathbf{n}_2|}}. \]

The cleanest way to see this is to look straight down the line where the planes meet. That line shrinks to a single point, each plane appears edge-on as a line, and the two angles below are visibly the same.

Formulas after derivation (keep cold)

Point–plane: \(\dfrac{|ax_0+by_0+cz_0+d|}{\sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}}\) for \(ax+by+cz+d=0\).

Parallel planes: \(\dfrac{|d_1-d_2|}{|\mathbf{n}|}\).

Point–line: \(\dfrac{|\overrightarrow{AP}\times\mathbf{d}|}{|\mathbf{d}|}\).

Skew lines: \(\dfrac{|\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}\cdot(\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2)|}{|\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2|}\).

Line–plane angle: \(\sin\alpha=\dfrac{|\mathbf{d}\cdot\mathbf{n}|}{|\mathbf{d}||\mathbf{n}|}\).

D1

Distance from \(P(2,-1,3)\) to plane \(x+2y+2z-5=0\).

Solution

\(\frac{|2+2(-1)+2(3)-5|}{\sqrt{1+4+4}}=\frac{|1|}{3}=\frac13\).

D2

Distance from \(Q(0,4,-2)\) to plane \(2x-y+2z+1=0\).

Solution

\(\frac{|0-4-4+1|}{\sqrt{4+1+4}}=\frac{7}{3}\).

D3

Distance from point \(P(3,2,1)\) to line \((1,0,2)+t(2,1,-1)\).

Solution

\(\overrightarrow{AP}=(2,2,-1)\), \(\mathbf{d}=(2,1,-1)\). Cross \(=( -1,0,-2)\), magnitude \(\sqrt5\). \(|\mathbf{d}|=\sqrt6\). Distance \(=\sqrt5/\sqrt6=\sqrt{30}/6\).

D4

Angle between line direction \((1,2,2)\) and plane \(2x-y+2z=4\).

Solution

Normal \((2,-1,2)\). \(\sin\phi=\dfrac{|d\cdot n|}{|d||n|}=\dfrac{|2-2+4|}{3\cdot3}=\frac49\). So \(\phi=\sin^{-1}(4/9)\approx26.4^\circ\).

D5

Angle between lines with directions \((2,1,-2)\) and \((1,-2,2)\).

Solution

Dot \(=2-2-4=-4\). Magnitudes \(3,3\). Acute angle uses absolute value: \(\cos\theta=4/9\), \(\theta\approx63.6^\circ\).

D6

Shortest distance between skew lines \(L_1:(0,0,0)+t(1,1,0)\), \(L_2:(1,0,2)+s(0,1,1)\).

Solution

\(\mathbf{d}_1\times\mathbf{d}_2=(1,-1,1)\), magnitude \(\sqrt3\). \(\overrightarrow{A_1A_2}=(1,0,2)\). Dot with cross \(=1+0+2=3\). Distance \(=|3|/\sqrt3=\sqrt3\).

D7

Distance between parallel planes \(3x-2y+6z=4\) and \(3x-2y+6z=-8\).

Solution

Write as \(3x-2y+6z-4=0\), \(3x-2y+6z+8=0\). Distance \(=\frac{|(-4)-(+8)|}{\sqrt{9+4+36}}=\frac{12}{7}\).

D8

Find point-plane distance from origin to \(4x+4y+7z=14\).

Solution

\(\frac{|0+0+0-14|}{\sqrt{16+16+49}}=\frac{14}{9}\).

D9

Distance from \(P(2,1,0)\) to line through \(A(1,1,1)\) and \(B(4,3,2)\).

Solution

\(\mathbf{d}=B-A=(3,2,1)\), \(\overrightarrow{AP}=(1,0,-1)\). Cross \((2,-4,2)\), magnitude \(2\sqrt6\). \(|d|=\sqrt{14}\). Distance \(=2\sqrt6/\sqrt{14}=2\sqrt{21}/7\).

D10

Find angle between planes \(x+z=0\) and \(x+y=0\).

Solution

Normals \((1,0,1)\), \((1,1,0)\). Dot \(=1\). Magnitudes \(\sqrt2,\sqrt2\). \(\cos\theta=1/2\Rightarrow\theta=60^\circ\).

D11

Line direction \((1,1,1)\) with plane \(x+y+z=7\): angle between line and plane?

Solution

Plane normal \((1,1,1)\) is parallel to line direction. Line is perpendicular to plane, so line-plane angle is \(90^\circ\).

D12

For point \(P(4,-2,1)\), find foot of perpendicular to plane \(x-y+2z=3\).

Solution

Normal \((1,-1,2)\). Foot is \(P-\lambda n\). Enforce plane:

\((4-\lambda)-(-2+\lambda)+2(1-2\lambda)=3\Rightarrow8-6\lambda=3\Rightarrow\lambda=5/6\).

Foot \(=\left(4-\frac56,\ -2+\frac56,\ 1-\frac{10}{6}\right)=\left(\frac{19}{6},-\frac{7}{6},-\frac{2}{3}\right)\).
