4.7AThe Weierstrass $t$ substitution

Set $t=\tan(x/2)$. This turns every rational expression in $\sin x$ and $\cos x$ into a rational expression in $t$. It is especially useful when neither a simple identity nor an obvious $u$-substitution clears the mixture of sine and cosine.

The three results

$$\sin x=\frac{2t}{1+t^2},\qquad\cos x=\frac{1-t^2}{1+t^2},\qquad dx=\frac{2\,dt}{1+t^2}.$$

Substitute all three, simplify fully in $t$, integrate, then return to $t=\tan(x/2)$.

Worked example

Find $\int dx/(1+\sin x)$. The denominator becomes $(1+t^2+2t)/(1+t^2)=(t+1)^2/(1+t^2)$, so

$$\int\frac{2\,dt/(1+t^2)}{(t+1)^2/(1+t^2)}=\int\frac{2}{(t+1)^2}\,dt=-\frac2{t+1}+C.$$

Therefore $-2/(1+\tan(x/2))+C$. It may look different from an answer produced by another method; differentiating is the reliable comparison.

Domain note

$t=\tan(x/2)$ is undefined at odd multiples of $\pi$. Antiderivatives are always considered on intervals where the original integrand is defined, so record any exclusions from the original question.

4.7BChoosing a trig substitution for radicals

Trig substitutions are designed so a Pythagorean identity replaces the square root by a square. Match the expression under the radical before doing any algebra:

Pattern map

$$\sqrt{a^2-x^2}:\ x=a\sin\theta;\qquad \sqrt{a^2+x^2}:\ x=a\tan\theta;\qquad \sqrt{x^2-a^2}:\ x=a\sec\theta.$$

Then $\sqrt{a^2-a^2\sin^2\theta}=a\cos\theta$, $\sqrt{a^2+a^2\tan^2\theta}=a\sec\theta$, and $\sqrt{a^2\sec^2\theta-a^2}=a|\tan\theta|$. Choose a suitable $\theta$-interval so the sign is known.

When the question specifies a substitution

If an exam says “use $x=a\sin\theta$”, show the required $dx$ and transform the limits if the integral is definite. Do not switch back to $x$ partway through. The instruction is testing your ability to execute the method, not to find a shortcut.

Worked example

For $\int\sqrt{9-x^2}\,dx$, take $x=3\sin\theta$, $dx=3\cos\theta\,d\theta$. The radical is $3\cos\theta$, giving $9\int\cos^2\theta\,d\theta$. After a half-angle step this is $\frac92\theta+\frac94\sin2\theta+C$.

Back-substitute: $\theta=\arcsin(x/3)$ and $\sin2\theta=2\sin\theta\cos\theta=2x\sqrt{9-x^2}/9$. Hence $\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{9-x^2}+\frac92\arcsin(x/3)+C$.

4.7CRationalising the numerator

A radical in a numerator can sometimes be made useful by multiplying by its conjugate. This is not decoration: it creates a difference of squares, often revealing a denominator that cancels or a standard $f'/f$ pattern.

Before a long substitution

Try rewriting $\sqrt{x^2+a^2}-x$ as $\frac{a^2}{\sqrt{x^2+a^2}+x}$ by multiplying by the conjugate. This often exposes a reciprocal form or simplifies a definite-integral pairing.

PracticeTry before revealing

1
Using $t=\tan(x/2)$, find $\int\frac{dx}{1+\cos x}$.
Solution$1+\cos x=2/(1+t^2)$ and $dx=2dt/(1+t^2)$, so the integral is $\int dt=t+C=\tan(x/2)+C$.
2
Use $t$ to find $\int\csc x\,dx$.
Solution$\sin x=2t/(1+t^2)$, hence $\int [2dt/(1+t^2)]/[2t/(1+t^2)]=\int dt/t=\ln|t|+C=\ln|\tan(x/2)|+C$.
3
Use $t$ to find $\int\frac{dx}{2+\cos x}$.
Solution$2+\cos x=(3+t^2)/(1+t^2)$, so the integral is $\int2dt/(t^2+3)=\frac2{\sqrt3}\arctan(t/\sqrt3)+C$; replace $t$ by $\tan(x/2)$.
4
Evaluate $\int\frac{dx}{\sqrt{16-x^2}}$ by $x=4\sin\theta$.
Solution$dx=4\cos\theta d\theta$, radical $=4\cos\theta$, so the integral is $\theta+C=\arcsin(x/4)+C$.
5
Evaluate $\int\frac{dx}{x^2+9}$ by $x=3\tan\theta$.
Solution$dx=3\sec^2\theta d\theta$, denominator $9\sec^2\theta$. The result is $\theta/3+C=\frac{1}{3}\arctan(x/3)+C$.
6
Evaluate $\int\sqrt{4+x^2}\,dx$ using $x=2\tan\theta$.
SolutionThe transformed integral is $4\int\sec^3\theta d\theta$. Thus $2\sec\theta\tan\theta+2\ln|\sec\theta+\tan\theta|+C$, or $\frac{x}{2}\sqrt{x^2+4}+2\ln|x+\sqrt{x^2+4}|+C$ (absorbing a constant).
7
State an appropriate substitution for $\sqrt{x^2-25}$ and explain the identity used.
SolutionUse $x=5\sec\theta$. Then $x^2-25=25(\sec^2\theta-1)=25\tan^2\theta$, by $\sec^2\theta-1=\tan^2\theta$.
8
Evaluate $\int_0^3\frac{dx}{\sqrt{9-x^2}}$ using $x=3\sin\theta$.
SolutionThe transformed bounds are $\theta=0$ to $\pi/2$. The integrand becomes $d\theta$, so the answer is $\pi/2$.
9
Rationalise $\sqrt{x^2+1}-x$.
SolutionMultiply by $(\sqrt{x^2+1}+x)/(\sqrt{x^2+1}+x)$: $\sqrt{x^2+1}-x=\frac{1}{\sqrt{x^2+1}+x}$.
10
Why is $x=3\sin\theta$ a poor first choice for $\sqrt{x^2+9}$?
SolutionIt produces $\sqrt{9(1+\sin^2\theta)}$, which is not simplified by a Pythagorean identity. $x=3\tan\theta$ gives $3\sqrt{1+\tan^2\theta}=3\sec\theta$, exactly the intended cancellation.
