Recurrence relationships

Build reduction formulae from integration by parts, use parity-aware base cases, and turn families of integrals into reliable sequences of smaller calculations.

4.9A What a recurrence is and how to derive one

A recurrence relationship, also called a reduction formula, relates a member of an indexed integral family to an earlier, simpler member. It is useful when the same structure occurs for many powers: derive once, then calculate systematically. The essential habits are to define the family clearly, reduce its index, and identify the correct base cases.

Let $I_n=\int\sin^n x\,dx$. Split off one sine and use parts with $u=\sin^{n-1}x$, $dv=\sin x\,dx$. Then $du=(n-1)\sin^{n-2}x\cos x\,dx$ and $v=-\cos x$.

DERIVATION / SINE POWER$$I_n=-\sin^{n-1}x\cos x+(n-1)\int\sin^{n-2}x\cos^2x\,dx.$$

Replace $\cos^2x$ by $1-\sin^2x$. The new integral becomes $I_{n-2}-I_n$, so

$$I_n=-\sin^{n-1}x\cos x+(n-1)(I_{n-2}-I_n).$$

Collect the $I_n$ terms: $nI_n=-\sin^{n-1}x\cos x+(n-1)I_{n-2}$. Therefore

$$I_n=-\frac{\sin^{n-1}x\cos x}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}I_{n-2}.$$
PITFALL / THE COEFFICIENT IS EARNED

The denominator $n$ arises when $I_n+(n-1)I_n$ is combined. If your result has no $n$ in the denominator, retrace the collection step. Also keep $I_{n-2}$ as the same family; replacing it with a different integrand breaks the recurrence.

4.9B Definite trigonometric families

For $S_n=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^n x\,dx$, evaluate the product term at the endpoints. For $n\ge2$ it vanishes at both ends, leaving a particularly clean recurrence:

$$S_n=\frac{n-1}{n}S_{n-2},\qquad S_0=\frac\pi2,\quad S_1=1.$$

Reducing by two divides the family into two tracks. Even exponents finish at $S_0$ and therefore include $\pi$; odd exponents finish at $S_1$ and therefore are rational. This parity observation lets you sanity-check a result before doing any arithmetic.

WORKED EXAMPLE / $S_6$

Reduce successively:

$$S_6=\frac56S_4=\frac56\cdot\frac34S_2=\frac56\cdot\frac34\cdot\frac12S_0.$$

Now insert $S_0=\frac\pi2$:

$$S_6=\frac{5\cdot3\cdot1}{6\cdot4\cdot2}\cdot\frac\pi2=\frac{5\pi}{32}.$$

Writing every link prevents an accidental change from an even to an odd base case.

4.9C Other families and when reduction is worthwhile

The approach is broader than powers of sine. For $J_n=\int x^ne^x\,dx$, parts produces $J_n=x^ne^x-nJ_{n-1}$. This lowers the power by one and terminates at $J_0$. A recurrence is worthwhile when the lowered-index integral is recognisably in the same family and reaches a known base case.

Do not force a recurrence where a single substitution is shorter. For example, an integral containing a matching inner derivative should usually be handled by substitution, even if it contains a power. The recurrence method earns its place through repeatability.

PITFALL / INDEFINITE CONSTANTS

For indefinite recurrences, one global $+C$ is enough in the final answer. Adding a new arbitrary constant at each stage obscures the structure. For definite recurrences, there is no $+C$ at all; the bounds determine a number.

4.9D Reading and using a reduction formula safely

A recurrence is a compact instruction, not a single answer. Read its direction carefully: $I_n$ is expressed in terms of a lower index, so you may use it only after deciding which base case the sequence will reach. When the gap is two, $I_8$ follows the even chain $I_8,I_6,I_4,I_2,I_0$, while $I_7$ follows the odd chain $I_7,I_5,I_3,I_1$. Never jump between these chains.

It is also important to distinguish an indefinite family from a definite one. Indefinite formulae retain an $x$-dependent product term because no bounds have been applied. In definite formulae, that term may vanish at special endpoints. Check the endpoints rather than assuming it vanishes: a different interval can leave a nonzero boundary contribution.

For a numerical result, write the multiplier sequence before simplifying. This preserves cancellations and makes it easy to spot an inverted fraction. A recurrence multiplier of $\frac{n-1}{n}$ is less than one for positive $n$, so an integral of $\sin^n x$ on $[0,\frac\pi2]$ should decrease as the exponent increases.

WORKED EXAMPLE / TWO PARITY TRACKS

Find $S_7=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^7x\,dx$. Keep reducing by two:

$$S_7=\frac67S_5=\frac67\cdot\frac45S_3=\frac67\cdot\frac45\cdot\frac23S_1.$$

Since $S_1=1$,

$$S_7=\frac{6\cdot4\cdot2}{7\cdot5\cdot3}=\frac{16}{35}.$$

The absence of $\pi$ is expected for an odd exponent. Contrast this with an even chain, which ends at $S_0=\frac\pi2$.

WORKED EXAMPLE / A NONVANISHING BOUNDARY TERM

For $\int_0^{\pi/3}\sin^2x\,dx$, do not use the $S_n$ shortcut because the upper endpoint is not $\frac\pi2$. Start from the indefinite result:

$$\int\sin^2x\,dx=\frac{x}{2}-\frac{\sin x\cos x}{2}+C.$$

Then

$$\left[\frac{x}{2}-\frac{\sin x\cos x}{2}\right]_0^{\pi/3}=\frac\pi6-\frac12\left(\frac{\sqrt3}{2}\right)\left(\frac12\right)=\frac\pi6-\frac{\sqrt3}{8}.$$

The product term did not vanish at $\frac\pi3$.

PITFALL / FALSE PATTERNS

Do not infer a recurrence from two isolated values. Its coefficient and boundary term must be derived from a valid identity and integration by parts. Once derived, it is legitimate to reuse it, but only for the same family and conditions. Changing from sine to cosine, changing limits, or changing an exponent step changes the formula that is justified.

RECOGNITION / MONOTONIC SIZE CHECK

On $[0,\frac\pi2]$, $0\le\sin x\le1$. Raising a number in this interval to a larger positive power cannot increase it, so $S_{n+2}\le S_n$. The recurrence confirms this because $S_{n+2}=\frac{n+1}{n+2}S_n$ and the multiplier is less than $1$. This does not calculate an answer, but it is a strong independent check on the direction of a fraction.

For indefinite integrals, there is no comparable order check because the integrand may change sign and the result is a function. Instead differentiate the proposed recurrence result, or test a convenient value of $x$ after differentiating.

SUMMARY / WHAT TO WRITE

For a recurrence question, present the family definition, your $u$ and $dv$ choices, the identity that returns the lower-index family, and the rearrangement that isolates the original integral. When applying it, show each reduced index and finish with the correct base case. This format communicates the reasoning as well as the final value, and it makes coefficient errors easy to locate.

Practice Try before revealing

1
State the base cases for $I_n=\int\sin^n x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

The recurrence reduces the index by two, so parity determines the base case. For even $n$, use $I_0$; for odd $n$, use $I_1$.

$$I_0=\int1\,dx=x+C,\qquad I_1=\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x+C.$$
2
Use the recurrence to find $I_2$.
Solution / worked steps

Insert $n=2$ into $I_n=-\frac{\sin^{n-1}x\cos x}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}I_{n-2}$:

$$I_2=-\frac{\sin x\cos x}{2}+\frac12I_0=-\frac12\sin x\cos x+\frac{x}{2}+C.$$

Using $\sin x\cos x=\frac12\sin2x$ gives the equivalent form $\frac{x}{2}-\frac14\sin2x+C$.

3
Use the recurrence to find $I_3$.
Solution / worked steps

Set $n=3$ and use the odd base case:

$$I_3=-\frac{\sin^2x\cos x}{3}+\frac23I_1=-\frac{\sin^2x\cos x}{3}-\frac23\cos x+C.$$

Both terms are required. The recurrence does not say to discard the boundary-style product term.

4
Find $S_2=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^2x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

For the definite family, $S_n=\frac{n-1}{n}S_{n-2}$. Thus

$$S_2=\frac12S_0=\frac12\int_0^{\pi/2}1\,dx=\frac12\cdot\frac{\pi}{2}=\frac{\pi}{4}.$$
5
Find $S_4=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Reduce in steps of two:

$$S_4=\frac34S_2=\frac34\cdot\frac{\pi}{4}=\frac{3\pi}{16}.$$

Do not use $S_1$ here: the even sequence must end at $S_0$.

6
Find $S_5=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^5x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Follow the odd sequence down to $S_1=1$:

$$S_5=\frac45S_3=\frac45\cdot\frac23S_1=\frac{8}{15}.$$

There is no $\pi$ because the odd base case is $S_1=1$, not $S_0=\frac\pi2$.

7
Derive a recurrence for $J_n=\int x^ne^x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Choose $u=x^n$ and $dv=e^x\,dx$. Then $du=nx^{n-1}\,dx$ and $v=e^x$.

$$J_n=x^ne^x-\int e^x(nx^{n-1})\,dx=x^ne^x-nJ_{n-1}.$$

This lowers the power by one, so the base case is $J_0=e^x+C$.

8
Use $J_n=x^ne^x-nJ_{n-1}$ to find $J_2$.
Solution / worked steps

First reduce to $J_1$, then to $J_0$:

$$J_2=x^2e^x-2J_1=x^2e^x-2(xe^x-J_0).$$ $$J_2=x^2e^x-2xe^x+2e^x=(x^2-2x+2)e^x+C.$$
9
Why does the boundary term vanish for $S_n$ when $n\ge2$?
Solution / worked steps

The term from the indefinite recurrence is $-\frac1n\sin^{n-1}x\cos x$. At $x=0$, $\sin^{n-1}0=0$. At $x=\frac\pi2$, $\cos\left(\frac\pi2\right)=0$.

$$\left[-\frac1n\sin^{n-1}x\cos x\right]_0^{\pi/2}=0-0=0.$$

That is why the definite recurrence contains only $S_{n-2}$.

10
State the cosine-family analogue for $C_n=\int\cos^n x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Use $u=\cos^{n-1}x$ and $dv=\cos x\,dx$. The product term has the opposite sign pattern because $d(\cos x)=-\sin x\,dx$.

$$C_n=\frac{\sin x\cos^{n-1}x}{n}+\frac{n-1}{n}C_{n-2}+C.$$

For the definite family on $[0,\frac\pi2]$, the same multiplier $\frac{n-1}{n}$ remains.
