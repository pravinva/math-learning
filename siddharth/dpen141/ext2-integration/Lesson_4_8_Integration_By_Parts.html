4.8A Formula, purpose, and a defensible choice

Integration by parts is the product rule read backwards. Starting with $(uv)'=u\,v'+v\,u'$, integrate both sides and rearrange:

$$\int u\,dv=uv-\int v\,du.$$

The notation is deliberate. You choose a factor to call $u$ and the rest, including $dx$, to call $dv$. The method succeeds when differentiating $u$ makes it simpler and integrating $dv$ is routine. It is a way to transfer difficulty, not an automatic response to every product.

RECOGNITION / LIATE WITH REASONS

A useful priority is logarithmic, inverse trig, algebraic, trig, exponential. Put $\ln x$, $\arcsin x$, or $\arctan x$ into $u$ because differentiation simplifies them. Put an exponential or sine/cosine into $dv$ because its antiderivative is immediate. Always check both halves of the decision: a simple $du$ is not enough if $v$ is difficult or unavailable.

WORKED EXAMPLE / ONE PASS

Evaluate $\int x\cos x\,dx$. Choose $u=x$ and $dv=\cos x\,dx$. Then $du=dx$ and $v=\sin x$.

$$\int x\cos x\,dx=x\sin x-\int\sin x\,dx=x\sin x+\cos x+C.$$

The minus sign comes from the formula; the plus appears only because $\int\sin x\,dx=-\cos x$.

PITFALL / PARTS VS SUBSTITUTION

A visible product does not force parts. In $\int2x\cos(x^2)\,dx$, the factor $2x$ is the derivative of the inside $x^2$, so substitution collapses the expression immediately. Conversely, $xe^x$ has no inner-function derivative pair, so parts is the productive choice.

4.8B Repeated parts and the tabular pattern

Products of a polynomial with $e^{ax}$, $\sin(ax)$, or $\cos(ax)$ often need several passes. Each differentiation lowers the polynomial degree, eventually producing zero. This is the signal that repeated parts will terminate.

A tabular layout is only bookkeeping: put successive derivatives of the polynomial in one column, successive antiderivatives of the other factor in the other column, and attach alternating signs $+,-,+,-,\ldots$. Read the diagonal products and stop as soon as the derivative column reaches zero. The table does not replace the formula; it prevents sign and coefficient slips.

WORKED EXAMPLE / POLYNOMIAL TIMES EXPONENTIAL

For $\int x^2e^x\,dx$, take $u=x^2$ and $dv=e^x\,dx$:

$$\int x^2e^x\,dx=x^2e^x-2\int xe^x\,dx.$$

Apply parts again to the remaining integral: $\int xe^x\,dx=xe^x-e^x$. Substitute this exact result rather than trying to guess the signs:

$$x^2e^x-2(xe^x-e^x)=(x^2-2x+2)e^x+C.$$
PITFALL / STOPPING TOO EARLY

After one application, $\int x^2\cos x\,dx$ still contains $\int x\sin x\,dx$. That is progress, not a final answer. Continue until the remaining integral is elementary, then collect all terms. A derivative check is especially valuable when trigonometric signs alternate.

4.8C Looping integrals and checking work

Sometimes two applications of parts recreate the original integral. Typical examples are $e^{ax}\sin(bx)$ and $e^{ax}\cos(bx)$. Do not restart the calculation: name the original integral and solve the resulting algebraic equation.

WORKED EXAMPLE / THE INTEGRAL RETURNS

Let $I=\int e^x\sin x\,dx$. With $u=e^x$ and $dv=\sin x\,dx$,

$$I=-e^x\cos x+\int e^x\cos x\,dx.$$

Apply parts to the remaining integral: $\int e^x\cos x\,dx=e^x\sin x-I$. Therefore

$$I=-e^x\cos x+e^x\sin x-I,$$ $$2I=e^x(\sin x-\cos x),\qquad I=\frac{e^x}{2}(\sin x-\cos x)+C.$$

The $2$ appears because $I$ is moved from the right side to the left; it is not a trigonometric identity.

CHECK / DIFFERENTIATE THE FINAL FORM

Differentiate $\frac12e^x(\sin x-\cos x)$. The product rule gives $\frac12e^x(\sin x-\cos x)+\frac12e^x(\cos x+\sin x)=e^x\sin x$. This check catches the most common looping error: a wrong sign in the second pass.

4.8D A practical decision process

Before writing $u$ and $dv$, spend a few seconds classifying the integrand. Is there a composite function with its derivative present? If yes, try substitution first. Is there a rational expression whose denominator may divide the numerator, split, or factor? Use those algebraic methods first. Integration by parts is most natural when the difficulty comes from a product in which one factor improves under differentiation.

For each proposed choice, predict one line ahead. If $u=x^3$ and $dv=e^x\,dx$, then $du=3x^2\,dx$ is smaller and $v=e^x$ is easy: promising. If $u=e^x$ and $dv=x^3\,dx$, then $du=e^x\,dx$ is not simpler while $v=\frac{x^4}{4}$ has increased the polynomial degree: unpromising. This prediction is more reliable than memorising a priority list mechanically.

In an exam solution, state the choice and its derivatives clearly. A marker can then follow the sign, constants, and the origin of each new integrand. Writing $u=\cdots$, $du=\cdots$, $dv=\cdots$, $v=\cdots$ is not filler; it is an error-control system.

WORKED EXAMPLE / A LOGARITHM WITH A POWER

Evaluate $\int x^3\ln x\,dx$ for $x>0$. The logarithm becomes simpler when differentiated, so choose $u=\ln x$ and $dv=x^3\,dx$. Then $du=\frac1x\,dx$ and $v=\frac{x^4}{4}$.

$$\int x^3\ln x\,dx=\frac{x^4}{4}\ln x-\int\frac{x^4}{4}\cdot\frac1x\,dx=\frac{x^4}{4}\ln x-\frac14\int x^3\,dx.$$

Finish the elementary integral:

$$\int x^3\ln x\,dx=\frac{x^4}{4}\ln x-\frac{x^4}{16}+C.$$

The residual integral is simpler because the logarithm disappeared. That is the intended transfer of difficulty.

PITFALL / CONSTANTS AND CHAIN FACTORS

Carry constants through every pass. For example, $\int x\cos(3x)\,dx$ requires $v=\frac13\sin(3x)$, and the next integral still carries $\frac13$. A missing chain factor can survive several lines and produce a plausible-looking but incorrect answer. Pull ordinary constant multipliers outside before beginning whenever that makes the structure clearer.

CHECKLIST / BEFORE YOU BOX AN ANSWER

Ask: did the chosen $u$ actually simplify? Did $v$ include any chain-rule coefficient? Is every remaining integral evaluated? Does the final answer contain one arbitrary constant only? Finally, differentiate the result when the algebra is short enough. These checks are faster than redoing a full integration after a sign error has propagated.

Practice Try before revealing

1
Find $\int xe^x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Choose $u=x$ because differentiating it removes the polynomial, and take $dv=e^x\,dx$. Thus $du=dx$ and $v=e^x$.

$$\int xe^x\,dx=xe^x-\int e^x\,dx=xe^x-e^x+C=(x-1)e^x+C.$$

A quick derivative check gives $e^x+(x-1)e^x=xe^x$.

2
Find $\int x\sin(2x)\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Let $u=x$, $dv=\sin(2x)\,dx$. The chain factor means $v=-\frac12\cos(2x)$, not simply $-\cos(2x)$.

$$\int x\sin(2x)\,dx=-\frac{x}{2}\cos(2x)+\frac12\int\cos(2x)\,dx=-\frac{x}{2}\cos(2x)+\frac14\sin(2x)+C.$$
3
Find $\int \ln x\,dx$ for $x>0$.
Solution / worked steps

Although there is only one visible factor, write $\ln x=\ln x\cdot1$. Take $u=\ln x$ and $dv=dx$, so $du=\frac1x\,dx$ and $v=x$.

$$\int\ln x\,dx=x\ln x-\int x\left(\frac1x\right)dx=x\ln x-x+C.$$
4
Find $\int x^2\cos x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Differentiate the polynomial twice. First use $u=x^2$, $dv=\cos x\,dx$.

$$\int x^2\cos x\,dx=x^2\sin x-2\int x\sin x\,dx.$$

For the remaining integral, parts gives $\int x\sin x\,dx=-x\cos x+\sin x$. Substitution back yields

$$x^2\sin x+2x\cos x-2\sin x+C.$$
5
Find $\int x\ln x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Choose $u=\ln x$ and $dv=x\,dx$. Then $du=\frac1x\,dx$ and $v=\frac{x^2}{2}$.

$$\int x\ln x\,dx=\frac{x^2}{2}\ln x-\int\frac{x^2}{2}\cdot\frac1x\,dx=\frac{x^2}{2}\ln x-\frac12\int x\,dx.$$

Therefore the answer is $\frac{x^2}{2}\ln x-\frac{x^2}{4}+C$.

6
Find $\int e^x\cos x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Name the integral $I$. Parts with $u=e^x$, $dv=\cos x\,dx$ gives $I=e^x\sin x-\int e^x\sin x\,dx$.

$$\int e^x\sin x\,dx=-e^x\cos x+I,$$

so $I=e^x\sin x+e^x\cos x-I$. Hence $2I=e^x(\sin x+\cos x)$ and

$$I=\frac{e^x}{2}(\sin x+\cos x)+C.$$
7
Find $\int \arctan x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Inverse trig functions are strong candidates for $u$. Take $u=\arctan x$, $dv=dx$, hence $du=\frac{dx}{1+x^2}$ and $v=x$.

$$\int\arctan x\,dx=x\arctan x-\int\frac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx.$$

The remaining integral is substitution with $w=1+x^2$: it is $\frac12\ln(1+x^2)$. Therefore $x\arctan x-\frac12\ln(1+x^2)+C$.

8
Choose $u$ and $dv$ for $\int e^x\ln x\,dx$, then set up the result.
Solution / worked steps

Use $u=\ln x$ rather than $e^x$: $\frac{d}{dx}\ln x=\frac1x$ is simpler, while $e^x$ is effortless to integrate. Thus $dv=e^x\,dx$, $v=e^x$.

$$\int e^x\ln x\,dx=e^x\ln x-\int\frac{e^x}{x}\,dx.$$

The remaining integral is not elementary. This is a useful warning: LIATE is a guide, but a good choice need not guarantee an elementary final answer.

9
Evaluate $\int_0^1 xe^x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

From Question 1, an antiderivative is $(x-1)e^x$. Apply the bounds to the whole expression:

$$[(x-1)e^x]_0^1=(1-1)e^1-(0-1)e^0=0-(-1)=1.$$

For a definite integral, do not add a new $C$.

10
Explain why $\int 2x\cos(x^2)\,dx$ is a substitution question rather than a parts question.
Solution / worked steps

The inside function is $x^2$ and its derivative $2x$ is already present. Let $u=x^2$, so $du=2x\,dx$.

$$\int2x\cos(x^2)\,dx=\int\cos u\,du=\sin u+C=\sin(x^2)+C.$$

Parts would manufacture a more complicated integral; recognise the reverse chain rule first.

11
Find $\displaystyle\int x^2\ln x\,dx$ for $x>0$.
Solution / worked steps

$u=\ln x$, $dv=x^2 dx$: $\dfrac{x^3}{3}\ln x-\dfrac{1}{3}\int x^2 dx=\dfrac{x^3}{3}\ln x-\dfrac{x^3}{9}+C$.

12
Find $\displaystyle\int e^{2x}\sin 3x\,dx$ (looping parts).
Solution / worked steps

After two applications: $\dfrac{e^{2x}}{13}(2\sin 3x-3\cos 3x)+C$.

13
Find $\displaystyle\int\arcsin x\,dx$ on $(-1,1)$.
Solution / worked steps

$u=\arcsin x$, $dv=dx$: $x\arcsin x-\int\dfrac{x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}dx=x\arcsin x+\sqrt{1-x^2}+C$.

14
Find $\displaystyle\int x\sec^2 x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

$u=x$, $dv=\sec^2 x dx$: $x\tan x-\int\tan x\,dx=x\tan x+\ln|\cos x|+C$.

15
Set up (but justify the choice of $u$) $\displaystyle\int\dfrac{\ln x}{x^2}\,dx$, then finish.
Solution / worked steps

$u=\ln x$, $dv=x^{-2}dx$: $-\dfrac{\ln x}{x}-\int(-\dfrac1x)\dfrac1x dx=-\dfrac{\ln x}{x}-\dfrac1x+C$.

16
Find $\displaystyle\int x^3 e^{x}\,dx$ using tabular parts (or three reductions).
Solution / worked steps

$e^{x}(x^3-3x^2+6x-6)+C$.

17
Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2} x\cos x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

$u=x$, $dv=\cos x dx$: $[x\sin x]_0^{\pi/2}-\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin x\,dx=1$.

18
Find $\displaystyle\int\ln(x^2+1)\,dx$ (parts + leftover arctan integral).
Solution / worked steps

$x\ln(x^2+1)-2x+2\tan^{-1}x+C$.

19
Which is more appropriate for $\displaystyle\int x(2x+1)^{9}\,dx$: parts or substitution? Carry out the better method.
Solution / worked steps

Substitution $u=2x+1$ (or expand) beats parts: $x=\dfrac{u-1}{2}$ gives a polynomial in $u$.

20
Explain how DETAIL/LIATE would choose $u$ in $\displaystyle\int x\arctan x\,dx$, then find the integral.
Solution / worked steps

$u=\arctan x$ (inverse trig before algebraic). Result: $\dfrac{x^2+1}{2}\arctan x-\dfrac{x}{2}+C$.
