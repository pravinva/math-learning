4.10A FTC, signed area, and disciplined evaluation

If $F'(x)=f(x)$, the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus states $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=F(b)-F(a)=[F(x)]_a^b$. The notation means evaluate the complete antiderivative at the upper limit, then subtract the complete value at the lower limit. Parentheses are not optional when $F$ contains more than one term.

A definite integral is a signed accumulation. A region above the $x$-axis contributes positively; a region below contributes negatively. Therefore an answer of zero can describe cancellation, not the absence of area.

AREA VS INTEGRAL

For geometric area between a curve and the axis, split at every zero where the sign changes and integrate $|f(x)|$. You may not simply take the absolute value of a final signed integral: cancellation can hide substantial area. Establish the sign on each subinterval first.

WORKED EXAMPLE / COMPLETE BRACKETS

Evaluate $\int_1^3(2x-5)\,dx$. An antiderivative is $x^2-5x$:

$$[x^2-5x]_1^3=(9-15)-(1-5)=-6-(-4)=-2.$$

The negative value agrees with the graph: the line is below the axis for more of this interval than it is above.

4.10B Symmetry, parity, and reflection

On symmetric bounds $[-a,a]$, test the formula rather than relying on a sketch. If $f(-x)=-f(x)$, $f$ is odd and $\int_{-a}^a f(x)\,dx=0$. If $f(-x)=f(x)$, $f$ is even and $\int_{-a}^a f(x)\,dx=2\int_0^a f(x)\,dx$. A sum of an even and an odd function is generally neither, but you can integrate its components separately.

Reflection on $[0,a]$ says $\int_0^a f(x)\,dx=\int_0^a f(a-x)\,dx$. Add these equal forms to obtain $2I=\int_0^a[f(x)+f(a-x)]\,dx$. This is useful when the two terms combine to a constant or a simpler expression.

WORKED EXAMPLE / PAIRED REFLECTION

Let $I=\int_0^1\frac{dx}{1+x}$. Reflection gives $I=\int_0^1\frac{dx}{2-x}$. Adding them does not immediately make a constant, so direct integration is preferable. This is an important recognition point: reflection is a tool, not a compulsory step.

In contrast, when $f(x)+f(a-x)$ simplifies, averaging can avoid a difficult antiderivative.

PITFALL / REVERSED BOUNDS

With $u=a-x$, $du=-dx$. Either retain the reversed bounds and the minus sign, or reverse the bounds and remove it; doing both twice creates an incorrect sign. Write one intermediate line before simplifying.

4.10C Absolute values and improper endpoints

Absolute value changes the integrand by sign, so locate every root of the expression inside $|\cdot|$ before applying the FTC. On each interval, replace $|g(x)|$ by $g(x)$ where $g\ge0$ and by $-g(x)$ where $g<0$.

An improper integral is a limit, not an integral with infinity substituted into a formula. For example, $\int_1^\infty f(x)\,dx$ means $\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_1^b f(x)\,dx$. The limit must exist as a finite number for the integral to converge.

PITFALL / ENDPOINT SINGULARITIES

If the integrand is undefined at an endpoint or inside the interval, split the interval and introduce a separate one-sided limit for each problematic point. A single antiderivative does not remove the need to check convergence.

4.10D A reliable definite-integral workflow

Begin by reading the question’s target. “Evaluate” usually asks for signed integral; “find the area” asks for a nonnegative geometric quantity. Next inspect the interval and integrand before integrating. Mark zeros, undefined points, symmetry about the origin, and any obvious substitution or reflection. This inspection often determines whether you need to split the interval or can shorten the calculation.

After choosing an antiderivative, use bracket notation all the way through substitution: $[F(x)]_a^b=F(b)-F(a)$. Do not distribute the lower-bound minus sign mentally across a long expression. For trigonometric values, substitute exact values before converting anything to decimals. Exact forms retain cancellation and make later algebra auditable.

A final reasonableness check should compare sign and size with the graph or the integrand’s behaviour. If a function is nonnegative and at most $3$ on an interval of length $2$, its integral must lie between $0$ and $6$. Such bounds are simple but reveal many incorrect signs and misplaced factors.

WORKED EXAMPLE / SIGNED INTEGRAL VERSUS AREA

Consider $f(x)=x^2-x$ on $[0,2]$. The roots are $0$ and $1$, so $f$ is negative on $(0,1)$ and positive on $(1,2)$. Its signed integral is

$$\int_0^2(x^2-x)\,dx=\left[\frac{x^3}{3}-\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^2=\frac83-2=\frac23.$$

For area, split and reverse the first sign:

$$\int_0^1(x-x^2)\,dx+\int_1^2(x^2-x)\,dx=\frac16+\frac56=1.$$

The two answers differ because the integral includes cancellation while area does not.

WORKED EXAMPLE / EVEN AND ODD COMPONENTS

Evaluate $\int_{-1}^1(x^4+3x^3+2)\,dx$. The term $3x^3$ is odd, so its contribution is zero. The remaining terms are even:

$$\int_{-1}^1(x^4+3x^3+2)\,dx=2\int_0^1(x^4+2)\,dx.$$ $$2\left[\frac{x^5}{5}+2x\right]_0^1=2\left(\frac15+2\right)=\frac{22}{5}.$$

Splitting a mixed expression into parity components is often clearer than expanding a full antiderivative over symmetric limits.

PITFALL / INFINITY IS NOT A NUMBER

Expressions such as $[-1/x]_1^\infty$ are informal shorthand only. The rigorous calculation has a finite variable endpoint first, followed by a limit. This distinction matters because some improper integrals diverge even though an antiderivative exists, for example $\int_1^\infty\frac{1}{x}\,dx$.

RECOGNITION / SPLIT BEFORE FTC

Two situations require a split: an absolute value whose inside changes sign, and an improper point where the integrand is undefined. The split points are part of the mathematics, not merely a presentation choice. For instance, $\int_{-1}^1\frac{1}{x}\,dx$ is not zero by odd symmetry because the integral is improper at $x=0$ and the one-sided integrals diverge. Symmetry may be used only after the integral is known to be properly defined or convergent.

When a problem has several features, resolve the domain first, then use symmetry or an antiderivative on the valid pieces. This order prevents a visually appealing shortcut from masking a non-existent integral.

Practice Try before revealing

1
Evaluate $\int_0^2(3x^2-4x)\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

An antiderivative is $x^3-2x^2$. Keep the brackets intact while substituting:

$$[x^3-2x^2]_0^2=(8-8)-(0-0)=0.$$

The answer is zero because signed regions cancel; it does not say that there is no geometric area.

2
Evaluate $\int_{-2}^2x^3\,dx$ using symmetry.
Solution / worked steps

Test parity: $f(-x)=(-x)^3=-x^3=-f(x)$, so $x^3$ is odd. On symmetric limits, opposite signed areas cancel.

$$\int_{-2}^{2}x^3\,dx=0.$$

This is faster than finding $\frac{x^4}{4}$, though that method confirms the same result.

3
Evaluate $\int_{-2}^2(x^2+1)\,dx$ using symmetry.
Solution / worked steps

Here $f(-x)=(-x)^2+1=f(x)$, so the function is even. Double the half-interval integral:

$$2\int_0^2(x^2+1)\,dx=2\left[\frac{x^3}{3}+x\right]_0^2=2\left(\frac83+2\right)=\frac{28}{3}.$$
4
Find the geometric area between $y=x-1$ and the $x$-axis on $[0,2]$.
Solution / worked steps

The line crosses the axis at $x=1$. It is negative before that point and positive after it, so split and reverse the negative piece:

$$\int_0^1(1-x)\,dx+\int_1^2(x-1)\,dx.$$ $$\left[x-\frac{x^2}{2}\right]_0^1+\left[\frac{x^2}{2}-x\right]_1^2=\frac12+\frac12=1.$$
5
Show that $\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+x}\,dx=\int_0^1\frac{1-x}{2-x}\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Use the reflection substitution $u=1-x$. Then $dx=-du$, and the bounds change from $x=0,1$ to $u=1,0$:

$$\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+x}\,dx=\int_1^0\frac{1-u}{2-u}(-du)=\int_0^1\frac{1-u}{2-u}\,du.$$

Rename $u$ as $x$; it is a dummy variable.

6
Evaluate $\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+x}\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Split the rational expression: $\frac{x}{1+x}=1-\frac1{1+x}$.

$$\int_0^1\frac{x}{1+x}\,dx=\left[x-\ln(1+x)\right]_0^1=(1-\ln2)-(0-\ln1).$$

Since $\ln1=0$, the value is $1-\ln2$.

7
Evaluate $\int_0^\pi\sin x\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

An antiderivative is $-\cos x$. Evaluate upper bound minus lower bound:

$$[-\cos x]_0^\pi=-\cos\pi-(-\cos0)=1-(-1)=2.$$

Writing the substituted signs explicitly avoids the common error $-1-1$.

8
Evaluate $\int_0^1|2x-1|\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

The expression inside the absolute value changes sign at $2x-1=0$, or $x=\frac12$. Thus

$$|2x-1|=\begin{cases}1-2x,&0\le x<\frac12,\\2x-1,&\frac12\le x\le1.\end{cases}$$ $$\int_0^{1/2}(1-2x)\,dx+\int_{1/2}^1(2x-1)\,dx=\frac14+\frac14=\frac12.$$
9
Evaluate $\lim_{b\to\infty}\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx$.
Solution / worked steps

Do not insert infinity as an ordinary bound. First integrate to a finite $b$:

$$\int_1^b x^{-2}\,dx=\left[-\frac1x\right]_1^b=-\frac1b+1.$$

Now take the limit: $\lim_{b\to\infty}\left(1-\frac1b\right)=1$. The improper integral converges to $1$.

10
Use reflection to evaluate $\int_0^a\frac{dx}{x+(a-x)+1}$ for $a>0$.
Solution / worked steps

Before applying a technique, simplify the denominator: $x+(a-x)+1=a+1$. The integrand is constant and also unchanged by $x\mapsto a-x$.

$$\int_0^a\frac{dx}{a+1}=\left[\frac{x}{a+1}\right]_0^a=\frac{a}{a+1}.$$

Reflection is consistent here, but simplification is the shortest path.
