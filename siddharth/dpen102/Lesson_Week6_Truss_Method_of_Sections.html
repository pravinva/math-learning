Start here The big picture — what this week is about

A truss is a pin-jointed assembly of two-force members. Method of sections cuts the truss so three unknown member forces can be solved from $\Sigma F_x$, $\Sigma F_y$, $\Sigma M$.

Zero-force members declutter the model; global reactions come first; then the cut algorithm classifies tension vs compression.

Why this matters

Bridge and roof frames are sized member-by-member. Sections beat joints when you only need a few internal forces.

Formula summary · Week 6
FormulaMeaning / use
\(\Sigma F_x=0\)Horizontal equilibrium of the whole truss or of a cut portion.
\(\Sigma F_y=0\)Vertical equilibrium. Reactions plus member vertical components balance the applied loads.
\(\Sigma M=0\)Moment equilibrium about any point; use \(M=F\times d_\perp\).
\(\Sigma M_{O}=0\)Take moments about the point \(O\) where two unknown cut members cross, both drop out, isolating the third member in one step.
\(F_x=F\cos\theta,\ F_y=F\sin\theta\)Resolve a member's axial force \(F\) into horizontal and vertical components along its angle \(\theta\).
\(\cos\theta=\dfrac{\text{run}}{L},\ \sin\theta=\dfrac{\text{rise}}{L}\)Direction cosines from geometry, with member length \(L=\sqrt{\text{run}^2+\text{rise}^2}\). (A 3-4-5 bar gives \(\tfrac35,\tfrac45\).)
\(F>0\Rightarrow\) tensionUnder the standard "assume tension" convention, a positive answer means the member pulls (tension).
\(F<0\Rightarrow\) compressionA negative answer means the member pushes (compression); keep the magnitude, reverse the arrow.
\(\le 3\) unknowns per cutA planar section gives only three equations (\(\Sigma F_x,\Sigma F_y,\Sigma M\)), so cut through at most three unknown members.

Symbols: \(F\) = axial force in a member (N); \(F_x,F_y\) = its horizontal/vertical components; \(\theta\) = member angle to horizontal; \(L\) = member length; \(d_\perp\) = perpendicular distance from a force to the moment point; \(R_A,R_B\) = support reactions; \(\Sigma\) = capital sigma, "sum of"; \(O\) = chosen moment centre.

Sign shortcut: always draw member arrows pulling away from the cut (tension). Then the algebra decides the sign for you, no re-guessing needed.

Intro What a truss is & why we study it

Look inside almost any large bridge, an electricity pylon, a stadium roof, a construction crane, or the rafters in a house, and you'll see the same thing: straight bars joined into a lattice of triangles. That structure is a truss. Instead of using one enormous, heavy beam to span a gap, engineers build a web of slender bars that together carry the same load using a fraction of the material. Trusses are how we cross wide distances cheaply and lightly.

The magic ingredient is the triangle. Push on the corner of a four-sided frame and it collapses into a parallelogram, it has no inherent stiffness. A triangle can't do that: its shape is fixed by the length of its three sides, so it holds firm. Build up a chain of triangles and you get a rigid structure that turns any load into simple pushes and pulls along its bars, rather than bending. That is why every bar in an ideal truss carries only axial force, straight tension (being stretched) or compression (being squashed), and never bending.

So the whole engineering question for a truss is delightfully focused: for each bar, is it being pulled or pushed, and how hard? Answer that and you can pick a bar thick enough not to snap (if in tension) or buckle (if in compression). Finding those bar forces is exactly what this lesson is about.

A simple truss: straight bars pinned at joints into triangles. Top chord tends to compress, bottom chord to stretch, web members brace between them.

Why should I care?

The top and bottom bars of a truss (the chords) act like the top and bottom of a beam: one squashes, the other stretches, and together they resist bending across the whole span, but with mostly empty space in between, saving weight and cost. Get one bar's force wrong and you either waste steel or risk a collapse. Because a bar in compression can buckle long before it would ever crush, knowing which bars are in compression (not just how big the force is) genuinely decides whether a structure stands up. Method of sections is the fast, targeted way to get that answer for any single bar you care about.

The approach, in one breath

Trusses have two standard analysis routes. Method of joints marches from joint to joint solving \(\Sigma F_x=0,\Sigma F_y=0\) at each, great when you want every member, but tedious if you only need one bar buried in the middle. Method of sections (this week) is the shortcut: imagine slicing the truss into two pieces with a single cut that passes through the bar you want. Each cut bar becomes an external force on the free body, and because the whole piece must still be in equilibrium, three equations (\(\Sigma F_x,\Sigma F_y,\Sigma M\)) solve directly for up to three unknown bars, often getting your target in a single line by choosing the moment centre cleverly. The sections below build this up: the modelling assumptions, spotting zero-force members, finding reactions, the step-by-step cutting algorithm, and finally reading tension vs compression from the sign.

A truss is analysed under three assumptions: loads act only at joints, members are two-force members carrying purely axial force (no bending), and joints behave as ideal frictionless pins. Under these assumptions, method of joints (solving each joint's \(\Sigma F_x=0,\Sigma F_y=0\) one at a time) works, but is slow if you only need one member deep inside the truss. Method of sections cuts straight through up to three unknown members at once and solves for them directly, using the same equilibrium tools, applied to a whole cut portion of the truss instead of one joint.

One straight cut exposes at most three unknown bar forces (\(F_1,F_2,F_3\)). Keep either side as a free body; three equilibrium equations solve all three directly.

Mental model. Some members carry zero force under a given load case — remove them to simplify FBDs.

Method. Apply the two classic joint rules (two collinear members; three members with two collinear and no external load).

Watch for. Zero-force under one load case may not be zero under another.

Some members carry no force at all under a given loading, and can be spotted by inspection, no calculation needed, which trims the analysis before it even starts.

Two rules

Rule 1: if only two non-collinear members meet at an unloaded joint, both are zero-force members.

Rule 2: if three members meet at an unloaded joint and two of them are collinear (in a straight line), the third (non-collinear) member is a zero-force member, and the two collinear members carry equal force.

Worked example

At joint A, members AB and AC meet at 90°, with no external load applied at A and nothing else connected there. State the force in each.

Rule 1 applies directly: two non-collinear members, unloaded joint. Both AB and AC are zero-force members.

Practice 1

At joint D, three members meet: DE and DF are collinear, DG is at an angle to them, and there is no load at D. Which member is zero-force?

Solution

DG, the non-collinear third member (Rule 2).

Practice 2

At joint H, only two members meet, HI and HJ, not collinear, and there's no external load at H. State the force in each.

Solution

Both are zero-force members (Rule 1).

Practice 3

At joint K, KL and KM are collinear, KN is at an angle, but there IS an external load applied at K. Does Rule 2 still apply? Explain.

Solution

No. Rule 2 only applies at unloaded joints. With an external load present, KN generally must carry a nonzero force to help balance it.

Practice 4

A truss has 8 members. By inspection you identify 2 zero-force members. How many members must actually be solved via joints/sections?

Solution

6. The 2 zero-force members are already known without calculation, leaving only the remaining 6 to analyse.

Practice 5

Zero-force members carry no force under this specific load case, so why do engineers still include them in the truss at all?

Solution

They provide bracing and stability, preventing buckling or shifting under other loading conditions (wind, construction loads, unbalanced live loads) not present in this particular idealised case. Zero-force here does not mean zero-force always.

Mental model. Global support reactions are external loads for every later cut.

Method. FBD of the whole truss, $\Sigma M=0$ and $\Sigma F=0$ for reactions, then cut.

Watch for. Missing a support reaction shows up as inconsistent section equations.

Before any cut can be made, the truss's overall support reactions must be known, exactly the same beam-style reaction-finding from Weeks 4–5, just applied to the truss as a whole rigid assembly.

Worked example

A truss spans 8 m, pin at A (left), roller at B (right), with a single 500 N downward load at a joint 3 m from A. Find the reactions.

\(\Sigma M_A\): \(R_B(8)=500(3)=1500 \Rightarrow R_B=187.5\text{ N}\).

\(\Sigma F_y\): \(R_A=500-187.5=312.5\text{ N}\).

Practice 6

Truss span 10 m, pin-roller, 800 N load at 4 m from A. Find the reactions.

Solution

\(R_B=800(4)/10=320\text{ N}\). \(R_A=480\text{ N}\).

Practice 7

Truss span 12 m, two loads: 400 N at 3 m, 600 N at 8 m from A. Find the reactions.

Solution

\(\Sigma M_A\): \(R_B(12)=400(3)+600(8)=6000 \Rightarrow R_B=500\text{ N}\). \(R_A=1000-500=500\text{ N}\).

Practice 8

Truss span 6 m, single 300 N load exactly at midspan. Find the reactions.

Solution

Symmetric: \(R_A=R_B=150\text{ N}\).

Practice 9

Explain why the global support reactions must be found before cutting a truss with the method of sections.

Solution

The cut portion's equilibrium equations include any support reaction acting on it. If those reactions are still unknown, the cut piece has too many unknowns (member forces plus unknown reactions) to solve with only three equilibrium equations.

3 The method of sections algorithm

Method of Section Problem 1

Manas Patnaik · ~12:00

Worked numerical section.

Open on YouTube ↗

Mental model. Cut ≤3 members, keep one side, write three equilibrium equations.

Method. Choose a moment centre that eliminates two unknowns when possible, then solve the rest.

Watch for. Assume tension (away from joint/cut); negative answer means compression.

Cut through no more than three unknown members, keep one side, and use moments cleverly to isolate one unknown at a time.

The 5-step algorithm

1. Find global support reactions. 2. Cut through at most 3 unknown members of interest. 3. Take moments about the point where two of the unknown members' lines of action intersect, this eliminates both from that equation, isolating the third. 4. Use \(\Sigma F_x,\Sigma F_y\) for the remaining unknowns. 5. Classify each result: positive (under a tension assumption) means tension, negative means compression.

Worked example

A truss: span 16 m, height 3 m, panel width 4 m (giving 3-4-5 diagonals). Bottom joints A(0,0), C(4,0), E(8,0), G(12,0), I(16,0); top joints B(4,3), D(8,3), F(12,3); pin at A, roller at I. A single 800 N load acts at D. By symmetry, \(R_A=R_I=400\text{ N}\).

Cut through panel 2 (top chord BD, diagonal EB, bottom chord CE). Keep the left portion (joints A, C, B, with \(R_A=400\text{ N}\), nothing else since the load at D sits to the right of this cut).

\(\Sigma M_E=0\) (both \(F_{CE}\) and \(F_{EB}\) pass through E, eliminating them): \(R_A(8)=F_{BD}(3) \Rightarrow F_{BD}=400(8)/3=1066.67\text{ N}\) (compression, typical for a top chord).

\(\Sigma M_B=0\) (both \(F_{BD}\) and \(F_{EB}\) pass through B): \(R_A(4)=F_{CE}(3) \Rightarrow F_{CE}=400(4)/3=533.33\text{ N}\) (tension, typical for a bottom chord).

\(\Sigma F_y=0\): \(R_A=F_{EB}\sin\theta\), with \(\sin\theta=3/5\) for this 3-4-5 diagonal: \(F_{EB}=400(5)/3=666.67\text{ N}\) (tension).

Practice 10

Using the same truss and loading as the worked example, cut through panel 3 (members: top chord DF, diagonal GD, bottom chord EG). By left–right symmetry with the worked example, state \(F_{DF}\).

Solution

By symmetry (load at midspan, symmetric supports): \(F_{DF}=F_{BD}=1066.67\text{ N}\) (compression).

Practice 11

For the same panel 3 cut, state \(F_{EG}\) (bottom chord) and \(F_{GD}\) (diagonal) by symmetry.

Solution

\(F_{EG}=F_{CE}=533.33\text{ N}\) (tension). \(F_{GD}=F_{EB}=666.67\text{ N}\) (tension).

Practice 12

Now the load is moved to joint F(12,3) instead of D, with magnitude 600 N. Find the new reactions \(R_A,R_I\).

Solution

\(\Sigma M_A\): \(R_I(16)=600(12)=7200 \Rightarrow R_I=450\text{ N}\). \(R_A=600-450=150\text{ N}\).

Practice 13

Using the load-at-F scenario from Practice 12, cut through panel 2 (top BD, diagonal EB, bottom CE) using the left portion (\(R_A=150\text{ N}\), no load between A and this cut). Find \(F_{BD}\), \(F_{CE}\), and \(F_{EB}\).

Solution

\(\Sigma M_E\): \(F_{BD}=150(8)/3=400\text{ N}\) (compression).

\(\Sigma M_B\): \(F_{CE}=150(4)/3=200\text{ N}\) (tension).

\(\Sigma F_y\): \(F_{EB}=150(5)/3=250\text{ N}\) (tension).

Practice 14

In the worked example, \(F_{BD}=1066.67\text{ N}\) but \(F_{CE}=533.33\text{ N}\), exactly half. Explain why, referencing the moment arms used in each equation.

Solution

\(F_{BD}\) came from \(R_A(8)=F_{BD}(3)\), using moment centre E, 8 m from A. \(F_{CE}\) came from \(R_A(4)=F_{CE}(3)\), using moment centre B, only 4 m from A. The numerator halves (8 vs 4) because E and B sit at different horizontal distances from the support, a direct geometric consequence of where each moment centre is located, not an error.

Mental model. Tension pulls on the cut face; compression pushes. Report both magnitude and type.

Method. Stick to one assumption convention and interpret the sign at the end.

Watch for. Do not flip signs mid-solution — change interpretation only in the final sentence.

Always assume tension as the default direction when setting up the equations. The sign of the result then tells you the physical answer directly.

Key idea

Positive result (under a tension assumption) → member is in tension. Negative result → the assumed direction was wrong, the member is actually in compression, with that magnitude.

Worked example

Solving a cut, an engineer assumes tension for a bottom-chord member and gets a result of \(-450\text{ N}\). What does this mean?

A negative result under a tension assumption means the member is actually in compression, with magnitude 450 N.

Practice 15

A diagonal member's force comes out as \(+320\text{ N}\) under a tension assumption. State its actual condition and magnitude.

Solution

Tension, 320 N (the positive sign confirms the assumed direction).

Practice 16

A top chord in a simply-supported, gravity-loaded truss is calculated to have a force of \(-890\text{ N}\) assuming tension. Is this consistent with the general expectation for top chords?

Solution

Yes. Top chords in simply-supported, gravity-loaded trusses are typically in compression, and the negative sign confirms exactly that (890 N compression).

Practice 17

A bottom chord's calculation gives \(+610\text{ N}\) under a tension assumption. Is this the expected sign for a bottom chord? Explain.

Solution

Yes. Bottom chords are typically in tension in this type of truss, and the positive sign confirms it.

Practice 18

Why is it good practice to always assume tension as the default direction when setting up method-of-sections equations, before you know the actual answer?

Solution

A consistent default convention means the sign of the final result directly tells you the physical state (positive = tension, negative = compression), without re-deriving the assumed direction case by case for every single member. It standardises the bookkeeping and avoids sign confusion across a larger truss with many members.

Practice 19

A truss span is 12 m, pin at A and roller at I. Joint loads are 300 N at \(x=3\), 450 N at \(x=6\), and 250 N at \(x=10\). Find \(R_A,R_I\).

Solution

\(\Sigma M_A: R_I(12)=300(3)+450(6)+250(10)=6100\Rightarrow R_I=508.33\text{ N}\).

\(\Sigma F_y=0\Rightarrow R_A=300+450+250-508.33=491.67\text{ N}\).

Practice 20

For Practice 19, if your section cut is between \(x=6\) and \(x=10\), why is solving reactions first essential to avoid an underdetermined cut FBD?

Solution

The left free body would include \(R_A\), 300 N, and 450 N plus up to three unknown cut-member forces. Without known \(R_A\), that is four unknowns minimum, but only three equilibrium equations exist.

Once reactions are known, the same cut has at most three unknown member forces and can be solved directly with \(\Sigma M,\Sigma F_x,\Sigma F_y\).

Practice 21

A 3-4-5 diagonal member carries \(F=750\text{ N}\) in tension. Resolve its force into global components (\(x\) horizontal, \(y\) vertical).

Solution

For 3-4-5 geometry, \(\cos\theta=4/5,\ \sin\theta=3/5\).

\(F_x=750(4/5)=600\text{ N}\). \(F_y=750(3/5)=450\text{ N}\).

Sign depends on assumed direction along the member; magnitudes are 600 N horizontal and 450 N vertical.

Practice 22

In a section equation you obtain \(F_{DG}=-420\text{ N}\) under a tension assumption along DG. State physical meaning and give vector-direction correction.

Solution

Negative under tension assumption means member DG is in compression of magnitude 420 N.

For the FBD, reverse the originally assumed arrow direction along DG; keep magnitude 420 N.

Practice 23

Cut passes through members AB, BC, and AC. You choose moments about joint C. Explain which unknowns are eliminated and why this often isolates one force immediately.

Solution

Any cut-member force whose line of action passes through C has zero moment arm about C, so it vanishes from \(\Sigma M_C\).

Here, BC and AC both pass through C, leaving only AB in the moment equation; that gives AB directly in one step.

Practice 24

For a section through panel 2 of a 16 m truss, \(R_A=420\text{ N}\), panel height 3 m, and moment centre at \(E\) located 8 m from A. Solve \(F_{BD}\) using \(\Sigma M_E=0\).

Solution

Taking moments about \(E\): \(R_A(8)=F_{BD}(3)\).

\(F_{BD}=420(8)/3=1120\text{ N}\).

For gravity-loaded trusses this top chord member is typically compression, so classify as \(1120\text{ N}\) compression.

Practice 25

In the same setup as Practice 24, using \(\Sigma M_B=0\) with a 4 m horizontal lever arm from A to C-panel cut, compute \(F_{CE}\) if \(R_A=420\text{ N}\).

Solution

\(\Sigma M_B: R_A(4)=F_{CE}(3)\).

\(F_{CE}=420(4)/3=560\text{ N}\).

Bottom chords in this loading are generally tension, so \(F_{CE}=560\text{ N}\) tension.

Practice 26

A cut through diagonal EB gives vertical equilibrium equation \(R_A-F_{EB}\sin\theta=0\), with \(R_A=420\text{ N}\), \(\sin\theta=3/5\). Solve \(F_{EB}\) and classify.

Solution

\(420-F_{EB}(3/5)=0\Rightarrow F_{EB}=420(5/3)=700\text{ N}\).

Positive under tension assumption means member EB is in tension of 700 N.

Practice 27

Joint X has members XY and XZ collinear, and member XW non-collinear. A 120 N downward load acts at X. Can XW be taken as zero-force by inspection? Justify with equilibrium logic.

Solution

No. The unloaded-joint condition is violated because external 120 N acts at X.

That vertical load generally requires a vertical component from one or more members, often forcing XW to carry nonzero force.

Practice 28

A student cuts through 4 unknown members in one section and tries to solve all from one side. Identify the mathematical issue and the correct remedy.

Solution

Planar statics provides only three independent equations, so 4 unknown cut forces produce an underdetermined system.

Remedy: choose a different section cutting at most 3 unknowns, or use additional analysis (e.g., another section/joint equations) after solving some members first.

Practice 29

Truss span 15 m, loads: 500 N at 5 m and 700 N at 11 m from A. Find reactions and comment which support carries more and why.

Solution

\(\Sigma M_A: R_B(15)=500(5)+700(11)=10200\Rightarrow R_B=680\text{ N}\).

\(R_A=1200-680=520\text{ N}\).

Right support carries more because the heavier 700 N load is closer to B, increasing its moment demand about A.

Practice 30

In a symmetric truss with symmetric loading about midspan, explain which member-force equalities can be used to reduce computation after solving one side.

Solution

Mirror members about the symmetry axis have equal force magnitude and same tension/compression type (e.g., left top chord panel force equals right counterpart).

So solving one representative panel by sections often gives the mirrored panel forces directly, cutting workload roughly in half.

Practice 31

You assume tension for three cut members and obtain \((+510,-260,+180)\text{ N}\). Provide full classifications and explain what sign consistency tells you about setup quality.

Solution

\(+510\): tension 510 N. \(-260\): compression 260 N. \(+180\): tension 180 N.

Mixed signs are normal; they do not imply error by themselves. Consistency means each value is interpreted against the same initial tension convention.

Practice 32

For a section, forces in members m1 and m2 intersect at point P. Member m3 has perpendicular moment arm 2.8 m about P. External moments about P from known loads/reactions sum to \(1960\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\) (counterclockwise). Solve \(F_{m3}\).

Solution

With m1 and m2 eliminated at P: \(F_{m3}(2.8)=1960\).

\(F_{m3}=1960/2.8=700\text{ N}\).

Sign depends on the assumed arrow for \(F_{m3}\); magnitude is 700 N.

Practice 33

A diagonal member has rise 2.4 m and run 3.2 m. If its solved axial force is 500 N compression, find horizontal and vertical components transmitted at a joint.

Solution

Length \(=\sqrt{3.2^2+2.4^2}=4.0\), so direction cosines are \(3.2/4=0.8\) and \(2.4/4=0.6\).

Component magnitudes: \(F_x=500(0.8)=400\text{ N}\), \(F_y=500(0.6)=300\text{ N}\).

Compression means the joint is pushed along the member toward the opposite joint; use that sense when placing signs.

Practice 34

A section equation gives \(F_{top}=980\text{ N}\) compression and \(F_{bottom}=980\text{ N}\) tension. Provide a structural interpretation in terms of chord action under sagging global behavior.

Solution

This is the classic truss-beam bending pattern: top chord shortens (compression) and bottom chord elongates (tension).

Near-equal magnitudes indicate these two chords form the principal force couple resisting bending in that panel.

Practice 35

Why does choosing the "lighter" side of the cut (fewer external loads) often reduce arithmetic errors, even though either side gives the same exact answer?

Solution

Fewer forces mean fewer terms in each equilibrium equation, so there are fewer sign and moment-arm opportunities for mistakes.

Both sides are valid by statics equivalence, but the simpler FBD improves reliability under exam time pressure.

Practice 36

Exam-check workflow: list the minimum sequence to solve one interior member by method of sections and include one numerical self-check that should be true at the end.

Solution

Sequence: (1) global reactions, (2) choose cut through \(\le3\) unknowns including target member, (3) isolate easier side, (4) use moments about intersection point of two unknowns to isolate third, (5) use \(\Sigma F_x,\Sigma F_y\) for remaining unknowns, (6) classify signs.

Self-check: substitute solved member forces back into all three equilibrium equations for the cut FBD; each should close to zero within rounding tolerance.
