Start here The big picture — what this week is about

The centroid is the geometric 'balance point' of an area. Composite shapes use addition (and subtraction for holes) of $A\bar{x}$ moments.

Second moment of area $I$ measures how area is spread from an axis — the key section property for bending stress. Parallel-axis theorem builds composite $I$.

Why this matters

Beam bending stress $\sigma=My/I$ is only as good as your $I$. Wrong centroid ⇒ wrong $I$ ⇒ wrong design.

The roadmap — how the pieces connect

Composite centroids → holes/subtraction → $I$ and parallel-axis → composite $I$.

Intro What centroids & \(I\) are & why they matter

Every flat shape has a geometric balance point, the place where you could balance that shape on a pin if it were a thin plate of uniform thickness. That point is the centroid, written \((\bar x,\bar y)\). For a rectangle or circle it sits dead in the middle; for an L-shape or a plate with a hole cut out, it drifts toward the side that still has more material. Finding the centroid is the first job whenever you need to know where a body's weight acts, or where to place the neutral axis of a beam.

The second big idea this week is the second moment of area \(I\) (also called the area moment of inertia). It measures how far a cross-section's material is spread from a chosen axis. A tall, thin rectangle bends much less than a short, fat one of the same area, because more of its material sits far from the bending axis, and \(I\) captures exactly that. Beam deflection and bending stress both depend on \(I\): larger \(I\) means a stiffer, stronger beam for the same material.

Left: an L-shape's centroid is pulled toward the heavier base. Right: same area, different \(I\), tall sections resist bending far better.

Why should I care?

Every steel I-beam, timber joist, and concrete lintel is sized using \(I\). Put the same amount of steel in a solid square and it will sag; stretch it into an I-shape (flanges far from the centre) and \(I\) skyrockets because of the \(Ad^2\) term, giving you stiffness "for free." Centroids tell you where the neutral axis sits and where the weight of an odd-shaped plate acts. Miss either and your beam design or your tipping calculation is wrong from the start.

The approach, in one breath

Section 0 builds composite centroids by adding simple pieces. Section 1 handles holes by treating them as negative area. Section 2 introduces \(I\) for standard shapes and the parallel-axis theorem that lets you shift axes. Section 3 puts it all together: find the composite centroid first, then transfer each piece's \(I\) onto that shared axis and sum. Every composite problem is the same two-step dance: locate the centroid, then assemble \(I\).

Mental model. $\bar{x}=\sum(A_i x_i)/\sum A_i$ (and likewise $\bar{y}$) — area-weighted average of piece centroids.

Method. Split into rectangles/triangles/circles, tabulate $A$, $x$, $y$, then compute $\bar{x}$, $\bar{y}$.

Watch for. Centroid coordinates are measured from a single consistent origin.

The centroid of a composite shape is an area-weighted average of each piece's own centroid, larger pieces pull the average toward themselves, exactly like a weighted mean. Break the shape into rectangles (or triangles, circles), look up each piece's own centroid, then plug into the formulas below.

Composite L-section: each piece has its own centroid; the combined centroid is the area-weighted average of the two.

Key idea

$$\bar x = \frac{\sum A_ix_i}{\sum A_i}, \qquad \bar y = \frac{\sum A_iy_i}{\sum A_i}.$$

Worked example 1

Two rectangles: \(R_1\) area 8, centroid \((2,1)\); \(R_2\) area 6, centroid \((5,1.5)\). Find the composite centroid.

\(\bar x = \dfrac{8(2)+6(5)}{14}=\dfrac{46}{14}=3.286\).

\(\bar y = \dfrac{8(1)+6(1.5)}{14}=\dfrac{17}{14}=1.214\).

Worked example 2

An L-shaped section: base rectangle 6×2 (area 12, centroid \((3,1)\)), upright rectangle 2×4 sitting on top of its right end (area 8, centroid \((5,4)\)). Find the composite centroid.

\(\bar x = \dfrac{12(3)+8(5)}{20}=\dfrac{76}{20}=3.8\).

\(\bar y = \dfrac{12(1)+8(4)}{20}=\dfrac{44}{20}=2.2\).

Practice 1

Two rectangles: \(A_1=10\) at \((1,2)\), \(A_2=15\) at \((4,3)\). Find the composite centroid.

Solution

\(\bar x=\dfrac{10(1)+15(4)}{25}=\dfrac{70}{25}=2.8\). \(\bar y=\dfrac{10(2)+15(3)}{25}=\dfrac{65}{25}=2.6\).

Practice 2

Three equal squares, each area 4, centroids at \((1,1),(3,1),(5,1)\). Find the composite centroid.

Solution

\(\bar x=\dfrac{4(1)+4(3)+4(5)}{12}=\dfrac{36}{12}=3\). \(\bar y=1\) (all pieces share the same \(y\)).

Practice 3

A rectangle \(A_1=20\) at \((2,5)\) and a triangle \(A_2=6\) at \((6,2)\). Find the composite centroid.

Solution

\(\bar x=\dfrac{20(2)+6(6)}{26}=\dfrac{76}{26}=2.923\). \(\bar y=\dfrac{20(5)+6(2)}{26}=\dfrac{112}{26}=4.308\).

Practice 4

A T-shape: top rectangle \(A_1=12\) at \((3,5)\), stem \(A_2=8\) at \((3,2)\). Find the composite centroid.

Solution

\(\bar x=3\) (symmetric, both pieces share \(x=3\)). \(\bar y=\dfrac{12(5)+8(2)}{20}=\dfrac{76}{20}=3.8\).

Practice 5

State the centroid location (from the flat edge) of a semicircle of radius \(r=6\), using \(\bar y=\dfrac{4r}{3\pi}\).

Solution

\(\bar y=\dfrac{4(6)}{3\pi}=\dfrac{24}{9.4248}=2.546\).

Practice 6

Explain why an L-shaped composite section's centroid does NOT sit at the "visual middle" of the shape.

Solution

\(\bar x,\bar y\) are area-weighted averages, so the piece with more area pulls the composite centroid toward itself, exactly like a weighted average leans toward the larger weight. The "visual middle" only coincides with the centroid for symmetric shapes.

Mental model. A hole is negative area in both $A$ and $A x$ sums.

Method. Add solid pieces, subtract hole pieces with the hole's own centroid location.

Watch for. Do not place the hole centroid at the big shape centroid unless it truly is concentric.

A hole is just negative area, plug it into the exact same formulas with a minus sign. Removing material on one side of the gross centroid shifts the balance point the other way, away from the hole.

Cutting a hole on the right pulls the centroid left: less area on the right means the balance point moves toward the remaining material.

Worked example 1

A 10×8 rectangle (area 80, centroid \((5,4)\)) has a circular hole radius 2 (area \(=12.57\)) centered at \((7,4)\). Find the composite centroid.

\(\bar x=\dfrac{80(5)-12.57(7)}{80-12.57}=\dfrac{312.04}{67.43}=4.629\).

\(\bar y=\dfrac{80(4)-12.57(4)}{67.43}=4.0\) (unchanged, since the hole's \(y\) matches the gross centroid's \(y\)).

Worked example 2

A 12×6 rectangle (area 72, centroid \((6,3)\)) has two square holes: side 2 (area 4) at \((3,3)\), and side 1.5 (area 2.25) at \((9,4)\). Find the composite centroid.

Total area \(=72-4-2.25=65.75\).

\(\bar x=\dfrac{72(6)-4(3)-2.25(9)}{65.75}=\dfrac{399.75}{65.75}=6.080\).

\(\bar y=\dfrac{72(3)-4(3)-2.25(4)}{65.75}=\dfrac{195}{65.75}=2.966\).

Practice 7

An 8×6 rectangle (area 48, centroid \((4,3)\)) with a circular hole radius 1.5 (area 7.069) at \((5,3)\). Find \(\bar x,\bar y\).

Solution

Total \(=40.93\). \(\bar x=\dfrac{48(4)-7.069(5)}{40.93}=\dfrac{156.66}{40.93}=3.828\). \(\bar y=3.0\) (unchanged).

Practice 8

A 10×10 square (area 100, centroid \((5,5)\)) with a square hole side 3 (area 9) at \((2,2)\). Find \(\bar x,\bar y\).

Solution

Total \(=91\). \(\bar x=\bar y=\dfrac{100(5)-9(2)}{91}=\dfrac{482}{91}=5.297\).

Practice 9

A 6×4 rectangle (area 24, centroid \((3,2)\)) with two circular holes, each radius 1, at \((2,2)\) and \((4,2)\). Find \(\bar x,\bar y\).

Solution

Total \(=24-3.1416-3.1416=17.717\). \(\bar x=\dfrac{72-6.283-12.566}{17.717}=3.0\) (symmetric). \(\bar y=2\) (unchanged).

Practice 10

Explain why removing a hole located exactly AT the gross shape's centroid doesn't shift \(\bar x\) or \(\bar y\) at all.

Solution

The hole's coordinates equal the gross centroid's coordinates, so the amount subtracted from the numerator is exactly proportional to what's subtracted from the denominator, algebraically, removing area exactly at the current balance point can't shift that balance point.

Practice 11

A 12×8 rectangle (area 96, centroid \((6,4)\)) has a rectangular hole 4×2 (area 8) centered at \((9,6)\). Find \(\bar x,\bar y\).

Solution

Total \(=88\). \(\bar x=\dfrac{576-72}{88}=5.727\). \(\bar y=\dfrac{384-48}{88}=3.818\).

Practice 12

A washer is formed by removing a 2 cm-radius hole from the centre of a 6 cm-radius disk. Where is the composite centroid, and why?

Solution

Still at the exact centre. Since both the gross disk and the hole are concentric, sharing the same centroid, removing the hole doesn't move the balance point at all.

Mental model. $I$ about an axis = own-centroid $I_c$ plus $Ad^2$ shift to a parallel axis.

Method. Look up or compute $I_c$ for each piece, measure $d$ to the target axis, sum $I_c+Ad^2$.

Watch for. Parallel-axis only — axes must be parallel. Never use $Ad^2$ alone without $I_c$.

Second moment of area \(I\) measures how resistant a cross-section is to bending, larger \(I\) means a stiffer beam for the same material. The parallel axis theorem lets you shift \(I\) from a shape's own centroidal axis to any other parallel axis: the farther you move, the larger the \(Ad^2\) contribution grows.

Parallel-axis theorem: start from the easy centroidal \(I_c\), then add \(Ad^2\) to shift to any parallel reference axis.

Key idea

Rectangle about its own centroidal axis: $$I=\frac{bh^3}{12}.$$ Parallel axis theorem: $$I_{other} = I_{centroidal} + Ad^2,$$ where \(d\) is the distance between the two parallel axes.

Worked example 1

A rectangular beam cross-section, \(b=100\text{ mm}\), \(h=200\text{ mm}\). Find \(I\) about its own centroidal axis.

\(I=\dfrac{100(200)^3}{12}=\dfrac{800{,}000{,}000}{12}=66{,}666{,}667\text{ mm}^4\).

Worked example 2

The same rectangle's centroid sits 150 mm from a reference axis. Find \(I\) about that reference axis.

\(A=100(200)=20{,}000\text{ mm}^2\).

\(I_{ref}=66{,}666{,}667+20{,}000(150)^2=66{,}666{,}667+450{,}000{,}000=516{,}666{,}667\text{ mm}^4\).

Practice 13

A rectangle, \(b=80\text{ mm}, h=150\text{ mm}\). Find its centroidal \(I\).

Solution

\(I=\dfrac{80(150)^3}{12}=\dfrac{270{,}000{,}000}{12}=22{,}500{,}000\text{ mm}^4\).

Practice 14

A rectangle, \(b=60\text{ mm}, h=250\text{ mm}\). Find its centroidal \(I\).

Solution

\(I=\dfrac{60(250)^3}{12}=\dfrac{937{,}500{,}000}{12}=78{,}125{,}000\text{ mm}^4\).

Practice 15

A rectangle, \(A=20{,}000\text{ mm}^2\), centroidal \(I=40{,}000{,}000\text{ mm}^4\), centroid 100 mm from a reference axis. Find \(I\) about that axis.

Solution

\(I_{ref}=40{,}000{,}000+20{,}000(100)^2=40{,}000{,}000+200{,}000{,}000=240{,}000{,}000\text{ mm}^4\).

Practice 16

A shape has \(A=5000\text{ mm}^2\), centroidal \(I=1{,}000{,}000\text{ mm}^4\), centroid 80 mm from a reference axis. Find \(I_{ref}\), and what fraction came from the transfer term.

Solution

Transfer term \(=5000(80)^2=32{,}000{,}000\text{ mm}^4\). \(I_{ref}=1{,}000{,}000+32{,}000{,}000=33{,}000{,}000\text{ mm}^4\). Fraction from transfer \(=32{,}000{,}000/33{,}000{,}000=97.0\%\).

Practice 17

Two identical rectangles, each centroidal \(I=10{,}000{,}000\text{ mm}^4\) and \(A=8000\text{ mm}^2\), placed with centroids 30 mm on either side of a reference axis. Find the total \(I\) about that axis.

Solution

Each: \(10{,}000{,}000+8000(30)^2=10{,}000{,}000+7{,}200{,}000=17{,}200{,}000\text{ mm}^4\). Total \(=2(17{,}200{,}000)=34{,}400{,}000\text{ mm}^4\).

Practice 18

Explain why the parallel-axis transfer term (\(Ad^2\)) makes I-beams put most of their material in the flanges, far from the centroid, rather than spreading it evenly.

Solution

Because the transfer term is \(Ad^2\), doubling the distance \(d\) quadruples that term's contribution. Material far from the axis contributes vastly more stiffness per unit area than material near it, so I-beams concentrate material at the flanges to maximise \(I\) for a given amount of material, rather than wasting it near the centroid where it barely helps.

Parallel Axis Theorem

Jeff Hanson · ~12:00

$Ad^2$ checks.

Mental model. Composite $I$ is the sum of each piece's contribution about the same final axis.

Method. Find the composite centroid first if the neutral axis is through the centroid, then apply PAT piece by piece.

Watch for. Using the wrong reference axis (top fibre vs centroidal) breaks $\sigma=My/I$.

For a composite section: find the composite centroid first (Sections 0–1), then apply the parallel axis theorem to each piece individually, shifting its own \(I\) to the shared composite axis, then sum everything.

Worked example

A T-section: flange 100×20 mm (area 2000, centroid at \(y=190\text{ mm}\)), stem 20×180 mm (area 3600, centroid at \(y=90\text{ mm}\)). Find the composite centroidal \(I\).

Composite centroid: \(\bar y=\dfrac{2000(190)+3600(90)}{5600}=\dfrac{704{,}000}{5600}=125.71\text{ mm}\).

Flange's own \(I_1=\dfrac{100(20)^3}{12}=66{,}667\text{ mm}^4\). Stem's own \(I_2=\dfrac{20(180)^3}{12}=9{,}720{,}000\text{ mm}^4\).

Distances to composite centroid: \(d_1=|190-125.71|=64.29\text{ mm}\), \(d_2=|90-125.71|=35.71\text{ mm}\).

\(I_{1,total}=66{,}667+2000(64.29)^2=8{,}333{,}067\text{ mm}^4\). \(I_{2,total}=9{,}720{,}000+3600(35.71)^2=14{,}310{,}720\text{ mm}^4\).

Composite \(I=8{,}333{,}067+14{,}310{,}720=22{,}643{,}787\text{ mm}^4\).

T-section: locate the composite centroid first, then transfer each piece's \(I\) onto that shared axis with \(I_c+Ad^2\).

Practice 19

For the T-section above, is the composite centroid closer to the stem's own centroid (90) or the flange's (190)? Why, given the areas?

Solution

Closer to the stem (distance 35.71 vs 64.29), since the stem has more area (3600 vs 2000), pulling the composite centroid toward itself.

Practice 20

A composite section: \(R_1\) area 1500, own \(I=50{,}000\text{ mm}^4\), centroid 40 mm from the composite axis; \(R_2\) area 2500, own \(I=120{,}000\text{ mm}^4\), centroid 25 mm away (opposite side). Find the total composite \(I\).

Solution

\(R_1\): \(50{,}000+1500(40)^2=2{,}450{,}000\text{ mm}^4\). \(R_2\): \(120{,}000+2500(25)^2=1{,}682{,}500\text{ mm}^4\). Total \(=4{,}132{,}500\text{ mm}^4\).

Practice 21

Explain why you must find the OVERALL composite centroid first, before applying the parallel axis theorem to each piece.

Solution

The parallel-axis theorem shifts a piece's own centroidal \(I\) to a different, chosen reference axis. If that reference were each piece's own local centroid, you'd just recover each piece's own \(I\) with no meaningful combination, the whole point is measuring every piece's contribution relative to the same shared axis, which must be the composite centroid.

Practice 22

If a piece's own centroid happens to sit exactly at the composite centroid (\(\bar y=125.71\text{ mm}\)), what is its transfer term, and why?

Solution

Zero. The transfer term is \(Ad^2\), and \(d=0\) when the piece's own centroid coincides with the composite centroid, its full contribution comes purely from its own centroidal \(I\), with no transfer needed.

Practice 23

Two composite sections have the same total area and height. Section A concentrates area far from the centroid (I-beam-like); Section B spreads it evenly (solid rectangle). Which has the larger \(I\), and why?

Solution

Section A. The transfer term \(Ad^2\) rewards area placed far from the axis quadratically, concentrating area at the extremes maximises \(I\) for a given total area, exactly why I-beams are shaped the way they are.

Practice 24

Composite centroid: rectangle \(A_1=18\) at \((2,1.5)\), rectangle \(A_2=12\) at \((6,4)\), and triangle \(A_3=6\) at \((5,1)\). Find \((\bar x,\bar y)\).

\(\bar x=\dfrac{18(2)+12(6)+6(5)}{36}=\dfrac{138}{36}=3.833\).

\(\bar y=\dfrac{18(1.5)+12(4)+6(1)}{36}=\dfrac{81}{36}=2.250\).

Practice 25

An L-shape is modeled by two non-overlapping rectangles: \(A_1=20\) at \((2,1)\), \(A_2=10\) at \((5,4)\). Find centroid and explain briefly which piece dominates the centroid shift.

Solution

Total area \(=30\). \(\bar x=\dfrac{20(2)+10(5)}{30}=3.0\). \(\bar y=\dfrac{20(1)+10(4)}{30}=2.0\).

The larger area \(A_1\) pulls centroid toward \((2,1)\), so centroid sits closer to the lower-left piece.

Practice 26

Gross rectangle \(12\times8\) (\(A_g=96\), centroid \((6,4)\)) has a circular hole radius 1.8 at \((9,5)\). Find net centroid.

Solution

Hole area \(A_h=\pi(1.8)^2=10.179\). Net area \(A=96-10.179=85.821\).

\(\bar x=\dfrac{96(6)-10.179(9)}{85.821}=\dfrac{484.39}{85.821}=5.644\).

\(\bar y=\dfrac{96(4)-10.179(5)}{85.821}=\dfrac{333.10}{85.821}=3.881\).

Practice 27

A plate \(10\times6\) has two equal circular holes radius 1 at \((3,3)\) and \((7,3)\). Find centroid and justify symmetry result.

Solution

Gross centroid is \((5,3)\). Hole pair is symmetric about \(x=5\) and both lie on \(y=3\).

By symmetry, subtraction moments left/right and above/below balance equally, so centroid remains \((5,3)\).

Practice 28

A T-section uses flange \(120\times20\) and web \(20\times160\), web centered under flange. Take bottom of web as \(y=0\). Find \(\bar y\).

Flange: \(A_1=120(20)=2400\), \(y_1=160+10=170\). Web: \(A_2=20(160)=3200\), \(y_2=80\).

\(\bar y=\dfrac{2400(170)+3200(80)}{2400+3200}=\dfrac{664000}{5600}=118.57\text{ mm}\).

Practice 33

An I-like built section has web \(A_w=3000\text{ mm}^2,\ I_w=22\times10^6\text{ mm}^4\) at \(d_w=0\), and two flanges each \(A_f=1800\text{ mm}^2,\ I_f=2.5\times10^6\text{ mm}^4,\ d_f=85\text{ mm}\). Compute total \(I\).

Practice 37

Given \(\sigma=Mc/I\), two candidate sections see same \(M\) and \(c\): Section A has \(I=18\times10^6\), Section B has \(I=30\times10^6\) mm\(^4\). Compare stress ratio \(\sigma_A/\sigma_B\).

Practice 43

Rectangular tube approximation: outer \(180\times120\), inner void \(140\times80\), concentric. Compute centroidal \(I_x\).

Solution

Concentric means same centroidal axis, so subtract centroidal inertias.

\(I_{outer}=180(120)^3/12=25{,}920{,}000\text{ mm}^4\).

Practice 44

If section depth is fixed at 300 mm, compare qualitatively and quantitatively how doubling width \(b\) versus doubling depth \(h\) changes rectangle \(I=bh^3/12\).

Solution

\(I\) is linear in \(b\): doubling \(b\) doubles \(I\) (2x).

\(I\) is cubic in \(h\): doubling \(h\) multiplies \(I\) by \(2^3=8\).

So increasing depth is far more effective for bending stiffness than increasing width.

Practice 45

A composite section has calculated centroid \( \bar y=72\text{ mm}\). Engineer mistakenly uses \(d=|y_i-0|\) instead of \(|y_i-\bar y|\) in parallel-axis terms. Explain precisely why this is wrong.

Solution

Parallel-axis theorem needs distance between each piece's centroidal axis and the target axis. If target is composite centroidal axis, distance must be \(|y_i-\bar y|\).

Using \(|y_i-0|\) shifts to a different axis (datum axis), producing an \(I\) about the wrong reference and invalid stress predictions for centroidal bending.

Practice 46

Solution

Deficit \(=40-36.8=3.2\times10^6\). Percentage shortfall \(=3.2/40=8.0\%\).

A high-leverage fix is moving/add area farther from centroid (e.g., thicker/deeper flanges), because \(Ad^2\) grows with \(d^2\), boosting \(I\) efficiently.
