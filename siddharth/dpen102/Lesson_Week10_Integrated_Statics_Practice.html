What's on the test

The exam does not test weeks in isolation. Almost every question chains two or three of the skills below. Here is the examinable checklist, week by week, with the specific things you are expected to be able to do.

Weeks 1–3 — Forces, vectors & equilibrium

  • Resolve a force into components: \(F_x=F\cos\theta\), \(F_y=F\sin\theta\). Watch which angle is given (from horizontal or vertical).
  • Draw a correct free-body diagram: every external force, no internal ones, correct directions.
  • Apply \(\Sigma F_x=0\), \(\Sigma F_y=0\), \(\Sigma M=0\) to find unknown forces and reactions.
  • Moments: \(M=F\times d_\perp\). Choosing a smart moment centre kills an unknown instantly.

Week 4 — Stress, strain & internal actions

  • \(\sigma=F/A\) and \(\varepsilon=\Delta L/L_0\); unit discipline (MPa = N/mm²).
  • Allowable stress design: \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=\sigma_y/\mathrm{FoS}\); required area \(A_{req}=F/\sigma_{\text{allow}}\); minimum diameter from area.
  • Cut a member and find internal \(N\), \(V\), \(M\) at a section by equilibrium of one side.

Week 5 — Shear force & bending moment diagrams

  • Reactions first, always. Then build \(V(x)\) left-to-right as a running total of upward forces.
  • \(\dfrac{dV}{dx}=-w\), \(\dfrac{dM}{dx}=V\); \(M\) peaks where \(V\) crosses zero.
  • Standard results: \(M_{\max}=PL/4\) (central point load), \(wL^2/8\) (UDL), \(PL\) and \(wL^2/2\) (cantilever).
  • Shapes: point load → step in SFD; UDL → sloping SFD and parabolic BMD.

Week 6 — Trusses (method of sections)

  • Global reactions first, then one cut through at most three unknown members.
  • Pick the moment centre where two unknowns intersect to solve the third in one line.
  • Sign reading: assume tension (arrow pulling away from the body); a positive answer confirms tension, negative means compression.
  • Zero-force member rules (two non-collinear members at an unloaded joint; the odd member of three).

Week 7 — Friction

  • \(F_s\le\mu_sN\) (impending motion uses the equals sign); \(F_k=\mu_kN\) once sliding.
  • On an incline: \(N=mg\cos\theta\), driving component \(=mg\sin\theta\); tipping point at \(\tan\theta=\mu_s\).
  • Careful with \(N\): a push angled downward increases \(N\); a pull angled upward reduces it.
  • Belt friction: \(T_2/T_1=e^{\mu\beta}\), with \(\beta\) in radians.

Week 8 — Centroids & second moment of area

  • Composite centroid \(\bar y=\dfrac{\Sigma A_i\bar y_i}{\Sigma A_i}\); holes count as negative area.
  • Parallel-axis theorem \(I=I_c+Ad^2\) — the \(Ad^2\) term usually dominates.
  • Rectangle \(I_c=bh^3/12\); the \(h^3\) is why depth matters so much.
  • Bending stress \(\sigma=Mc/I\), with \(c\) the larger distance from the centroid to an extreme fibre.

Week 9 — Work & energy

  • \(W=Fd\cos\theta\); \(K=\frac12mv^2\); \(U_g=mgh\); \(U_s=\frac12kx^2\).
  • Energy bookkeeping: \(mgh = \frac12mv^2 + \frac12kx^2 + f_kd\). Friction always removes energy.
  • At maximum spring compression the block is momentarily at rest (\(v=0\)) — and don't forget it keeps descending through the compression distance on an incline.

Exam technique — where marks are actually lost

  • Units. Mixing mm and m is the single biggest source of lost marks. \(1\text{ MPa}=1\text{ N/mm}^2\); keep moments in N·mm when \(I\) is in mm⁴.
  • No FBD. Method marks are awarded for the diagram. Draw it even when you think you can do it in your head.
  • Radians vs degrees in belt friction and in calculator mode.
  • Sanity check. One sentence: is the magnitude plausible, is the sign sensible, does the direction match intuition?

Integrated 1Weeks 1–4

A 15 kg sign hangs from the far end of a horizontal, weightless boom pinned to a wall. A cable runs from that same end to a higher point on the wall, at 35° to the boom, boom length 2 m. (a) Find the cable tension. (b) If the cable's allowable stress is 150 MPa, find the minimum cable diameter.

The cable and the sign both act at the same point, 2 m from the pin.

Solution

Step 1 — FBD of the boom. Three things act on it: the pin reaction at A (unknown magnitude and direction), the cable tension \(T\) at 35° to the boom at the far end, and the sign's weight \(W=15(9.8)=147\text{ N}\) straight down, also at the far end.

Step 2 — kill the pin reaction. Take moments about A. The pin's own reaction has zero lever arm about itself, so it vanishes and \(T\) is the only unknown left.

Step 3 — only the perpendicular component of \(T\) makes a moment. The boom is horizontal, so the useful component is \(T\sin35^\circ\), acting 2 m out:

$$\Sigma M_A=0:\quad T\sin35^\circ(2)-147(2)=0$$

The 2 m cancels (both act at the same point), leaving \(T\sin35^\circ=147\).

$$T=\frac{147}{\sin35^\circ}=\frac{147}{0.5736}=256.3\text{ N}$$

(a) \(T=256.3\text{ N}\)

Step 4 — size the cable (Week 4). The cable must carry 256.3 N without exceeding 150 MPa:

$$A_{req}=\frac{T}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}}=\frac{256.3}{150\times10^6}=1.709\times10^{-6}\text{ m}^2=1.709\text{ mm}^2$$

Step 5 — area to diameter. For a solid circular cable \(A=\pi d^2/4\), so \(d=\sqrt{4A/\pi}\):

$$d=\sqrt{\frac{4(1.709)}{\pi}}=\sqrt{2.176}=1.475\text{ mm}$$

(b) \(d_{\min}=1.48\text{ mm}\)

Verify: a sub-2 mm cable seems slender for 15 kg, but 150 MPa is a high allowable stress and the load is modest, so the small size checks out. Note \(T>W\) because the cable is shallow — only its vertical component fights gravity.

Integrated 2Weeks 4–5

A simply-supported beam, span 5 m, carries a single 2000 N load at 2 m from A. (a) Find the reactions. (b) Find \(V\) and \(M\) just left of the load. (c) Describe the shape of the SFD and BMD.

Solution

Step 1 — reactions first, always. Take moments about A so \(R_A\) (zero lever arm) drops out:

$$\Sigma M_A=0:\quad R_B(5)-2000(2)=0\ \Rightarrow\ R_B=\frac{4000}{5}=800\text{ N}$$

Step 2 — vertical equilibrium gives the other.

$$\Sigma F_y=0:\quad R_A+800-2000=0\ \Rightarrow\ R_A=1200\text{ N}$$

Check: \(R_A+R_B=1200+800=2000\text{ N}\) = total load. Also \(R_A>R_B\) because the load sits nearer A. Both make sense.

(a) \(R_A=1200\text{ N}\), \(R_B=800\text{ N}\)

Step 3 — cut just left of the load (at \(x=2^-\)) and keep the left segment. The only force on it besides the cut is \(R_A\):

$$\Sigma F_y=0:\ V=R_A=1200\text{ N}\qquad \Sigma M_{\text{cut}}=0:\ M=R_A(2)=2400\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$

(b) \(V=1200\text{ N}\), \(M=2400\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\)

Step 4 — the shapes. No distributed load anywhere, so the SFD is flat between forces and the BMD is straight between them.

Shear steps down by the full 2000 N at the load and crosses zero there — which is exactly where the bending moment peaks.

(c) SFD: constant \(+1200\text{ N}\) from A, drops instantly by 2000 N to \(-800\text{ N}\) at the load, stays there to B, then \(R_B\) returns it to zero. BMD: rises linearly from 0 to \(2400\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\) at the load (where \(V\) changes sign), then falls linearly back to zero at B.

Cross-check with the standard formula \(M_{\max}=Pab/L=2000(2)(3)/5=2400\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\). Matches.

Integrated 3Weeks 4 & 6

Using the Week 6 truss (span 16 m, height 3 m, panel width 4 m, pin at A, roller at I, single 800 N load at D) and its panel-2 top-chord result \(F_{BD}=1066.67\text{ N}\) compression: if that member's material has \(\sigma_y=250\text{ MPa}\) and required \(FoS=2.5\), find the minimum cross-sectional area needed.

Solution

\(\sigma_{allow}=250/2.5=100\text{ MPa}\). \(A_{req}=1066.67/(100\times10^6)=1.067\times10^{-5}\text{ m}^2=10.67\text{ mm}^2\).

Verify: a genuine method-of-sections result (Week 6) now drives an allowable-stress sizing calculation (Week 4), exactly how the two weeks connect in practice.

Integrated 4Weeks 7 & 9

A 5 kg block starts at rest at the top of a 30° incline and slides 4 m down it (measured along the incline), \(\mu_k=0.2\). Using energy methods, find its speed at the bottom.

Solution

Step 1 — set up the energy ledger. The block starts at rest and ends moving, so gravity puts energy in and friction takes some out:

$$mgh=\tfrac12mv^2+f_kd$$

Step 2 — energy in (gravity). The distance 4 m is measured along the slope, so the vertical drop is \(h=d\sin\theta\):

$$h=4\sin30^\circ=4(0.5)=2\text{ m}\qquad mgh=5(9.8)(2)=98\text{ J}$$

Step 3 — energy out (friction). On an incline the normal force is only the perpendicular part of the weight, \(N=mg\cos\theta\):

$$N=5(9.8)\cos30^\circ=49(0.8660)=42.43\text{ N}$$

$$f_k=\mu_kN=0.2(42.43)=8.487\text{ N}$$

Friction acts over the full 4 m of sliding:

$$f_kd=8.487(4)=33.95\text{ J}$$

Step 4 — what's left becomes kinetic energy.

$$\tfrac12mv^2=98-33.95=64.05\text{ J}$$

$$v=\sqrt{\frac{2(64.05)}{5}}=\sqrt{25.62}=5.06\text{ m/s}$$

\(v=5.06\text{ m/s}\)

Verify: frictionless would give \(v=\sqrt{2gh}=\sqrt{39.2}=6.26\text{ m/s}\). Friction removed about 35% of the energy, so a slower 5.06 m/s is exactly the right ballpark. Note \(\tan30^\circ=0.577\gg\mu_k=0.2\), so the block definitely does slide.

Integrated 5Weeks 4 & 8

A beam's cross-section is the T-shape from Week 8 (composite centroid \(\bar y=125.71\text{ mm}\), composite \(I=22{,}643{,}787\text{ mm}^4\), total section height 200 mm). If this section carries an internal bending moment \(M=15\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}\), find the maximum bending stress using \(\sigma=Mc/I\) (the natural extension of Week 8's \(I\) into an actual stress check).

Solution

The larger extreme-fibre distance is on the bottom: \(c=125.71\text{ mm}\) (the top is only \(200-125.71=74.29\text{ mm}\) from the centroid, smaller).

\(M=15{,}000{,}000\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}\). \(\sigma=\dfrac{Mc}{I}=\dfrac{15{,}000{,}000(125.71)}{22{,}643{,}787}=\dfrac{1{,}885{,}650{,}000}{22{,}643{,}787}=83.28\text{ MPa}\).

Integrated 6Weeks 4 & 7

Using Week 7's wedge worked example (\(P=277.3\text{ N}\) to lift a 500 N block, \(\theta=15^\circ,\mu=0.25\)), the lifted block is supported by a rod of diameter 12 mm. Find the stress in the rod under the 500 N load.

Integrated 7Weeks 4 & 5

A cantilever beam, fixed at A, length 3 m, carries a UDL \(w=200\text{ N/m}\) over its full length plus a 400 N point load at the free end. (a) Find the reaction force and moment at A. (b) Find \(V\) and \(M\) at midspan (1.5 m from A).

Solution

Step 1 — replace the UDL by its resultant. A UDL of \(w\) over length \(L\) has total \(wL\), acting at the middle of that length:

$$W_{\text{UDL}}=200(3)=600\text{ N}\ \text{ acting at }1.5\text{ m from A}$$

Step 2 — vertical equilibrium. A fixed support supplies both a force and a moment:

$$\Sigma F_y=0:\quad R_{Ay}=600+400=1000\text{ N}$$

Step 3 — moment at the fixed end. Take moments about A; each load contributes force × distance from A:

$$M_A=600(1.5)+400(3)=900+1200=2100\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$

(a) \(R_{Ay}=1000\text{ N}\), \(M_A=2100\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\)

Cross-check against the standard cantilever results: \(wL^2/2=200(9)/2=900\) and \(PL=400(3)=1200\); adding them gives 2100 N·m. Matches.

Step 4 — cut at midspan, keep the free-end side. This side is easier because it has no unknown reactions on it. The segment from the cut to the tip is 1.5 m long and carries:

• UDL on this segment: \(200(1.5)=300\text{ N}\), acting at its middle, \(0.75\text{ m}\) from the cut.
• The tip load: \(400\text{ N}\), at \(1.5\text{ m}\) from the cut.

Step 5 — equilibrium of that segment.

$$V=300+400=700\text{ N}$$

$$M=300(0.75)+400(1.5)=225+600=825\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}$$

(b) \(V=700\text{ N}\), \(M=825\text{ N}\cdot\text{m}\)

Verify: both are smaller than the values at the fixed end (1000 N and 2100 N·m), which is right — on a cantilever, \(V\) and \(M\) both grow steadily towards the support and vanish at the free tip.

Integrated 8Weeks 1 & 7

A 25 kg crate on a 20° incline is connected by a rope (parallel to the slope) to a hanging 10 kg mass over a frictionless pulley at the top. \(\mu_s\) between crate and incline is 0.3. Determine whether the system is in equilibrium.

Solution

Step 1 — weights. Crate \(W_c=25(9.8)=245\text{ N}\); hanging mass \(W_h=10(9.8)=98\text{ N}\).

Step 2 — resolve the crate's weight along and perpendicular to the slope.

$$W_{\parallel}=245\sin20^\circ=245(0.3420)=83.79\text{ N}\ \text{(down the slope)}$$

$$N=W_{\perp}=245\cos20^\circ=245(0.9397)=230.23\text{ N}$$

Step 3 — the friction the surface can supply. This is the ceiling, not the actual value:

$$F_s^{\max}=\mu_sN=0.3(230.23)=69.07\text{ N}$$

Step 4 — which way does the system want to move? The rope pulls the crate up the slope with \(T=98\text{ N}\) (a frictionless pulley just redirects the hanging weight). Gravity pulls it down the slope with 83.79 N. Since \(98>83.79\), the crate tends to be dragged up the incline.

Step 5 — how much friction is actually needed? Only enough to make up the difference:

$$F_{\text{req}}=98-83.79=14.21\text{ N}\ \text{(acting down the slope, opposing the impending motion)}$$

Step 6 — compare against the ceiling.

$$F_{\text{req}}=14.21\text{ N}\ \ll\ F_s^{\max}=69.07\text{ N}\quad\checkmark$$

The system is in equilibrium; nothing moves and \(a=0\). Actual friction force = 14.21 N down-slope.

Verify: friction is only using about 21% of its available capacity, so there is a comfortable margin. Note the classic trap — you must compare the required friction against \(\mu_sN\), not assume friction equals \(\mu_sN\). Static friction only supplies what is needed.

Integrated 9Weeks 7 & 9

A 3 kg block slides down a frictionless 25° incline from rest, travels 2 m, then compresses a spring (\(k=1000\text{ N/m}\)) aligned along the incline. Find the maximum spring compression.

Solution

At maximum compression \(x\), the block is momentarily at rest, so ALL height dropped (over the full \(2+x\) distance along the slope) converts to spring PE:

\(mg(2+x)\sin\theta=\dfrac12kx^2 \Rightarrow 3(9.8)(2+x)(0.4226)=500x^2 \Rightarrow 12.42(2+x)=500x^2\).

\(500x^2-12.42x-24.84=0\). Solving: \(x=\dfrac{12.42+\sqrt{12.42^2+4(500)(24.84)}}{1000}=\dfrac{12.42+223.24}{1000}=0.236\text{ m}\).

Integrated 10Weeks 4 & 7

A cable runs over a drum behaving like a belt-friction system (wrap angle \(\pi\) rad, \(\mu=0.2\)) to hold a load, with a worker holding the free end at 200 N. (a) Find the maximum load \(W\) it can support without slipping. (b) That load hangs from a rod with allowable stress 120 MPa, find the minimum rod diameter.

Friction around the drum lets a small hold force \(T_1\) balance a much larger load \(T_2\).

Solution

Step 1 — identify which tension is which. In \(T_2/T_1=e^{\mu\beta}\), \(T_2\) is always the tight (larger) side and \(T_1\) the slack side. Here the worker's 200 N is the slack side and the load is the tight side.

Step 2 — check the units of \(\beta\). It must be in radians. Here \(\beta=\pi\) rad (a half wrap, 180°), already correct.

Step 3 — apply the belt equation.

$$\frac{T_2}{T_1}=e^{\mu\beta}=e^{0.2\pi}=e^{0.6283}=1.874$$

$$W=T_2=200(1.874)=374.8\text{ N}$$

(a) \(W_{\max}=374.8\text{ N}\)

Step 4 — size the hanger rod (Week 4). The rod carries the full load \(W\):

$$A_{req}=\frac{W}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}}=\frac{374.8}{120\times10^6}=3.123\times10^{-6}\text{ m}^2=3.123\text{ mm}^2$$

Step 5 — convert area to diameter.

$$d=\sqrt{\frac{4A}{\pi}}=\sqrt{\frac{4(3.123)}{\pi}}=\sqrt{3.976}=1.994\text{ mm}$$

(b) \(d_{\min}=1.99\text{ mm}\), so specify a 2 mm rod.

Verify: the drum nearly doubles the holdable load for free — that is exactly why capstans and bollards work. Watch the trap: had \(\beta\) been given as 180° and used directly, \(e^{0.2(180)}\) would be astronomically wrong.

Integrated 11Weeks 3, 5 & 11

A simply supported beam, span 6 m, carries a 2.4 kN vertical point load at midspan and a 1.8 kN force at 30° below horizontal applied at \(x=4\) m. (a) Resolve the angled force. (b) Find vertical reactions. (c) Find the largest bending moment. (d) If allowable bending stress is 95 MPa and extreme fiber distance is 60 mm, find minimum required \(I\).

Solution

\(F_y=1.8\sin30^\circ=0.9\text{ kN}\) downward, \(F_x=1.8\cos30^\circ=1.56\text{ kN}\) (balanced by pin horizontal reaction, no effect on BMD).

Total downward vertical load \(=2.4+0.9=3.3\text{ kN}\). Moments about A: \(R_B(6)=2.4(3)+0.9(4)=10.8\Rightarrow R_B=1.8\text{ kN}\), so \(R_A=1.5\text{ kN}\).

Shear changes sign at \(x=3\) (just after mid-load): \(M_{max}=R_A(3)=4.5\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}=4.5\times10^6\text{ Nmm}\).

Use \(\sigma=Mc/I\Rightarrow I_{min}=Mc/\sigma=4.5\times10^6(60)/95=2.84\times10^6\text{ mm}^4\).

A cantilever, length 2.8 m, carries a UDL \(w=0.9\text{ kN/m}\) over full length and a tip load \(1.4\text{ kN}\). Internal moment at the wall is applied to a rectangular section \(b=80\text{ mm}\), \(h=180\text{ mm}\). Find bending stress at extreme fiber.

Integrated 14Weeks 7 & 9

A 6 kg block starts from rest 3.2 m up a \(24^\circ\) incline (\(\mu_k=0.18\)), then on reaching bottom compresses a spring (\(k=1200\text{ N/m}\)) on level frictionless ground. Find maximum compression.

Integrated 16Weeks 1, 7 & 11

A 30 kg crate on a horizontal floor is pushed by a force \(P\) at 20° below horizontal. \(\mu_s=0.35\), \(\mu_k=0.28\). (a) Find the minimum \(P\) to start motion. (b) If \(P=170\) N and the crate is moving, find acceleration.

Because \(P\) points downward, it presses the crate into the floor and increases \(N\) — which increases friction. This is the crux of the problem.

Solution

Step 1 — resolve \(P\). At 20° below horizontal: horizontal component \(P\cos20^\circ\) (drives motion), vertical component \(P\sin20^\circ\) pointing down.

Step 2 — vertical equilibrium gives \(N\). The crate does not lift off, so the floor must support the weight plus the downward push:

$$N=mg+P\sin20^\circ=30(9.8)+P(0.3420)=294+0.3420P$$

Step 3 — condition for impending motion. The horizontal drive exactly equals the maximum static friction:

$$P\cos20^\circ=\mu_sN=0.35(294+0.3420P)$$

Step 4 — collect the \(P\) terms. This is the step people rush:

$$0.9397P=102.9+0.1197P\ \Rightarrow\ P(0.9397-0.1197)=102.9$$

$$P=\frac{102.9}{0.8200}=125.5\text{ N}$$

(a) \(P_{\min}\approx126\text{ N}\) to start the crate moving.

$$N=294+170(0.3420)=294+58.1=352.1\text{ N}$$

$$f_k=\mu_kN=0.28(352.1)=98.6\text{ N}$$

$$F_{\text{net}}=P\cos20^\circ-f_k=170(0.9397)-98.6=159.7-98.6=61.1\text{ N}$$

$$a=\frac{F_{\text{net}}}{m}=\frac{61.1}{30}=2.04\text{ m/s}^2$$

(b) \(a=2.04\text{ m/s}^2\)

Verify: \(P=170\text{ N}\) comfortably exceeds the 126 N needed to break away, so a positive acceleration is expected. Had \(P\) been angled upward instead, \(N\) would have dropped and the crate would have been much easier to push — which is why you pull a trolley rather than push it down.

Checking from the left gives the same: \(2.5(6)-10(3)=15-30=-15\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}\) ✓

(c) \(M_{x=3}=+7.5\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}\) (sagging), \(M_B=-15\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}\) (hogging). \(M_{\max}=15\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}\) in magnitude, at the support B.

Verify: the biggest moment is negative and sits over the support, not at midspan — the signature of an overhang. Practically, that means the beam's top fibres are in tension at B, which is where reinforcement would go.

Integrated 32Week 8

An I-section is built from two flanges, each 150 mm × 20 mm, and a web 10 mm × 200 mm, giving a total depth of 240 mm. Find \(I\) about the horizontal centroidal axis, and state what fraction comes from the \(Ad^2\) transfer terms.

Solution

Step 1 — locate the centroid. The section is symmetric top-to-bottom, so the centroid is at mid-depth, 120 mm from either face. No \(\bar y\) calculation needed.

Step 2 — each flange. Its own centroid sits 10 mm in from the outer face, so the transfer distance is \(d=120-10=110\text{ mm}\):

$$A_f=150(20)=3000\text{ mm}^2,\qquad I_{c,f}=\frac{150(20)^3}{12}=100{,}000\text{ mm}^4$$

$$A_fd^2=3000(110)^2=3000(12{,}100)=36.3\times10^6\text{ mm}^4$$

$$I_f=0.1\times10^6+36.3\times10^6=36.4\times10^6\text{ mm}^4\ \text{each}$$

Step 3 — the web. Its centroid coincides with the section centroid, so \(d=0\) and there is no transfer term at all:

$$I_w=\frac{10(200)^3}{12}=\frac{10(8{,}000{,}000)}{12}=6.67\times10^6\text{ mm}^4$$

Step 4 — add everything.

$$I=2(36.4\times10^6)+6.67\times10^6=72.8\times10^6+6.67\times10^6=79.5\times10^6\text{ mm}^4$$

\(I=79.5\times10^6\text{ mm}^4\)

Step 5 — the fraction from transfer terms.

$$\frac{2(36.3\times10^6)}{79.5\times10^6}=\frac{72.6}{79.5}=91\%$$

About 91% of \(I\) comes from the \(Ad^2\) terms, i.e. purely from having pushed the flanges far from the axis.

Verify: the flanges' own \(I_c\) contributes a laughable 0.2 million out of 79.5 million. This is the entire design philosophy of an I-beam — the shape matters far more than the amount of steel, because \(I\) rewards distance quadratically.

Integrated 33Week 7

A 10° wedge is driven under a 2 kN block to lift it. The coefficient of friction is \(\mu=0.2\). Using the friction-angle model \(P=W\tan(\theta+\phi)\) where \(\tan\phi=\mu\): (a) find the driving force \(P\). (b) Determine whether the wedge is self-locking.

Solution

Step 1 — find the friction angle. The friction angle \(\phi\) is the angle whose tangent is \(\mu\):

$$\phi=\arctan(0.2)=11.31^\circ$$

Step 2 — apply the wedge formula. Driving the wedge in must overcome both the geometric rise (\(\theta\)) and friction (\(\phi\)):

$$P=W\tan(\theta+\phi)=2000\tan(10^\circ+11.31^\circ)=2000\tan(21.31^\circ)$$

$$P=2000(0.3902)=780\text{ N}$$

(a) \(P=780\text{ N}\)

Step 3 — the self-locking test. A wedge stays put when released if friction alone can hold it, which happens when the wedge angle is smaller than the friction angle:

$$\theta<\phi\ ?\qquad 10^\circ<11.31^\circ\quad\checkmark$$

(b) The wedge is self-locking — remove \(P\) and the block stays lifted rather than squeezing the wedge back out.

Verify: without friction you would need only \(2000\tan10^\circ=353\text{ N}\), so friction more than doubles the effort to insert the wedge. That is the trade-off: friction costs you on the way in but is exactly what makes the wedge safe once placed. Self-locking is why chocks, door wedges, and machine-tool wedges work at all.

Integrated 34Weeks 5 & 11

A simply-supported steel beam spans 8 m and carries a single central point load \(P\). The chosen section has \(S=I/c=500\times10^3\text{ mm}^3\), with \(\sigma_y=250\text{ MPa}\) and a required FoS of 1.67. Find the maximum safe value of \(P\).

Solution

Step 1 — capacity: what stress is allowed?

$$\sigma_{\text{allow}}=\frac{\sigma_y}{\mathrm{FoS}}=\frac{250}{1.67}=149.7\text{ MPa}$$

Step 2 — turn allowable stress into an allowable moment. Since \(\sigma=M/S\), the section can resist:

$$M_{\text{allow}}=\sigma_{\text{allow}}S=149.7(500\times10^3)=74.85\times10^6\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}=74.85\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$$

Step 3 — demand: express \(M_{\max}\) in terms of \(P\). For a central point load on a simple span:

$$M_{\max}=\frac{PL}{4}=\frac{P(8)}{4}=2P\ \ (\text{kN}\cdot\text{m when }P\text{ is in kN})$$

Step 4 — set demand equal to capacity.

$$2P=74.85\ \Rightarrow\ P=37.4\text{ kN}$$

\(P_{\max}=37.4\text{ kN}\)

Verify the loop: at \(P=37.4\text{ kN}\), \(M=74.85\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}\) and \(\sigma=74.85\times10^6/500\times10^3=149.7\text{ MPa}\), exactly the allowable. Note this checks bending only; a full design would also check shear, deflection, and buckling.

Integrated 35Weeks 7 & 9

A rope passes over a fixed drum (\(\mu=0.25\), wrap angle \(\beta=3\pi/2\)) to lower a 60 kg crate at constant speed. (a) Find the hold force the worker must apply. (b) If the worker lets go completely and the crate falls freely 3 m, find its impact speed.

Solution

Step 1 — identify tight and slack sides. The crate's weight is the tight side:

$$T_2=60(9.8)=588\text{ N}$$

The worker's hold force is the slack side, \(T_1\), which is what we want.

Step 2 — the wrap angle in radians. \(\beta=3\pi/2=4.712\text{ rad}\) (one and a half half-turns, i.e. 270°).

Step 3 — apply belt friction. "Constant speed" means motion impends, so the equality holds:

$$\frac{T_2}{T_1}=e^{\mu\beta}=e^{0.25(4.712)}=e^{1.178}=3.248$$

$$T_1=\frac{588}{3.248}=181\text{ N}$$

(a) The worker needs only \(181\text{ N}\) to hold a 588 N crate.

The drum multiplies the worker's effort by 3.25×. Wrapping one more turn would multiply it again, which is exactly how a capstan or a climber's belay device works.

Step 4 — free fall (Week 9). With the rope released there is no friction and no tension, so all the gravitational PE becomes kinetic energy:

$$mgh=\tfrac12mv^2\ \Rightarrow\ v=\sqrt{2gh}$$

The mass cancels:

$$v=\sqrt{2(9.8)(3)}=\sqrt{58.8}=7.67\text{ m/s}$$

(b) \(v=7.67\text{ m/s}\) (about 28 km/h)

Verify: the mass drops out of the free-fall answer, as it must. The contrast is the point of the question — 181 N of grip is the difference between a controlled lower and a 28 km/h impact.

Integrated 36Weeks 3 & 4

A wall bracket has a horizontal member AB of length 2 m pinned to the wall at A, and a diagonal strut from B down to the wall at C, 1.5 m below A. A 4 kN load hangs at B. (a) Find the force in each member, stating tension or compression. (b) If member AB has \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=120\text{ MPa}\), find its required area.

Solution

Step 1 — geometry of the strut. Put A at the origin, so \(B=(2,0)\) and \(C=(0,-1.5)\). The strut BC has length

$$L_{BC}=\sqrt{2^2+1.5^2}=\sqrt{6.25}=2.5\text{ m}$$

Its direction cosines from B toward C are \(\left(\dfrac{-2}{2.5},\dfrac{-1.5}{2.5}\right)=(-0.8,\,-0.6)\).

Step 2 — isolate joint B. Only three forces act there: the 4 kN load down, the force in AB, and the force in BC. Assume both members are in tension (pulling away from B).

Step 3 — vertical equilibrium. AB is horizontal, so it contributes nothing vertically. Only BC can fight the load:

$$\Sigma F_y=0:\quad -0.6F_{BC}-4=0\ \Rightarrow\ F_{BC}=-6.67\text{ kN}$$

Negative, so the tension assumption was wrong: BC carries 6.67 kN in compression (it is a strut, as the name suggests).

Step 4 — horizontal equilibrium.

$$\Sigma F_x=0:\quad -F_{AB}-0.8F_{BC}=0\ \Rightarrow\ -F_{AB}-0.8(-6.67)=0$$

$$F_{AB}=5.33\text{ kN}\ \text{(positive, so tension)}$$

(a) \(F_{AB}=5.33\text{ kN}\) tension; \(F_{BC}=6.67\text{ kN}\) compression.

Sanity: the strut pushes B up and outward; the horizontal tie holds B back against the wall. Both magnitudes exceed the 4 kN load because the geometry is shallow.

Step 5 — size the tie AB.

$$A_{req}=\frac{5330\text{ N}}{120\text{ N/mm}^2}=44.4\text{ mm}^2$$

(b) \(A_{req}=44.4\text{ mm}^2\) (a 7.6 mm diameter round bar would do).

Note that AB, being in tension, can be sized on stress alone. BC is in compression, so a real design would also have to check buckling, which for a slender 2.5 m strut would very likely govern over crushing.

Integrated 37Weeks 5, 8 & 11

A cantilever 2.5 m long carries a UDL of 5 kN/m over its full length. The section is rectangular with \(b=120\text{ mm}\) and the allowable bending stress is 10 MPa. (a) Find the design moment. (b) Find the minimum depth \(h\). (c) Verify your rounded choice.

Solution

Step 1 — the critical moment is at the fixed end. For a cantilever with a full UDL:

$$M_{\max}=\frac{wL^2}{2}=\frac{5(2.5)^2}{2}=\frac{5(6.25)}{2}=15.63\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$$

(a) \(M=15.63\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}=15.63\times10^6\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}\)

Note the cantilever's \(wL^2/2\) is four times the simply-supported \(wL^2/8\) for the same span and load. Cantilevers are expensive.

Step 2 — required section modulus.

$$S_{req}=\frac{M}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}}=\frac{15.63\times10^6}{10}=1{,}563{,}000\text{ mm}^3$$

Step 3 — relate \(S\) to the unknown depth.

$$S=\frac{bh^2}{6}=\frac{120h^2}{6}=20h^2$$

Step 4 — solve.

$$20h^2=1{,}563{,}000\ \Rightarrow\ h^2=78{,}150\ \Rightarrow\ h=279.6\text{ mm}$$

(b) \(h_{\min}=280\text{ mm}\)

Step 5 — verify the rounded section. With \(h=280\text{ mm}\):

$$S=20(280)^2=1{,}568{,}000\text{ mm}^3\qquad \sigma=\frac{15.63\times10^6}{1{,}568{,}000}=9.97\text{ MPa}$$

(c) \(9.97\text{ MPa}<10\text{ MPa}\) ✓ — passes, though only just. In practice you would step up to 300 mm for a sensible margin.

Integrated 39Week 7

A 40 kg block rests on a 25° incline with \(\mu_s=0.35\). A force \(P\) is applied parallel to the slope. Find the full range of \(P\) for which the block remains stationary.

Solution

Step 1 — the standard incline quantities. \(W=40(9.8)=392\text{ N}\).

$$W_{\parallel}=392\sin25^\circ=392(0.4226)=165.7\text{ N}\ \text{(down-slope)}$$

$$N=392\cos25^\circ=392(0.9063)=355.3\text{ N}$$

$$F^{\max}=\mu_sN=0.35(355.3)=124.3\text{ N}$$

Step 2 — why there are two limits. Friction can point either way, so there is a window of \(P\) values. Too little \(P\) and the block slides down; too much and you shove it up. Each boundary is where friction is fully mobilised.

Step 3 — lower limit (block about to slide DOWN). Friction acts up the slope at full value, helping \(P\):

$$P_{\min}+124.3=165.7\ \Rightarrow\ P_{\min}=41.4\text{ N}$$

Step 4 — upper limit (block about to slide UP). Now \(P\) is winning, so friction flips to act down the slope, resisting \(P\):

$$P_{\max}=165.7+124.3=290.0\text{ N}$$

$$41.4\text{ N}\ \le\ P\ \le\ 290.0\text{ N}$$

Verify: the window is centred on 165.7 N (the force needed with no friction at all) and extends \(\pm124.3\text{ N}\), exactly the friction capacity, in each direction. Note that \(\tan25^\circ=0.466>\mu_s=0.35\), so the block cannot sit there unaided — consistent with \(P_{\min}>0\).

Integrated 40Weeks 4, 5, 8 & 11

Full synthesis, exam style. A simply-supported steel beam spans 7 m and carries a UDL of 6 kN/m over its full length plus a 20 kN point load at midspan. The section has \(I=120\times10^6\text{ mm}^4\) and a total depth of 300 mm. Steel: \(\sigma_y=250\text{ MPa}\), required FoS = 1.6.

(a) Reactions. (b) \(M_{\max}\). (c) Maximum bending stress. (d) Does it pass? (e) How much extra central point load could still be added?

Solution

Step 1 — total load and reactions. The UDL total is \(wL=6(7)=42\text{ kN}\); adding the point load gives 62 kN. Both the UDL and the central load are symmetric about midspan, so the reactions split evenly:

$$R_A=R_B=\frac{62}{2}=31\text{ kN}$$

(a) \(R_A=R_B=31\text{ kN}\)

Step 2 — maximum moment by superposition. Both load cases peak at the same place (midspan), so their standard results can simply be added:

$$M_{\max}=\underbrace{\frac{wL^2}{8}}_{\text{UDL}}+\underbrace{\frac{PL}{4}}_{\text{point}}=\frac{6(7)^2}{8}+\frac{20(7)}{4}$$

$$M_{\max}=36.75+35=71.75\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$$

(b) \(M_{\max}=71.75\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}\)

Superposition is only legitimate here because both maxima coincide at midspan. If the point load were off-centre you would have to build the SFD and locate where \(V=0\).

Step 3 — bending stress. Convert to N and mm: \(M=71.75\times10^6\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}\), and \(c=300/2=150\text{ mm}\):

$$\sigma_{\max}=\frac{Mc}{I}=\frac{71.75\times10^6(150)}{120\times10^6}=\frac{10{,}762.5\times10^6}{120\times10^6}=89.7\text{ MPa}$$

(c) \(\sigma_{\max}=89.7\text{ MPa}\)

Step 4 — compare against the allowable.

$$\sigma_{\text{allow}}=\frac{\sigma_y}{\mathrm{FoS}}=\frac{250}{1.6}=156.25\text{ MPa}$$

$$89.7\text{ MPa}<156.25\text{ MPa}\quad\checkmark$$

(d) The beam passes, using only 57% of its allowable stress.

Step 5 — spare capacity. First find the largest moment the section could take:

$$M_{\text{allow}}=\frac{\sigma_{\text{allow}}I}{c}=\frac{156.25(120\times10^6)}{150}=125\times10^6\text{ N}\cdot\text{mm}=125\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$$

Spare moment available:

$$\Delta M=125-71.75=53.25\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}$$

Step 6 — convert spare moment back into load. An extra central point load \(\Delta P\) adds \(\Delta M=\Delta P\,L/4=1.75\,\Delta P\):

$$1.75\,\Delta P=53.25\ \Rightarrow\ \Delta P=30.4\text{ kN}$$

(e) A further 30.4 kN could be added at midspan before the allowable stress is reached.

Verify the whole chain: total central load would become \(20+30.4=50.4\text{ kN}\), giving \(M=36.75+50.4(7)/4=36.75+88.2=125\text{ kN}\cdot\text{m}\), exactly \(M_{\text{allow}}\). ✓ Remember this is a bending check only — deflection would very likely govern before you got anywhere near this load.
