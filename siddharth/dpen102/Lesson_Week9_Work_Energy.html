Work, Energy & Conservation

Energy methods answer "how fast?" and "how far?" without tracking acceleration every step of the way. Compare the total energy at a start state and an end state, and what happens in between can stay a mystery, as long as you account for any work done by non-conservative forces like friction.

Start here The big picture — what this week is about

Work–energy replaces force/acceleration integration with scalars: $U=\Delta T$ (and potential terms when conservative forces appear).

Gravitational and spring potential energy let you write conservation statements for many DPEN102 dynamics-style problems without finding every intermediate acceleration.

Why this matters

Impact, springs and height-change problems are faster and less error-prone with energy than with Newton's laws alone.

FormulaMeaning / use
\(W=F\cdot d\cos\alpha\)Work by a constant force: force component along the displacement times distance. Units: joules (J).
\(K=\dfrac12 mv^2\)Kinetic energy: energy of motion. Always \(\ge 0\).
\(W_{\mathrm{net}}=\Delta K=K_2-K_1\)Work–energy theorem: net work done on a body equals its change in kinetic energy.
\(U_g=mgh\)Gravitational potential energy relative to a chosen height datum (\(h=0\)).
\(U_s=\dfrac12 kx^2\)Elastic (spring) potential energy; \(x\) = stretch or compression from the natural length.
\(K_1+U_1=K_2+U_2\)Conservation of mechanical energy when only conservative forces act (no friction / drag losses).
\(K_1+U_1+W_{\mathrm{nc}}=K_2+U_2\)General energy balance: non-conservative work \(W_{\mathrm{nc}}\) (e.g. friction) enters as an extra term.

\(F_s=kx\)Hooke's law: spring force proportional to deflection (useful for FBDs; energy uses \(\tfrac12 kx^2\)).

Symbols: \(W\) = work (J); \(K\) = kinetic energy; \(U_g,U_s\) = gravitational / spring potential; \(m\) = mass (kg); \(v\) = speed (m/s); \(h\) = height above datum (m); \(k\) = spring stiffness (N/m); \(x\) = spring deflection (m); \(\alpha\) = angle between force and displacement; \(g\approx 9.8\text{ m/s}^2\); \(\Delta\) = capital Greek delta, meaning "change in".

Method tip: pick two clear states (start / end), list every energy form present at each, set totals equal (plus \(W_{\mathrm{nc}}\) if needed). You never need the path in between for conservative forces.

Intro What energy methods are & why we use them

Up to now, most statics problems asked "what force?" Energy methods ask a different question: what speed, what height, how far?, and they answer it by bookkeeping. Work is energy transferred by a force acting through a distance. Kinetic energy \(K\) is energy of motion. Potential energy \(U\) is energy stored by position, high up against gravity, or squashed into a spring. The golden rule is that energy is never created or destroyed: it only changes form.

That single idea is powerful because it lets you jump from a start state to an end state in one equation. You do not need the acceleration at every instant, or the shape of a curved ramp, or the force history of a spring. If you know the energies at both ends (and any work lost to friction), the rest follows.

Energy bookkeeping: list every form at the start, every form at the end, and any non-conservative work in between.

Why should I care?

A roller-coaster car on a curved track, a spring launching a block up a ramp, a crate sliding then flying off a ledge, all of these are painful with \(F=ma\) because the force keeps changing. With energy you write one balance and solve for speed or height directly. That is how engineers size escape velocities, bumper stops, bungee cords, and flywheels: compare stored energy in and kinetic energy out.

The approach, in one breath

Section 0 links net work to the change in kinetic energy. Section 1 stores energy as height (\(U_g=mgh\)) and uses conservation for frictionless falls and ramps. Mental model. Net work of all forces equals change in kinetic energy: $\sum U_{1\to2}=T_2-T_1$.

Method. Identify path, compute work for each force (or move conservative ones into PE), equate to $\Delta T$.

Watch for. Friction work is negative and path-dependent — it is not a potential.

Net work done on an object always shows up as a change in its kinetic energy, nothing more, nothing less. Push in the direction of motion and \(K\) rises; oppose the motion (brakes, friction) and \(K\) falls. If you know the work, you know the speed change, without finding acceleration first.

Key idea

$$W_{net} = \Delta K = K_2-K_1, \qquad K=\frac12mv^2.$$

Worked example 1

A 5 kg block starts at rest; a net force does 40 J of work on it. Find its final speed.

\(\Delta K=40\text{ J}=K_2-0\). \(40=\dfrac12(5)v^2 \Rightarrow v^2=16 \Rightarrow v=4\text{ m/s}\).

Worked example 2

A 10 kg cart moving at 3 m/s has +35 J of net work done on it. Find its final speed.

\(K_1=\dfrac12(10)(3)^2=45\text{ J}\). \(K_2=45+35=80\text{ J}\).

\(80=\dfrac12(10)v^2 \Rightarrow v^2=16 \Rightarrow v=4\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 1

An 8 kg object starts at rest, net work \(=64\text{ J}\). Find the final speed.

Solution

\(64=\dfrac12(8)v^2 \Rightarrow v^2=16 \Rightarrow v=4\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 2

A 3 kg ball moving at 6 m/s has \(-20\text{ J}\) of net work done on it. Find the final speed.

Solution

\(K_1=\dfrac12(3)(36)=54\text{ J}\). \(K_2=54-20=34\text{ J}\). \(v^2=2(34)/3=22.67 \Rightarrow v=4.76\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 3

A 12 kg crate accelerates from 2 m/s to 5 m/s. Find the net work done.

Solution

\(K_1=\dfrac12(12)(4)=24\text{ J}\). \(K_2=\dfrac12(12)(25)=150\text{ J}\). \(W_{net}=150-24=126\text{ J}\).

Practice 4

A 6 kg object decelerates from 8 m/s to 3 m/s. Find the net work done.

Solution

\(K_1=\dfrac12(6)(64)=192\text{ J}\). \(K_2=\dfrac12(6)(9)=27\text{ J}\). \(W_{net}=27-192=-165\text{ J}\).

Practice 5

A calculation gives a NEGATIVE final kinetic energy for some scenario. What does this physically mean?

Solution

It's physically impossible, KE can never be negative. It means the object actually reaches zero velocity (stops) before the full amount of work is delivered, the scenario as stated exceeds the object's available kinetic energy, so the true stopping point must be recalculated up to \(K=0\).

Practice 6

A 4 kg object at rest has 50 J of applied work done on it, while friction does \(-18\text{ J}\) simultaneously. Find the final KE and speed.

Solution

Net work \(=50-18=32\text{ J}=K_2\). \(v^2=2(32)/4=16 \Rightarrow v=4\text{ m/s}\).

Mental model. $V_g=mgh$ (datum choice is free). Dropping height converts PE to KE if no losses.

Method. Pick a datum, write $T_1+V_1=T_2+V_2$ when only conservative forces do work.

Watch for. Inconsistent datum between positions 1 and 2 is a silent error.

Height stores energy. Raise a mass and you bank \(mgh\) joules; let it fall and that bank converts into kinetic energy. Because gravity is a conservative force, the work it does depends only on the change in height, never on the path taken, a straight drop and a winding frictionless ramp give the same final speed from the same height.

Drop or slide from height \(h\): \(mgh\) converts to \(\tfrac12 mv^2\). Path shape does not matter if there is no friction.

Key idea

$$U_g=mgh, \qquad K_1+U_1=K_2+U_2 \text{ (no friction or other losses)}.$$

Worked example 1

A 2 kg ball is dropped from rest at height 10 m. Find its speed just before hitting the ground (ignore air resistance).

\(0+mgh=\dfrac12mv^2+0 \Rightarrow v=\sqrt{2gh}=\sqrt{2(9.8)(10)}=\sqrt{196}=14\text{ m/s}\).

Worked example 2

A 5 kg cart starts from rest atop a frictionless 8 m ramp. Find its speed at the bottom.

\(v=\sqrt{2(9.8)(8)}=\sqrt{156.8}=12.52\text{ m/s}\) (the mass cancelled out, as always for this formula).

Practice 7

A 3 kg ball is dropped from 20 m. Find its speed at the ground.

Solution

\(v=\sqrt{2(9.8)(20)}=\sqrt{392}=19.8\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 8

A cart on a frictionless 5 m ramp starts from rest. Find its speed at the bottom.

Solution

\(v=\sqrt{2(9.8)(5)}=\sqrt{98}=9.9\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 9

A 1 kg ball is thrown upward, reaching a max height of 4 m. Find its initial speed.

Solution

All KE converts to PE at the top: \(v=\sqrt{2(9.8)(4)}=\sqrt{78.4}=8.85\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 10

A frictionless roller coaster car starts from rest at the top of a 30 m hill. Find its speed at the bottom.

Solution

\(v=\sqrt{2(9.8)(30)}=\sqrt{588}=24.25\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 11

Explain why the mass cancels out of \(v=\sqrt{2gh}\), why a bowling ball and a tennis ball dropped from the same height hit the ground at the same speed (ignoring air resistance).

Solution

Both \(K=\dfrac12mv^2\) and \(U_g=mgh\) contain mass as a simple linear factor. Setting them equal and solving for \(v\) cancels \(m\) from both sides entirely, final speed depends only on \(g\) and \(h\), never on how heavy the object is.

Practice 12

A 2 kg object slides down a frictionless curved ramp of height 6 m (any shape). Find its speed at the bottom, and explain why the ramp's shape doesn't matter.

Solution

\(v=\sqrt{2(9.8)(6)}=\sqrt{117.6}=10.84\text{ m/s}\). The shape doesn't matter because energy conservation depends only on the start and end heights, not the path between them, as long as there's no friction or other energy loss.

Spring in the energy balance.

Mental model. Elastic PE $V_s=\tfrac12 k x^2$ measured from the unstretched length.

Method. Compute $x$ from geometry at each position; include $\Delta V_s$ in conservation.

Watch for. $x$ is deformation from unstretched, not from an arbitrary starting pose unless that pose is unstretched.

A stretched or compressed spring stores energy exactly like height does. The stored amount is \(\tfrac12 kx^2\), which grows with the square of the deflection, so doubling the compression stores four times the energy. Once released (frictionless), that bank converts cleanly into kinetic energy, or into gravitational PE if the block then climbs.

Spring launch on a frictionless surface: stored elastic energy becomes kinetic energy.

Key idea

$$U_s=\frac12kx^2, \qquad K_1+U_{s1}=K_2+U_{s2} \text{ (add } U_g \text{ if height also changes).}$$

Worked example 1

A spring, \(k=200\text{ N/m}\), compressed 0.15 m, launches a 0.5 kg block on a frictionless surface. Find its launch speed.

\(U_s=\dfrac12(200)(0.15)^2=2.25\text{ J}\), all converts to KE: \(2.25=\dfrac12(0.5)v^2 \Rightarrow v=3\text{ m/s}\).

Worked example 2

A spring, \(k=500\text{ N/m}\), compressed 0.2 m, launches a 1 kg block up a frictionless 20° incline. How far up the incline does it travel before stopping?

\(U_s=\dfrac12(500)(0.2)^2=10\text{ J}\), all converts to \(U_g=mgh\), \(h=d\sin20^\circ\):

\(10=1(9.8)(d)(0.342)=3.352d \Rightarrow d=2.984\text{ m}\).

Practice 13

A spring \(k=150\text{ N/m}\), compressed 0.1 m, launches a 0.3 kg block (frictionless). Find the launch speed.

Solution

\(U_s=\dfrac12(150)(0.01)=0.75\text{ J}\). \(v^2=2(0.75)/0.3=5 \Rightarrow v=2.236\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 14

A spring \(k=300\text{ N/m}\), compressed 0.25 m. Find the stored PE.

Solution

\(U_s=\dfrac12(300)(0.0625)=9.375\text{ J}\).

Practice 15

A 2 kg block moving at 4 m/s compresses a spring (\(k=800\text{ N/m}\), frictionless surface) until it momentarily stops. Find the maximum compression.

Solution

\(K_1=\dfrac12(2)(16)=16\text{ J}=U_s=\dfrac12(800)x^2 \Rightarrow x^2=0.04 \Rightarrow x=0.2\text{ m}\).

Practice 16

A spring \(k=400\text{ N/m}\), compressed 0.18 m, launches a 0.8 kg block up a frictionless 15° incline. Find the distance travelled before stopping.

Solution

\(U_s=\dfrac12(400)(0.0324)=6.48\text{ J}\). \(6.48=0.8(9.8)(d)(\sin15^\circ)=2.029d \Rightarrow d=3.194\text{ m}\).

Practice 17

Explain why, in a frictionless spring-launch problem, the block's final speed doesn't depend on the spring's natural length or mounting.

Solution

\(U_s=\dfrac12kx^2\) only depends on the spring constant and the compression distance from natural length, and energy conservation only cares about total stored energy, not the spring's absolute length or orientation, those details never enter the balance.

Practice 18

A spring launches a block horizontally, which then travels up a frictionless ramp. Explain why you can find the final height without knowing the intermediate speed.

Solution

Mechanical energy is conserved end-to-end, so spring PE converts directly to gravitational PE at the final point regardless of the path, you can go straight from \(U_{s1}\) to \(U_{g2}\) without ever needing the intermediate velocity as a separate step.

Mental model. Move gravity and springs into PE; leave friction (and other non-conservative work) on the work side.

Method. Write $T_1+V_1+U_{\rm nc}=T_2+V_2$ with a clear list of $U_{\rm nc}$ terms.

Watch for. Do not double-count: if a force is in PE, do not also put its work in $U$.

When both a spring and gravity act, track every energy form present at each state and set the totals equal. The spring may be "empty" by the end while height has grown, or vice versa, list \(K\), \(U_s\), and \(U_g\) at both ends so nothing is missed.

Key idea

$$K_1+U_{s1}+U_{g1}=K_2+U_{s2}+U_{g2}.$$

Worked example

A 2 kg block starts at rest against a spring (\(k=600\text{ N/m}\), compressed 0.3 m) at the bottom of a frictionless 25° incline. Find its speed after travelling 1.5 m up the incline (spring already released).

State 1: \(K_1=0\), \(U_{s1}=\dfrac12(600)(0.3)^2=27\text{ J}\), \(U_{g1}=0\).

State 2: \(U_{s2}=0\) (spring released). Height gained \(h=1.5\sin25^\circ=0.6339\text{ m}\), \(U_{g2}=2(9.8)(0.6339)=12.42\text{ J}\).

\(27=K_2+12.42 \Rightarrow K_2=14.58\text{ J} \Rightarrow v=\sqrt{2(14.58)/2}=3.818\text{ m/s}\).

Combined problem: spring PE at the bottom converts into a mix of KE and gravitational PE further up the incline.

Practice 19

A 1.5 kg block, spring \(k=500\text{ N/m}\) compressed 0.25 m, frictionless 20° incline. Find its speed after 1 m up the incline.

Solution

\(U_{s1}=\dfrac12(500)(0.0625)=15.625\text{ J}\). \(h=1\sin20^\circ=0.342\text{ m}\), \(U_{g2}=1.5(9.8)(0.342)=5.027\text{ J}\). \(K_2=15.625-5.027=10.598\text{ J}\). \(v=\sqrt{2(10.598)/1.5}=3.759\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 20

A 3 kg block, spring \(k=900\text{ N/m}\) compressed 0.2 m, frictionless 30° incline. Find its speed after 0.8 m.

Solution

\(U_{s1}=\dfrac12(900)(0.04)=18\text{ J}\). \(h=0.8\sin30^\circ=0.4\text{ m}\), \(U_{g2}=3(9.8)(0.4)=11.76\text{ J}\). \(K_2=18-11.76=6.24\text{ J}\). \(v=\sqrt{2(6.24)/3}=2.04\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 21

Using Practice 19's numbers, find the MAXIMUM distance the block travels up the incline before stopping.

Solution

At max distance, \(K_2=0\): \(15.625=1.5(9.8)(d)(\sin20^\circ)=5.027d \Rightarrow d=3.108\text{ m}\).

Practice 22

What does a negative calculated \(K_2\) tell you in a spring-launch-up-an-incline problem?

Solution

The assumed distance/height is physically unreachable with the given spring energy, the block runs out of KE (stops) before that point. Instead, solve for the maximum distance/height where \(K_2=0\).

Practice 23

A 2 kg block on a frictionless horizontal surface is pushed by a spring (\(k=400\text{ N/m}\), compression 0.15 m) and then travels up a frictionless ramp. Find the maximum height it reaches.

Solution

\(U_{s1}=\dfrac12(400)(0.0225)=4.5\text{ J}=mgh \Rightarrow h=4.5/(2\times9.8)=0.2296\text{ m}\).

Practice 24

Explain why a consistent reference point (height = 0) for gravitational PE matters, and how choosing a different reference point would (or wouldn't) change the final speed.

Solution

The reference height is arbitrary, only changes in height matter physically. As long as the same reference is used consistently for both the initial and final states, the calculated speed is identical regardless of which height is called zero, since only the difference in \(U_g\) enters the energy balance, not its absolute value.

Practice 25

A 7 kg crate moves at 5 m/s across a rough floor. Over 6 m, a motor does +300 J work while friction does \(-120\text{ J}\). Find the final speed and state whether the crate is accelerating overall.

Solution

Initial KE: \(K_1=\dfrac12(7)(5^2)=87.5\text{ J}\). Net work: \(W_{net}=300-120=180\text{ J}\).

\(K_2=K_1+W_{net}=87.5+180=267.5\text{ J}\). Then \(267.5=\dfrac12(7)v^2\Rightarrow v^2=76.43\Rightarrow v=8.74\text{ m/s}\).

Because net work is positive, KE increases, so the crate is accelerating overall.

Practice 26

A 2.5 kg slider enters a braking pad at 9 m/s and stops after 3.2 m. Assuming constant braking force, find the work done by the pad and the average braking force magnitude.

Solution

\(K_1=\dfrac12(2.5)(9^2)=101.25\text{ J}\), \(K_2=0\). So \(W_{net}=K_2-K_1=-101.25\text{ J}\).

Only the pad does work here, so \(W_{pad}=-101.25\text{ J}\). Using \(W=F\,d\cos180^\circ=-Fd\): \(F=101.25/3.2=31.64\text{ N}\).

Final answer: work \(=-101.25\text{ J}\), average braking force magnitude \(=31.6\text{ N}\).

Practice 27

A 4 kg block is pushed up a ramp by an external actuator. Between points A and B, height increases by 1.8 m and speed rises from 2 m/s to 4.5 m/s. Friction dissipates 35 J. Find the actuator work input.

Solution

Change in KE: \(\Delta K=\dfrac12(4)(4.5^2-2^2)=2(20.25-4)=32.5\text{ J}\).

Change in gravitational PE: \(\Delta U_g=mgh=4(9.8)(1.8)=70.56\text{ J}\).

Energy balance with loss: \(W_{actuator}=\Delta K+\Delta U_g+35=32.5+70.56+35=138.06\text{ J}\).

Required actuator work is \(\boxed{138\text{ J} \text{ (approx.)}}\).

Practice 28

A 1.2 kg puck starts from rest and is acted on by a variable net force \(F(x)=18-3x\) N along a straight path from \(x=0\) to \(x=4\) m. Find its speed at \(x=4\) m.

Solution

Net work is area under \(F\)-\(x\): \(W=\int_0^4(18-3x)\,dx=[18x-1.5x^2]_0^4=72-24=48\text{ J}\).

From rest, \(K_2=48\text{ J}\). So \(48=\dfrac12(1.2)v^2=0.6v^2\Rightarrow v^2=80\Rightarrow v=8.94\text{ m/s}\).

Final speed: \(\boxed{8.94\text{ m/s}}\).

Practice 29

A 9 kg cart at 6 m/s climbs a short frictionless rise and reaches a point 1.1 m higher. Find its speed there and the minimum initial speed needed to just reach that point.

Solution

Use \(K_1+U_{g1}=K_2+U_{g2}\). Take \(U_{g1}=0\): \(K_1=\dfrac12(9)(36)=162\text{ J}\), \(U_{g2}=9(9.8)(1.1)=97.02\text{ J}\).

\(K_2=162-97.02=64.98\text{ J}\Rightarrow v=\sqrt{2K_2/m}=\sqrt{2(64.98)/9}=3.80\text{ m/s}\).

For just reaching the point, set \(K_2=0\): \(\dfrac12mv_{min}^2=mgh\Rightarrow v_{min}=\sqrt{2gh}=\sqrt{2(9.8)(1.1)}=4.64\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 30

A 3 kg block is thrown upward from ground with 12 m/s. At 5 m height, an air-resistance model says 18 J has been dissipated. Find the speed at 5 m and compare with the no-drag prediction.

Solution

\(K_1=\dfrac12(3)(12^2)=216\text{ J}\). \(U_{g2}=3(9.8)(5)=147\text{ J}\). With drag loss 18 J: \(K_2=216-147-18=51\text{ J}\).

\(v=\sqrt{2K_2/m}=\sqrt{102/3}=5.83\text{ m/s}\).

No drag would give \(K_2=69\text{ J}\Rightarrow v=6.78\text{ m/s}\), so drag reduces speed by about \(0.95\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 31

Two frictionless tracks have the same start and finish heights: Track A is steep then flat, Track B is smooth curved. A 1 kg bead starts from rest 2.4 m above the finish on each track. Find the finish speed and explain why path shape does not matter.

Solution

For both tracks, \(mgh=\dfrac12mv^2\) with \(h=2.4\text{ m}\). Hence \(v=\sqrt{2gh}=\sqrt{2(9.8)(2.4)}=6.86\text{ m/s}\).

Same start and finish heights mean the same change in gravitational PE; with no non-conservative losses, final KE is identical regardless of route.

Practice 32

A 0.8 kg cart leaves a hill crest with 7 m/s and then descends 1.6 m while rolling resistance removes 6 J. Find the speed at the lower point.

Solution

\(K_1=\dfrac12(0.8)(7^2)=19.6\text{ J}\). Gain in KE from gravity: \(mgh=0.8(9.8)(1.6)=12.54\text{ J}\).

\(K_2=19.6+12.54-6=26.14\text{ J}\). Then \(v=\sqrt{2K_2/m}=\sqrt{52.28/0.8}=8.08\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 33

A 4 kg sled drops vertically by 1.5 m, then slides on horizontal snow for 4 m with constant friction 9 N. It starts from rest. Find the speed after the 4 m horizontal segment.

Solution

Gravity input: \(mgh=4(9.8)(1.5)=58.8\text{ J}\). Friction loss: \(W_f=-9(4)=-36\text{ J}\).

Net final KE: \(K_2=58.8-36=22.8\text{ J}\). So \(v=\sqrt{2(22.8)/4}=\sqrt{11.4}=3.38\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 34

A roller-coaster car enters a loop bottom at 15 m/s and rises 5.2 m to the top. Neglect losses. Find its speed at the top, then decide whether it reaches the top if entry speed were 10 m/s instead.

Solution

Case 1: \(v_1=15\). \(K_1/m=\dfrac12(15^2)=112.5\). PE rise per kg \(=gh=9.8(5.2)=50.96\).

\(K_2/m=112.5-50.96=61.54\Rightarrow v_2=\sqrt{2(61.54)}=11.09\text{ m/s}\).

Case 2: \(v_1=10\): \(K_1/m=50\), less than 50.96 needed just to gain height, so the car cannot reach the top.

Practice 35

A 6 kg package is lowered 3 m at constant speed by a motorized cable. Find the work done by gravity, work done by the cable, and net work.

Solution

Constant speed means \(\Delta K=0\), so net work is zero.

Gravity does positive work while moving downward: \(W_g=mgd=6(9.8)(3)=176.4\text{ J}\).

Cable must do equal negative work: \(W_{cable}=-176.4\text{ J}\). Net work \(=0\).

Practice 36

A 2 kg block slides down a \(25^\circ\) incline over 3 m with kinetic friction coefficient \(0.18\). It starts from rest. Use energy to find final speed.

Solution

Height drop: \(h=3\sin25^\circ=1.268\text{ m}\), so gravity input \(mgh=2(9.8)(1.268)=24.85\text{ J}\).

\(N=mg\cos25^\circ=17.76\text{ N}\). Friction \(f_k=\mu_kN=3.20\text{ N}\). Friction loss over 3 m: \(9.60\text{ J}\).

\(K_2=24.85-9.60=15.25\text{ J}\Rightarrow v=\sqrt{2(15.25)/2}=3.91\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 37

A spring \(k=450\text{ N/m}\) compressed 0.16 m launches a 1.8 kg block on a frictionless level surface. After leaving the spring it encounters a rough patch where friction is 11 N. How far into the rough patch before it stops?

Solution

Initial spring energy: \(U_s=\dfrac12(450)(0.16^2)=5.76\text{ J}\). This becomes KE before rough patch.

Friction removes energy: \(f d=5.76\Rightarrow d=5.76/11=0.524\text{ m}\).

Stopping distance in rough patch: \(\boxed{0.52\text{ m (approx.)}}\).

Practice 38

A 0.9 kg block compresses a spring (\(k=300\text{ N/m}\)) by 0.22 m and is released up a frictionless \(18^\circ\) incline. Find (a) maximum distance up slope, (b) speed after moving 1.2 m up slope.

Solution

\(U_s=\dfrac12(300)(0.22^2)=7.26\text{ J}\).

(a) At stop point \(K=0\): \(7.26=mg(d\sin18^\circ)\Rightarrow d=\dfrac{7.26}{0.9(9.8)(0.3090)}=2.66\text{ m}\).

(b) At \(d=1.2\): \(U_g=0.9(9.8)(1.2)(0.3090)=3.27\text{ J}\). So \(K=7.26-3.27=3.99\text{ J}\).

\(v=\sqrt{2K/m}=\sqrt{2(3.99)/0.9}=2.98\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 39

A 1.5 kg block arrives at a spring with speed 4 m/s on level ground. The spring constant is \(500\text{ N/m}\), and there is a constant opposing resistance of 6 N during compression. Find maximum compression.

Solution

Initial KE: \(K_1=\dfrac12(1.5)(4^2)=12\text{ J}\).

At max compression \(x\): \(12=\dfrac12(500)x^2+6x\Rightarrow 250x^2+6x-12=0\).

\(x=\dfrac{-6+\sqrt{36+12000}}{500}=\dfrac{-6+109.71}{500}=0.207\text{ m}\) (positive root only).

Practice 40

A spring \(k=650\text{ N/m}\) is compressed 0.12 m and launches a 0.4 kg block vertically upward through a guide. Assume a constant 1.8 N drag force during the upward travel after release. Find maximum height above release point.

Solution

Spring energy: \(U_s=\dfrac12(650)(0.12^2)=4.68\text{ J}\).

At maximum height \(h\), final KE is zero and energy goes into gravity plus drag: \(4.68=(mg+1.8)h=(0.4\cdot9.8+1.8)h=5.72h\).

\(h=4.68/5.72=0.818\text{ m}\).

Practice 41

A 2.2 kg carriage is attached to a spring \(k=280\text{ N/m}\) and pulled to \(x=0.19\) m on a horizontal rail, then released. At \(x=0.05\) m from equilibrium, find its speed (ignore losses).

Solution

Total energy at release: \(E=\dfrac12k(0.19)^2=\dfrac12(280)(0.0361)=5.054\text{ J}\).

Spring energy at \(x=0.05\): \(U_s=\dfrac12(280)(0.05^2)=0.35\text{ J}\). So \(K=E-U_s=4.704\text{ J}\).

\(v=\sqrt{2K/m}=\sqrt{9.408/2.2}=2.07\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 42

A spring launcher sends a 1 kg block up a frictionless ramp. Measured max height is 0.95 m when spring compression is 0.20 m. Determine the spring constant \(k\).

Solution

At top, \(K=0\), so \(\dfrac12k x^2 = mgh\).

\(\dfrac12k(0.20)^2 = 1(9.8)(0.95)=9.31\Rightarrow 0.02k=9.31\Rightarrow k=465.5\text{ N/m}\).

Estimated spring constant: \(\boxed{466\text{ N/m}}\).

Practice 43

A 1.6 kg block is released against a spring \(k=520\text{ N/m}\), compression 0.24 m, at the foot of a \(28^\circ\) incline. Kinetic friction on the incline is \(\mu_k=0.14\). Find the maximum distance up the incline.

Solution

Spring energy: \(U_s=\dfrac12(520)(0.24^2)=14.98\text{ J}\).

Opposing energy per meter along slope: \(mg\sin\theta+\mu_k mg\cos\theta = 1.6(9.8)(\sin28^\circ+0.14\cos28^\circ)\).

This equals \(15.68(0.4695+0.1236)=9.30\text{ J/m}\). At stop: \(14.98=9.30d\Rightarrow d=1.61\text{ m}\).

Practice 44

Using Practice 43 data, find the speed after moving 0.9 m up the incline from release.

Solution

From Practice 43, opposition is \(9.30\text{ J/m}\). Over \(0.9\text{ m}\), opposing energy \(=8.37\text{ J}\).

\(K=U_s-\text{opposition}=14.98-8.37=6.61\text{ J}\).

\(v=\sqrt{2K/m}=\sqrt{13.22/1.6}=2.87\text{ m/s}\).

Practice 45

A 2.4 kg block starts 1.3 m above the floor, then compresses a horizontal spring (\(k=900\text{ N/m}\)). A rough patch before the spring dissipates 7 J. Find maximum spring compression.

Solution

Available energy at spring contact: \(mgh-7=2.4(9.8)(1.3)-7=30.58-7=23.58\text{ J}\).

At max compression: \(\dfrac12kx^2=23.58\Rightarrow 450x^2=23.58\Rightarrow x^2=0.0524\Rightarrow x=0.229\text{ m}\).

Practice 46

A projectile-like slider system has three states: (1) spring compressed, ground level, rest; (2) spring relaxed, moving at ground level; (3) moving up a ramp 0.75 m higher with speed 3 m/s. For \(m=1.4\text{ kg}\) and \(k=400\text{ N/m}\), find required spring compression \(x\), ignoring losses.

Solution

Initial spring energy must equal final \(K+U_g\): \(\dfrac12kx^2=\dfrac12mv^2+mgh\).

Right side \(=\dfrac12(1.4)(3^2)+1.4(9.8)(0.75)=6.3+10.29=16.59\text{ J}\).

\(200x^2=16.59\Rightarrow x^2=0.08295\Rightarrow x=0.288\text{ m}\).

Practice 47

In a spring-plus-gravity problem, you computed \(K_2=-4\text{ J}\) at the target point. Explain the correct exam response in two explicit steps.

Solution

Step 1: State physical interpretation: \(K_2<0\) is impossible, so the object cannot reach the assumed target.

Step 2: Re-solve the limiting case with \(K=0\) at the turning point to find the true maximum reachable distance/height, and report that as the correct endpoint.

Practice 48

A 3 kg block is launched by a spring (\(k=600\text{ N/m}\), \(x=0.20\) m) up an incline. It rises to a point 1.0 m higher than launch and still has speed 2 m/s. Determine the total non-conservative work input and the average driving force if path length is 3.5 m.

Solution

Initial energy: \(E_1=U_s=\dfrac12(600)(0.2^2)=12\text{ J}\).

Final mechanical energy: \(E_2=K_2+U_g=\dfrac12(3)(2^2)+3(9.8)(1.0)=6+29.4=35.4\text{ J}\).

Because \(E_2>E_1\), external work must have been added. Non-conservative net work input is \(W_{nc}=E_2-E_1=+23.4\text{ J}\).

If modeled as a constant drive force along motion, average force is \(F_{avg}=W_{nc}/d=23.4/3.5=6.69\text{ N}\).
