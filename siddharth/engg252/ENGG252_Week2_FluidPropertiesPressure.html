Lay of the land — this block's toolkit

  • Surface tension: $\Delta P=2\sigma_s/R$ (droplet) or $4\sigma_s/R$ (bubble, two surfaces); capillary rise $h=2\sigma_s\cos\phi/(\rho gR)$.

0 Viscosity

Viscosity $\mu$ measures a fluid's internal resistance to shear (its "thickness" or resistance to flow). For a Newtonian fluid (the vast majority of common fluids: water, air, most oils), shear stress is directly proportional to the rate of shear strain (velocity gradient perpendicular to the flow direction): $\tau=\mu\dfrac{du}{dy}$ — Newton's law of viscosity, with units of $\mu$ in Pa$\cdot$s.

Kinematic viscosity $\nu=\mu/\rho$ (m$^2$/s) combines viscosity and density into a single property that appears naturally in the Reynolds number (Week 9) and many other dimensionless groups. Viscosity is strongly temperature-dependent: liquid viscosity decreases with rising temperature (molecules move more freely, weaker cohesive resistance), while gas viscosity increases with rising temperature (more energetic random molecular collisions transfer more momentum) — an important and sometimes counter-intuitive distinction between the two.

Key idea

$$\tau=\mu\frac{du}{dy} \quad \text{(Newton's law of viscosity)} \qquad \nu=\frac{\mu}{\rho} \qquad \mu_{\text{liquid}}\downarrow \text{ with } T\uparrow, \quad \mu_{\text{gas}}\uparrow \text{ with } T\uparrow$$

Worked example

Example. A fluid layer, thickness 2 mm, is sheared between two plates: the top plate moves at 0.5 m/s, the bottom plate is stationary. If $\mu=0.001\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ (approximately water), find the shear stress (assuming a linear velocity profile).

$\dfrac{du}{dy}\approx\dfrac{0.5-0}{0.002}=250\ \text{s}^{-1}$. $\tau=\mu\dfrac{du}{dy}=0.001(250)=0.25\ \text{Pa}$.

Practice 1

A fluid layer of thickness 3 mm is sheared with the top plate moving at 1.2 m/s relative to the stationary bottom plate. If $\mu=0.02\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$, find the shear stress (linear velocity profile).

Solution

$du/dy=1.2/0.003=400\ \text{s}^{-1}$. $\tau=0.02(400)=8$ Pa.

Practice 2

A fluid has $\mu=0.5\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$ and $\rho=850\ \text{kg/m}^3$. Find its kinematic viscosity.

Solution

$\nu=\mu/\rho=0.5/850=5.88\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$.

Practice 3

A shear stress of 15 Pa is measured across a fluid layer with a velocity gradient of $600\ \text{s}^{-1}$. Find the fluid's dynamic viscosity.

Solution

$\mu=\tau/(du/dy)=15/600=0.025\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$.

Practice 4

Two immiscible fluid layers, each 1 mm thick, sit between two plates (top plate moving at 0.8 m/s, bottom stationary). If both layers have the same viscosity, find the velocity at the interface between them, assuming linear velocity profiles in each layer.

Solution

With equal viscosity and equal thickness, shear stress is continuous across the interface and the overall velocity profile is linear across the full 2 mm gap; the interface (at the midpoint) has velocity $0.8/2=0.4$ m/s.

Practice 5

A cylinder of radius 50 mm rotates inside a slightly larger fixed cylindrical housing (radius 51 mm), with oil ($\mu=0.8\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) filling the 1 mm gap. If the inner cylinder surface moves at 2 m/s (tangentially), estimate the shear stress in the oil film (treating the gap as a thin planar layer, linear velocity profile).

Solution

$du/dy\approx2/0.001=2000\ \text{s}^{-1}$. $\tau=0.8(2000)=1600$ Pa.

Practice 6

Explain why motor oil is commonly rated with two viscosity numbers (e.g. "10W-40"), referencing the temperature dependence of liquid viscosity.

Solution

Since liquid viscosity decreases as temperature rises, a single viscosity rating would only describe the oil's behaviour at one specific temperature; the dual rating (e.g. 10W for cold/winter starting performance and 40 for hot/operating-temperature performance) reflects that modern multi-grade oils are formulated to maintain acceptable viscosity across a range of temperatures, addressing the fact that an engine must start reliably when cold (requiring the oil not to be too thick) and also lubricate effectively once hot (requiring the oil not to become too thin).

Practice 7

Explain, at the molecular level, why gas viscosity increases with temperature while liquid viscosity decreases with temperature.

Solution

In a gas, viscosity arises primarily from the random thermal motion of molecules carrying momentum between adjacent layers of flow (faster-moving, hotter molecules transfer momentum more vigorously via more frequent, more energetic collisions), so higher temperature directly increases this momentum-transfer mechanism, increasing viscosity. In a liquid, viscosity arises primarily from cohesive intermolecular forces resisting relative motion between adjacent fluid layers; higher temperature gives molecules more thermal energy to overcome these cohesive bonds more easily, weakening the resistance to relative sliding motion and thus decreasing viscosity — the two fluid states are dominated by fundamentally different physical mechanisms, leading to opposite temperature trends.

1 Surface Tension & Capillarity

Surface tension $\sigma_s$ (N/m) arises because molecules at a liquid's free surface experience an unbalanced inward cohesive pull (no liquid molecules above them to balance the attraction from below and sideways), causing the surface to behave like a stretched elastic membrane, minimising its own surface area. Water at room temperature: $\sigma_s\approx0.0728\ \text{N/m}$.

For a spherical droplet or bubble, surface tension creates an internal pressure excess: $\Delta P=\dfrac{2\sigma_s}{R}$ (droplet, one surface) or $\Delta P=\dfrac{4\sigma_s}{R}$ (soap bubble, two surfaces — inner and outer). Capillary rise/depression in a narrow tube results from the balance between surface tension (acting along the wetted perimeter) and the weight of the raised/depressed liquid column: $h=\dfrac{2\sigma_s\cos\phi}{\rho g R}$, where $\phi$ is the contact angle (water-glass: $\phi\approx0$, mercury-glass: $\phi\approx130\,{}^\circ$, giving capillary depression rather than rise).

Key idea

$$\Delta P_{\text{droplet}}=\frac{2\sigma_s}{R} \qquad \Delta P_{\text{bubble}}=\frac{4\sigma_s}{R} \qquad h_{\text{capillary}}=\frac{2\sigma_s\cos\phi}{\rho gR}$$

Worked example

Example. A water droplet has radius 1 mm. Find the excess internal pressure due to surface tension ($\sigma_s=0.0728\ \text{N/m}$).

$\Delta P=\dfrac{2\sigma_s}{R}=\dfrac{2(0.0728)}{0.001}=145.6\ \text{Pa}$.

Practice 8

A soap bubble has radius 5 mm and surface tension 0.025 N/m. Find the excess internal pressure (remember: two surfaces for a bubble).

Solution

$\Delta P=4\sigma_s/R=4(0.025)/0.005=20$ Pa.

Practice 9

A water droplet has an internal excess pressure of 200 Pa ($\sigma_s=0.0728\ \text{N/m}$). Find its radius.

Solution

$R=2\sigma_s/\Delta P=2(0.0728)/200=7.28\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}=0.728$ mm.

Practice 10

Find the capillary rise of water in a glass tube of radius 0.5 mm ($\sigma_s=0.0728\ \text{N/m}$, $\phi\approx0$, so $\cos\phi\approx1$).

Solution

$h=2\sigma_s\cos\phi/(\rho gR)=2(0.0728)(1)/(1000\times9.81\times0.0005)=0.1456/4.905=0.02969\ \text{m}=29.7$ mm.

Practice 11

Find the capillary depression of mercury in a glass tube of radius 0.5 mm ($\sigma_s=0.51\ \text{N/m}$, $\rho=13600\ \text{kg/m}^3$, $\phi=130\,{}^\circ$, $\cos130\,{}^\circ=-0.643$).

Solution

$h=2(0.51)(-0.643)/(13600\times9.81\times0.0005)=-0.6559/66.71=-0.00983\ \text{m}=-9.83$ mm (negative sign confirms depression, not rise).

Practice 12

Compare the capillary rise of water in two tubes of radius 0.2 mm and 1 mm (same fluid, same $\sigma_s$, $\phi\approx0$), and comment on the trend.

Solution

Since $h\propto1/R$, the narrower tube (0.2 mm) will have 5 times the capillary rise of the wider tube (1 mm) — capillary effects are far more significant in narrower tubes/pores, which is why capillarity is important for small-diameter phenomena (soil moisture, thin wicks, small pressure sensors) but negligible for typical engineering pipe sizes.

Practice 13

Explain why a small water droplet tends toward a spherical shape, using the concept of surface tension minimising surface area for a given volume.

Solution

Surface tension acts to minimise the total surface area of a liquid mass, since maintaining a larger surface area requires more energy to sustain (given the unbalanced inward molecular attraction at the surface). For a fixed volume, a sphere is the geometric shape with the smallest possible surface area, so surface tension naturally pulls a small, freely suspended liquid mass (where gravity's flattening effect is comparatively weak relative to surface tension, i.e. a small droplet) into a near-spherical shape, minimising its surface energy.

Practice 14

Explain, in physical terms, why the excess pressure inside a soap bubble is exactly double that inside a liquid droplet of the same radius and surface tension.

Solution

A liquid droplet has only one liquid-air interface (the outer surface), so surface tension acts across a single surface to balance the internal excess pressure, giving $\Delta P=2\sigma_s/R$. A soap bubble, by contrast, is a thin liquid film with two surfaces — an inner surface (facing the gas trapped inside) and an outer surface (facing the surrounding air) — each contributing its own surface tension effect; since both surfaces act together to resist the same internal pressure, the total effect doubles, giving $\Delta P=4\sigma_s/R$.

2 Vapor Pressure & Cavitation

Vapor pressure $P_v$ is the pressure at which a liquid boils at a given temperature (equivalently: the pressure exerted by a liquid's own vapor when in equilibrium with its liquid phase at that temperature). Vapor pressure increases with temperature — which is exactly why liquids boil at lower temperatures at higher altitude (lower atmospheric pressure): boiling occurs precisely when the liquid's vapor pressure reaches the local ambient pressure.

Cavitation occurs when the local pressure within a flowing liquid drops to (or below) the vapor pressure at the local temperature, causing vapor bubbles to form spontaneously within the liquid itself (not just at a free surface) — typically in low-pressure regions like the suction side of pumps, propeller tips, or narrow flow constrictions (previewing the Bernoulli-equation reasoning of Week 6, where fast flow corresponds to low pressure). These bubbles then violently collapse when they are swept into a higher-pressure region, causing noise, vibration, and severe pitting/erosion damage to nearby solid surfaces — a serious practical concern in pump and turbine design.

Key idea

$$\text{Cavitation occurs when local pressure} \le P_v(T) \qquad P_v \uparrow \text{ as } T\uparrow \qquad \text{Boiling occurs when } P_v=P_{\text{ambient}}$$

Worked example

Example. Water's vapor pressure at 100°C is 101.3 kPa (standard atmospheric pressure). Explain why water boils at exactly 100°C at sea level, but at a lower temperature on a high mountain.

Boiling occurs when $P_v(T)$ reaches the local ambient pressure. At sea level, ambient pressure is 101.3 kPa, which water's vapor pressure reaches exactly at 100°C.

On a high mountain, atmospheric pressure is lower (say, 70 kPa); water's vapor pressure reaches this lower value at a temperature below 100°C, so boiling begins at a correspondingly lower temperature — the well-known effect of reduced boiling temperature at altitude.

Practice 15

Water's vapor pressure at 25°C is about 3.17 kPa. Explain what this means physically.

Solution

At 25°C, water will boil if the surrounding (ambient/local) pressure drops to 3.17 kPa or below; equivalently, an open container of water at 25°C exerts a vapor pressure of 3.17 kPa in the space above it, in equilibrium with its liquid phase.

Practice 16

At sea level (atmospheric pressure 101.3 kPa), water boils at 100°C. On a mountain where atmospheric pressure is 80 kPa, will water boil at a higher or lower temperature than 100°C? Explain briefly.

Solution

Lower temperature, since boiling occurs when vapor pressure equals the local ambient pressure; a lower ambient pressure (80 kPa vs 101.3 kPa) is reached by water's vapor pressure curve at a lower temperature than 100°C.

Practice 17

A pump's suction line has a local pressure that drops to 2.5 kPa at a point where the water temperature is 25°C (vapor pressure at 25°C is 3.17 kPa). Will cavitation occur at this point? Explain.

Solution

Yes, cavitation will occur, since the local pressure (2.5 kPa) has dropped below the vapor pressure at that temperature (3.17 kPa) — vapor bubbles will spontaneously form in the liquid at this location.

Practice 18

Explain why cavitation damage (pitting/erosion) occurs specifically where vapor bubbles COLLAPSE, rather than where they initially form.

Solution

When a vapor bubble forms in a low-pressure region, it is relatively benign (simply a small pocket of vapor within the liquid); the damaging event occurs when that bubble is subsequently swept by the flow into a region of higher pressure (above the vapor pressure), causing it to collapse extremely rapidly and violently. This rapid collapse generates localised, very high-pressure microjets and shock waves directed at any nearby solid surface, which is what actually causes the material pitting and erosion damage — the formation itself is not damaging, but the violent collapse very much is.

Practice 19

Explain why cavitation is a particular concern on the suction (inlet) side of a centrifugal pump, referencing where the lowest pressures in the system typically occur.

Solution

The suction side of a centrifugal pump is where the fluid is being drawn in and accelerated toward the pump impeller, and (by the Bernoulli-type pressure-velocity relationship previewed in Week 6) the local pressure tends to be at its lowest anywhere in the pump/piping system at exactly this location, especially near the impeller eye where flow velocity is highest. If this locally low pressure drops to or below the fluid's vapor pressure at the operating temperature, cavitation will initiate right at the pump inlet/impeller — which is why pump manufacturers specify a minimum required suction pressure (Net Positive Suction Head) to keep the local pressure safely above the vapor pressure throughout the pump's operation.

3 Pressure & Pascal’s Law

Pressure $P=\dfrac{F}{A}$ (Pa) is the normal force per unit area exerted by a fluid on a surface (or, at a point within a fluid, in every direction equally — Pascal's law: pressure at a point in a fluid at rest is the same in all directions). This isotropy is a direct consequence of the fluid's inability to sustain shear stress at rest (Week 1, Section 0): if pressure varied with direction at a point, an unbalanced shear force would exist, which a static fluid cannot support.

Absolute pressure $P_{\text{abs}}$ is measured relative to a perfect vacuum (zero pressure); gauge pressure $P_{\text{gauge}}=P_{\text{abs}}-P_{\text{atm}}$ is measured relative to local atmospheric pressure (what most everyday pressure gauges, e.g. a tyre gauge, actually display) — always be clear which one a problem or instrument is referring to, since confusing the two is one of the most common sources of error in fluid statics problems (Week 3–4).

Key idea

$$P=\frac{F}{A} \qquad \text{Pascal’s law: pressure at a point in a static fluid is equal in all directions} \qquad P_{\text{abs}}=P_{\text{gauge}}+P_{\text{atm}}$$

Worked example

Example. A tyre gauge reads 220 kPa. If atmospheric pressure is 101.3 kPa, find the absolute pressure inside the tyre.

Tyre gauges always read gauge pressure. $P_{\text{abs}}=P_{\text{gauge}}+P_{\text{atm}}=220+101.3=321.3\ \text{kPa}$.

Practice 20

A force of 500 N acts uniformly over an area of 0.02 m$^2$. Find the pressure.

Solution

$P=500/0.02=25000\ \text{Pa}=25$ kPa.

Practice 21

A hydraulic system requires a pressure of 8 MPa to lift a load, applied over a piston of area 0.005 m$^2$. Find the required force.

Solution

$F=PA=8\times10^6(0.005)=40000\ \text{N}=40$ kN.

Practice 22

A pressure gauge reads $-30$ kPa (i.e. a vacuum gauge reading, below atmospheric). If atmospheric pressure is 101.3 kPa, find the absolute pressure.

Solution

$P_{\text{abs}}=P_{\text{gauge}}+P_{\text{atm}}=-30+101.3=71.3\ \text{kPa}$.

Practice 23

An absolute pressure of 250 kPa is measured. If atmospheric pressure is 100 kPa, find the equivalent gauge pressure.

Solution

$P_{\text{gauge}}=P_{\text{abs}}-P_{\text{atm}}=250-100=150\ \text{kPa}$.

Practice 24

Explain why Pascal's law (equal pressure in all directions at a point) is a direct consequence of a fluid's inability to sustain shear stress at rest, using a small triangular fluid element free body argument (conceptually, no full derivation needed).

Solution

Consider a small wedge-shaped fluid element at rest; if the pressure acting on its differently-oriented faces were unequal, the resulting net force imbalance would have a component tangential (shear-like) to at least one face, which a static fluid (by definition, unable to sustain shear stress at rest) cannot resist without deforming/flowing. Since the fluid is in static equilibrium (not flowing), no such unbalanced tangential force can exist, forcing the pressure to be identical in every direction at that point — Pascal's law follows directly and necessarily from the basic definition of a fluid at rest.

Practice 25

Explain the underlying principle of a hydraulic jack (or hydraulic car lift), using Pascal's law and the relationship $P=F/A$ for two connected pistons of different area.

Solution

Pascal's law guarantees that pressure applied at one point in a connected, static (or slow-moving) fluid is transmitted equally throughout the fluid to every other point. A hydraulic jack applies a modest force $F_1$ over a small piston area $A_1$, creating pressure $P=F_1/A_1$; this same pressure acts on a much larger piston of area $A_2$ elsewhere in the connected fluid system, producing a much larger output force $F_2=PA_2=F_1(A_2/A_1)$ — the area ratio provides mechanical advantage, allowing a small applied force to lift a much heavier load, which is exactly the working principle of hydraulic jacks, presses, and car lifts.
