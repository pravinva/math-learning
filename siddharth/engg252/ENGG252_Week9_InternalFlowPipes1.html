0 Laminar vs Turbulent Flow; the Reynolds Number

Internal (pipe) flow exists in two fundamentally different regimes. Laminar flow is smooth, orderly, with fluid moving in well-defined parallel layers (laminae) with no macroscopic mixing between them — occurs at low velocities/small pipe diameters/high viscosity. Turbulent flow is chaotic, with continuous three-dimensional eddying and mixing — occurs at high velocities/large diameters/low viscosity, and is actually the more common regime in most practical engineering pipe flows (water and air systems).

The Reynolds number $Re=\dfrac{\rho VD}{\mu}=\dfrac{VD}{\nu}$ (dimensionless, previewing the dimensional analysis of Week 12) is the ratio of inertial forces to viscous forces, and determines which regime occurs: for pipe flow, $Re<2300$: laminar; $2300<Re<4000$: transitional (unpredictable, avoided in careful design); $Re>4000$: turbulent. This single dimensionless number is one of the most important and frequently used quantities in the entire subject.

Key idea

$$Re=\frac{\rho VD}{\mu}=\frac{VD}{\nu} \qquad Re<2300:\text{ laminar} \qquad 2300<Re<4000:\text{ transitional} \qquad Re>4000:\text{ turbulent}$$

Worked example

Example. Water ($\rho=1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$, $\mu=0.001\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) flows through a 50 mm diameter pipe at 2 m/s. Find the Reynolds number and classify the flow.

$Re=\dfrac{\rho VD}{\mu}=\dfrac{1000(2)(0.050)}{0.001}=100{,}000$.

$Re=100{,}000\gg4000$ — turbulent flow (as is typical for water flowing at ordinary velocities through ordinary-sized pipes, confirming turbulent flow is indeed the more common practical case).

Practice 1

Water flows through a 25 mm diameter pipe at 0.5 m/s ($\rho=1000\ \text{kg/m}^3,\mu=0.001\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$). Find $Re$ and classify.

Solution

$Re=1000(0.5)(0.025)/0.001=12{,}500$ — turbulent.

Practice 2

A viscous oil ($\rho=880\ \text{kg/m}^3,\mu=0.5\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) flows through a 40 mm diameter pipe at 0.3 m/s. Find $Re$ and classify.

Solution

$Re=880(0.3)(0.040)/0.5=21.1$ — laminar (well below 2300).

Practice 3

Find the maximum velocity at which water ($\rho=1000\ \text{kg/m}^3,\mu=0.001\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) can flow through a 20 mm diameter pipe and remain laminar ($Re=2300$).

Solution

$V=Re\,\mu/(\rho D)=2300(0.001)/(1000\times0.020)=0.115\ \text{m/s}$ — a very low velocity, illustrating why laminar flow of water in ordinary-sized pipes is rare in practice.

Practice 4

A fluid with kinematic viscosity $\nu=1\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2/\text{s}$ flows through a 100 mm pipe at 5 m/s. Find $Re$ and classify.

Solution

$Re=VD/\nu=5(0.100)/(1\times10^{-5})=50{,}000$ — turbulent.

Practice 5

Two pipes carry the same fluid at the same velocity, but pipe A has twice the diameter of pipe B. Compare their Reynolds numbers.

Solution

Since $Re\propto D$ for fixed fluid and velocity, pipe A (larger diameter) has exactly twice the Reynolds number of pipe B.

Practice 6

Explain, at a physical level, why increasing the pipe diameter (with everything else held constant) tends to promote turbulence, using the concept of the ratio of inertial to viscous forces.

Solution

The Reynolds number represents the ratio of inertial forces (associated with the fluid's momentum and tendency to keep moving/mixing chaotically) to viscous forces (associated with the fluid's internal friction, which tends to smooth out and damp any disturbances, keeping flow orderly/laminar). Increasing pipe diameter increases $Re$ directly (since $Re\propto D$), meaning inertial effects become progressively more dominant relative to the viscous damping effects; with less relative viscous damping to suppress small disturbances, they are more likely to grow and cascade into full turbulent, chaotic motion rather than being smoothed out into orderly laminar layers.

Practice 7

Explain why the transitional Reynolds number range (roughly 2300-4000) is generally avoided in careful engineering pipe design, rather than being treated as simply an intermediate, predictable flow regime.

Solution

In the transitional range, the flow can intermittently and unpredictably switch between laminar and turbulent behaviour (or exhibit an unstable mixture of both), depending sensitively on factors like minor disturbances, pipe roughness, or entrance conditions that are difficult to fully control or predict in practice. This unpredictability makes it very difficult to reliably calculate friction losses, pressure drops, or other design quantities with confidence in this range, so engineers generally design pipe systems to operate clearly within either the laminar or (far more commonly) the turbulent regime, avoiding the ambiguous transitional zone wherever practically possible.

1 Entrance Region & Fully Developed Flow

When fluid enters a pipe (typically with a nearly uniform velocity profile), the no-slip condition (Week 6) forces the velocity near the wall to zero, and a boundary layer grows inward from the wall. The entrance region is the initial length of pipe over which this boundary layer grows until it fills the entire cross-section; beyond this point, the velocity profile no longer changes shape along the pipe — the flow becomes fully developed.

The hydrodynamic entrance length $L_h$ (distance to reach fully developed flow) differs substantially between regimes: for laminar flow, $L_h\approx0.05\,Re\,D$ (can be very long, since $Re$ can still be sizeable even in the laminar regime); for turbulent flow, $L_h\approx10D$ to $60D$ (a much shorter, roughly constant multiple of diameter, since turbulent mixing rapidly spreads the wall's influence across the whole cross-section). Nearly all standard pipe-flow friction-loss formulas (Section 2 onward, and Week 10) assume fully developed flow — a key underlying assumption worth remembering.

Key idea

$$L_{h,\text{laminar}}\approx0.05\,Re\,D \qquad L_{h,\text{turbulent}}\approx10D\text{ to }60D \qquad \text{Fully developed: velocity profile shape no longer changes with } x$$

Worked example

Example. Water flows through a 30 mm diameter pipe at $Re=1500$ (laminar). Find the entrance length.

$L_h\approx0.05\,Re\,D=0.05(1500)(0.030)=2.25\ \text{m}$ — a significant length relative to the small pipe diameter (75 pipe diameters), illustrating why laminar entrance effects can be a real practical concern in shorter pipe systems.

Practice 8

Find the laminar entrance length for a 20 mm diameter pipe at $Re=2000$.

Solution

$L_h=0.05(2000)(0.020)=2.0$ m.

Practice 9

Find the turbulent entrance length range (using both the 10D and 60D estimates) for a 100 mm diameter pipe.

Solution

$L_h\approx10(0.100)=1.0\ \text{m}$ to $60(0.100)=6.0\ \text{m}$.

Practice 10

A pipe system has a total length of 3 m and diameter 25 mm, with the flow being turbulent ($Re=50{,}000$). Using the entrance length estimate of $10D$ to $60D$, comment on whether the flow is likely fully developed for most of the pipe's length.

Solution

$10D=0.25\ \text{m}$ to $60D=1.5\ \text{m}$ — the entrance region occupies somewhere between about 8% and 50% of the total 3 m pipe length, meaning a significant portion of a short pipe like this may NOT be fully developed, and standard fully-developed friction-loss formulas may be less accurate for such a short system (a caution worth remembering, since most textbook formulas implicitly assume conditions well beyond the entrance region).

Practice 11

Compare the entrance length for the SAME pipe (diameter 40 mm) carrying the same fluid at $Re=2000$ (laminar) versus $Re=20{,}000$ (turbulent, using the $10D$ estimate), and comment on which regime develops fully sooner.

Solution

Laminar: $L_h=0.05(2000)(0.040)=4.0$ m. Turbulent: $L_h\approx10(0.040)=0.4$ m. The turbulent case develops fully MUCH sooner (a tenth of the laminar entrance length here), despite having a much higher Reynolds number — turbulent mixing very rapidly spreads the wall's influence across the whole pipe cross-section, in sharp contrast to the comparatively slow, diffusive spreading of viscous effects in laminar flow.

Practice 12

Explain, physically, why the laminar entrance length INCREASES with Reynolds number ($L_h\propto Re$), while the turbulent entrance length is comparatively INSENSITIVE to Reynolds number (staying within a roughly constant $10D$-$60D$ range regardless of exactly how large $Re$ is).

Solution

In laminar flow, momentum spreads inward from the wall purely by molecular viscous diffusion, a comparatively slow process; a higher Reynolds number (higher velocity relative to viscosity) means fluid travels farther downstream before this slow diffusive spreading has had enough time to reach the pipe centreline, directly explaining why $L_h$ scales with $Re$. In turbulent flow, by contrast, momentum is spread almost instantaneously (compared to the flow's own travel time) by vigorous, large-scale turbulent eddies/mixing, a process that is far less sensitive to the precise value of viscosity or velocity individually; this fundamentally different (and much faster) mixing mechanism is why the turbulent entrance length remains roughly constant (in terms of pipe diameters) regardless of the specific (turbulent-range) Reynolds number.

2 Laminar Flow in Pipes — the Hagen–Poiseuille Equation

For fully developed laminar pipe flow, exact analytical solutions are possible (unlike turbulent flow, which requires empirical correlations, Week 10). The velocity profile is exactly parabolic: $u(r)=u_{\max}\left[1-\left(\dfrac rR\right)^2\right]$, with average velocity exactly half the centreline maximum: $V_{\text{avg}}=\dfrac{u_{\max}}2$ (used already in Week 6, Section 1).

The pressure drop over length $L$ is given by the Hagen–Poiseuille equation: $\Delta P=\dfrac{32\mu LV_{\text{avg}}}{D^2}$ — an exact result, not an empirical correlation, directly derivable from a force balance on a cylindrical fluid element. Equivalently, in terms of head loss: $h_L=\dfrac{32\mu LV_{\text{avg}}}{\rho gD^2}$. Note the very strong dependence on diameter ($1/D^2$, and combined with the $V_{\text{avg}}\propto Q/D^2$ relationship, laminar head loss actually scales as $1/D^4$) — halving a pipe's diameter increases the laminar pressure drop, for the same flow rate, by a factor of 16.

Key idea

$$u(r)=u_{\max}\left[1-\left(\frac rR\right)^2\right] \qquad V_{\text{avg}}=\frac{u_{\max}}2 \qquad \Delta P=\frac{32\mu LV_{\text{avg}}}{D^2} \quad \text{(Hagen–Poiseuille, laminar flow)}$$

Worked example

Example. Oil ($\mu=0.4\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) flows laminarly through a 20 mm diameter pipe, length 5 m, at $V_{\text{avg}}=0.2$ m/s. Find the pressure drop.

$\Delta P=\dfrac{32\mu LV_{\text{avg}}}{D^2}=\dfrac{32(0.4)(5)(0.2)}{(0.020)^2}=\dfrac{12.8}{4\times10^{-4}}=32{,}000\ \text{Pa}=32$ kPa.

Practice 13

Oil ($\mu=0.3\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) flows laminarly through a 15 mm diameter pipe, length 3 m, at $V_{\text{avg}}=0.15$ m/s. Find the pressure drop.

Solution

$\Delta P=32(0.3)(3)(0.15)/(0.015)^2=4.32/2.25\times10^{-4}=19{,}200\ \text{Pa}=19.2$ kPa.

Practice 14

A laminar pipe flow has centreline velocity $u_{\max}=0.8$ m/s. Find the average velocity.

Solution

$V_{\text{avg}}=u_{\max}/2=0.4\ \text{m/s}$.

Practice 15

A laminar flow has $V_{\text{avg}}=0.5$ m/s. Find the centreline (maximum) velocity, and the velocity at $r=0.5R$ (half the pipe radius) using the parabolic profile.

Solution

$u_{\max}=2V_{\text{avg}}=1.0\ \text{m/s}$. $u(0.5R)=u_{\max}[1-(0.5)^2]=1.0(1-0.25)=0.75\ \text{m/s}$.

Practice 16

For a given laminar flow rate and pipe length, if the pipe diameter is reduced to half its original value, find the factor by which the pressure drop increases (using the $\Delta P\propto1/D^4$ relationship for FIXED flow rate $Q$, not fixed velocity).

Solution

Since $V_{\text{avg}}=Q/A\propto Q/D^2$, and $\Delta P\propto V_{\text{avg}}/D^2\propto(Q/D^2)/D^2=Q/D^4$: halving $D$ (for fixed $Q$) increases $\Delta P$ by a factor of $2^4=16$.

Practice 17

Find the required pressure drop to maintain a laminar flow rate of $2\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$ of oil ($\mu=0.35\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) through a 12 mm diameter, 4 m long pipe (first find $V_{\text{avg}}$ from $Q$, then apply Hagen-Poiseuille).

Solution

$A=\pi(0.006)^2=1.131\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $V_{\text{avg}}=Q/A=2\times10^{-4}/1.131\times10^{-4}=1.768\ \text{m/s}$. $\Delta P=32(0.35)(4)(1.768)/(0.012)^2=79.2/1.44\times10^{-4}=550{,}000\ \text{Pa}=550$ kPa.

Practice 18

Explain why the Hagen-Poiseuille equation is an EXACT analytical result (derivable directly from a force balance), while the corresponding turbulent-flow pressure-drop relationship (Week 10) must instead rely on empirical correlations and charts.

Solution

Laminar flow has a well-defined, smooth, mathematically tractable velocity profile (the parabolic profile), governed by a comparatively simple balance between the driving pressure gradient and viscous shear stress within the fluid — this balance can be solved exactly using standard calculus/differential equations, yielding the precise Hagen-Poiseuille result. Turbulent flow, by contrast, involves inherently chaotic, three-dimensional, time-varying eddying motion that has never been (and, current understanding suggests, likely cannot be) solved exactly from first principles for practical engineering purposes; friction losses in turbulent flow must instead be characterised empirically (from extensive experimental data, condensed into correlations like the Colebrook equation or the Moody chart, Week 10) rather than derived from a clean analytical force balance.

3 Wall Shear Stress & the Friction Factor

The wall shear stress $\tau_w$ (the viscous shear stress the fluid exerts on the pipe wall, and vice versa by Newton's third law) is directly related to the pressure drop driving the flow, via a simple force balance on the fluid within the pipe: $\tau_w=\dfrac{\Delta P\,D}{4L}$ (valid for any fully developed pipe flow, laminar or turbulent, since it follows from equilibrium alone, not from any assumption about the specific velocity profile shape).

The (Darcy) friction factor $f$ is a dimensionless quantity relating head loss to velocity and geometry: $h_L=f\dfrac{L}{D}\dfrac{V_{\text{avg}}^2}{2g}$ (the Darcy–Weisbach equation, the single most important and universal head-loss formula in pipe flow, valid for both laminar and turbulent flow, and the central equation of Week 10). For laminar flow specifically, comparing this to the Hagen–Poiseuille result (Section 2) gives an exact, simple formula: $f=\dfrac{64}{Re}$ — laminar friction factor depends only on Reynolds number, decreasing smoothly as $Re$ increases (right up to the transition point, where the behaviour changes entirely for turbulent flow, Week 10).

Key idea

$$\tau_w=\frac{\Delta P\,D}{4L} \qquad h_L=f\frac{L}{D}\frac{V_{\text{avg}}^2}{2g} \quad \text{(Darcy–Weisbach)} \qquad f_{\text{laminar}}=\frac{64}{Re}$$

Worked example

Example. Laminar flow in a 25 mm diameter pipe has $Re=1800$. Find the friction factor, then the head loss over a 10 m length at $V_{\text{avg}}=0.3$ m/s.

$f=64/Re=64/1800=0.03556$.

$h_L=f\dfrac{L}{D}\dfrac{V_{\text{avg}}^2}{2g}=0.03556\times\dfrac{10}{0.025}\times\dfrac{0.3^2}{2(9.81)}=0.03556(400)(0.004587)=0.06527\ \text{m}$.

Practice 19

Find the friction factor for laminar flow at $Re=1200$.

Solution

$f=64/1200=0.0533$.

Practice 20

A pipe (diameter 40 mm) has a pressure drop of 5 kPa over a length of 8 m. Find the wall shear stress.

Solution

$\tau_w=\Delta P\,D/(4L)=5000(0.040)/(4\times8)=200/32=6.25\ \text{Pa}$.

Practice 21

Find the head loss for laminar flow ($Re=2000$, so $f=64/2000=0.032$) through a 15 mm diameter pipe, length 6 m, at $V_{\text{avg}}=0.4$ m/s.

Solution

$h_L=0.032\times(6/0.015)\times(0.4^2/19.62)=0.032(400)(0.008155)=0.1044$ m.

Practice 22

A pipe carries laminar flow with wall shear stress 4 Pa, diameter 30 mm, length 5 m. Find the pressure drop.

Solution

$\Delta P=4\tau_wL/D=4(4)(5)/0.030=2666.7\ \text{Pa}=2.67$ kPa.

Practice 23

Verify, by direct substitution, that the Darcy-Weisbach equation with $f=64/Re$ reproduces the Hagen-Poiseuille pressure-drop formula, using $Re=\rho VD/\mu$ and $h_L=\Delta P/(\rho g)$.

Solution

$h_L=f\dfrac LD\dfrac{V^2}{2g}=\dfrac{64}{Re}\cdot\dfrac LD\cdot\dfrac{V^2}{2g}=\dfrac{64\mu}{\rho VD}\cdot\dfrac LD\cdot\dfrac{V^2}{2g}=\dfrac{32\mu LV}{\rho gD^2}$. Multiplying by $\rho g$: $\Delta P=\rho gh_L=\dfrac{32\mu LV}{D^2}$ — exactly the Hagen-Poiseuille formula from Section 2. ✓

Practice 24

Explain why the wall shear stress formula $\tau_w=\Delta P\,D/(4L)$ is valid for BOTH laminar and turbulent flow, while the specific relationship $f=64/Re$ is valid ONLY for laminar flow.

Solution

The wall shear stress formula follows purely from a force balance (equilibrium) on the fluid within a section of pipe: the net pressure force pushing the fluid forward must exactly balance the total wall friction force resisting it, regardless of whether the internal flow details are laminar or turbulent — this is a general equilibrium argument, independent of the specific flow regime or velocity profile shape. The relationship $f=64/Re$, by contrast, was derived specifically by combining the Darcy-Weisbach definition with the exact Hagen-Poiseuille laminar-flow solution (Section 2), which itself relies on the specific parabolic velocity profile that only occurs in laminar flow; turbulent flow has a fundamentally different (flatter, more complex, empirically-characterised) velocity profile, so the friction factor for turbulent flow follows entirely different empirical relationships (developed fully in Week 10), not this simple $64/Re$ formula.
