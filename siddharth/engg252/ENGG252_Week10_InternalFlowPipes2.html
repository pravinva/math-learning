0 Turbulent Flow in Pipes

Turbulent pipe flow has a much flatter (more uniform) velocity profile across most of the pipe cross-section compared to laminar flow's smooth parabola (Week 9), with a very thin region near the wall (the viscous sublayer) where velocity drops rapidly to zero. This flatter profile occurs because turbulent eddies continuously mix high-momentum fluid from the core toward the wall region, in stark contrast to laminar flow's purely molecular (much slower) momentum diffusion.

Unlike laminar flow, turbulent flow has no exact analytical solution for the velocity profile or friction factor (Week 9, Section 3) — both must instead be characterised using extensive experimental data, condensed into empirical correlations. The turbulent friction factor $f$ depends on both Reynolds number and the pipe's relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$ (where $\varepsilon$ is the average height of surface roughness protrusions), unlike the laminar case where $f=64/Re$ depends on $Re$ alone.

Key idea

$$\text{Turbulent: flat core profile, thin viscous sublayer near wall} \qquad f_{\text{turbulent}}=f(Re,\ \varepsilon/D) \quad \text{(empirical, no exact formula)}$$

Worked example

Example. Explain, conceptually, why a turbulent velocity profile is much "flatter" across most of the pipe than the laminar parabolic profile, even though both must still satisfy the no-slip condition at the wall.

In laminar flow, momentum is transferred purely by slow molecular diffusion (viscosity acting directly between adjacent fluid layers), producing a smooth, gradually-varying parabolic profile from the wall to the centreline.

In turbulent flow, vigorous, large-scale eddies continuously and rapidly mix fluid (and its momentum) across most of the pipe's cross-section, keeping the bulk of the flow at a nearly uniform velocity; only very close to the wall (where the no-slip condition still applies, and turbulent eddies are suppressed by the wall's presence) does the velocity drop sharply through a thin viscous sublayer — producing the characteristic "flat core, sharp near-wall drop" turbulent profile shape, quite different from laminar flow's smooth parabola.

Practice 1

Explain why the ratio $V_{\text{avg}}/u_{\max}$ is closer to 1 for turbulent flow (typically around 0.8) than for laminar flow (exactly 0.5), referencing the flatness of each profile.

Solution

Since turbulent flow has a much flatter (more uniform) velocity profile across most of the cross-section, with velocity closer to the centreline maximum value over a much larger fraction of the pipe area (only dropping sharply very near the wall), the area-averaged velocity is much closer to the maximum (centreline) value than in laminar flow's smoothly-varying parabola, where the average is exactly half the maximum by the shape of the parabola itself.

Practice 2

Explain the role of the viscous sublayer in turbulent pipe flow, and why it remains laminar-like in character even though the bulk of the flow is turbulent.

Solution

The viscous sublayer is an extremely thin region immediately adjacent to the pipe wall where the no-slip condition forces velocity to drop to zero; within this very thin layer, the local velocity gradients (and hence local Reynolds-number-like ratios based on the sublayer's own small length scale) are such that viscous forces genuinely dominate over the (locally suppressed, by the wall's presence) turbulent eddying motion, so the flow behaves in a smooth, laminar-like manner within this thin region, even while the bulk core flow farther from the wall is fully turbulent and chaotic.

Practice 3

Explain why the turbulent friction factor depends on relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$, while the laminar friction factor ($f=64/Re$, Week 9) does not depend on roughness at all.

Solution

In laminar flow, the (comparatively thick) smoothly-varying velocity profile is barely affected by small-scale wall roughness protrusions, since the viscous, orderly flow simply flows smoothly around or over small surface irregularities without significant disruption — laminar friction is governed almost entirely by the bulk viscous shear throughout the flow, not by fine wall details. In turbulent flow, by contrast, the thin viscous sublayer near the wall can be genuinely disrupted by wall roughness protrusions that are comparable in size to (or larger than) that thin sublayer's own thickness; roughness elements that poke through or disturb this sublayer directly increase turbulent mixing and drag near the wall, meaning the actual physical roughness height (relative to the pipe diameter) becomes a genuinely important factor in determining the overall friction factor for turbulent flow.

Practice 4

A pipe carries turbulent flow. Explain qualitatively (without calculation) what would happen to the friction factor if the SAME pipe were made from a much rougher material (larger $\varepsilon$), while keeping the Reynolds number unchanged.

Solution

Increasing the wall roughness (for a fixed Reynolds number, i.e. fixed flow velocity and fluid properties) increases the relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$, which generally INCREASES the turbulent friction factor $f$ — rougher walls disturb the viscous sublayer more significantly and generate more turbulent mixing and drag near the wall, resulting in a higher friction factor (and correspondingly higher head loss for the same flow conditions) compared to an otherwise identical smooth pipe.

1 The Moody Chart & the Colebrook Equation

The Moody chart is the standard graphical tool for finding the turbulent friction factor $f$, plotting $f$ against $Re$ for a family of curves, each representing a different relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$ — condensing decades of experimental pipe-flow data into a single, universally-used engineering chart. The underlying empirical formula behind the Moody chart is the (implicit) Colebrook equation: $$\dfrac1{\sqrt f}=-2.0\log_{10}\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7}+\dfrac{2.51}{Re\sqrt f}\right)$$

The Colebrook equation is implicit in $f$ (appears on both sides), requiring iterative solution or a chart/calculator; the widely-used Haaland equation gives an explicit, direct approximation (typically within about 2% of Colebrook) avoiding iteration: $$\dfrac1{\sqrt f}\approx-1.8\log_{10}\left[\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7}\right)^{1.11}+\dfrac{6.9}{Re}\right]$$ For fully smooth pipes ($\varepsilon\approx0$), both simplify since the roughness term vanishes, and $f$ depends on $Re$ alone.

Key idea

$$\frac1{\sqrt f}=-2.0\log_{10}\left(\frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7}+\frac{2.51}{Re\sqrt f}\right) \ \text{(Colebrook, implicit)} \qquad \frac1{\sqrt f}\approx-1.8\log_{10}\left[\left(\frac{\varepsilon/D}{3.7}\right)^{1.11}+\frac{6.9}{Re}\right] \ \text{(Haaland, explicit)}$$

Worked example

Example. Water flows through a commercial steel pipe ($\varepsilon=0.045$ mm), diameter 100 mm, at $Re=200{,}000$. Find $\varepsilon/D$, then estimate $f$ using the Haaland equation.

$\varepsilon/D=0.045/100=4.5\times10^{-4}$.

$\dfrac1{\sqrt f}\approx-1.8\log_{10}\left[\left(\dfrac{4.5\times10^{-4}}{3.7}\right)^{1.11}+\dfrac{6.9}{200{,}000}\right]=-1.8\log_{10}\left[3.13\times10^{-5}+3.45\times10^{-5}\right]=-1.8\log_{10}(6.58\times10^{-5})=-1.8(-4.182)=7.527$. $f=(1/7.527)^2=0.01765$.

Practice 5

Find the relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$ for a cast iron pipe ($\varepsilon=0.26$ mm), diameter 150 mm.

Solution

$\varepsilon/D=0.26/150=1.733\times10^{-3}$.

Practice 6

A smooth pipe ($\varepsilon\approx0$) has turbulent flow at $Re=50{,}000$. Using the Haaland equation (roughness term vanishes), estimate $f$.

Solution

$1/\sqrt f=-1.8\log_{10}(6.9/50000)=-1.8\log_{10}(1.38\times10^{-4})=-1.8(-3.86)=6.949$. $f=(1/6.949)^2=0.0207$.

Practice 7

Explain why the Colebrook equation is described as "implicit" in the friction factor $f$, and briefly describe how an engineer would typically solve it by hand (before spreadsheet/software tools became standard).

Solution

The friction factor $f$ appears on both sides of the Colebrook equation (directly as $1/\sqrt f$ on the left, and also inside the logarithm on the right via the $2.51/(Re\sqrt f)$ term); this means $f$ cannot simply be isolated algebraically by rearranging the equation. Traditionally, engineers solved it by ITERATION: guess an initial value of $f$ (e.g. from the explicit Haaland approximation, or an initial estimate), substitute it into the right-hand side to compute a new $f$, and repeat this process until the value of $f$ converges (stops changing significantly between successive iterations) — a straightforward but tedious hand-calculation process that the explicit Haaland approximation was specifically developed to avoid.

Practice 8

Explain why, for turbulent flow at a very high Reynolds number (the "fully rough" regime), the friction factor becomes essentially independent of Reynolds number, depending only on relative roughness (a limiting behaviour visible on the Moody chart as nearly horizontal curves at high $Re$).

Solution

At very high Reynolds number, the viscous sublayer near the wall becomes extremely thin (since higher velocity/inertia increasingly suppresses and thins this near-wall viscous-dominated region); once the sublayer becomes thinner than the physical roughness protrusions themselves, those roughness elements poke directly through the (now negligibly thin) viscous sublayer and interact directly with the fully turbulent core flow. In this "fully rough" regime, the friction is dominated entirely by form drag on the roughness elements themselves (a mechanism essentially independent of the fluid's viscosity, and hence independent of Reynolds number), which is why the Moody chart curves flatten out to become nearly horizontal (constant $f$ for a given $\varepsilon/D$) at sufficiently high Reynolds numbers.

Practice 9

A pipe's relative roughness is estimated as $\varepsilon/D=0.001$. Using the Haaland equation at $Re=100{,}000$, estimate $f$.

Solution

$1/\sqrt f=-1.8\log_{10}[(0.001/3.7)^{1.11}+6.9/100000]=-1.8\log_{10}[7.02\times10^{-5}+6.9\times10^{-5}]=-1.8\log_{10}(1.392\times10^{-4})=-1.8(-3.857)=6.942$. $f=(1/6.942)^2=0.02075$.

2 Minor Losses

Beyond the distributed friction loss along straight pipe sections ("major" losses, Sections 0–1), real piping systems also lose mechanical energy at fittings, valves, bends, entrances, and exits — so-called minor losses (a somewhat misleading name, since they are sometimes larger than the major/friction losses, especially in short pipe runs with many fittings). Each minor loss is characterised by a dimensionless loss coefficient $K_L$: $h_{L,\text{minor}}=K_L\dfrac{V^2}{2g}$, with typical published values e.g. $K_L\approx0.5$ (sharp-edged entrance), $K_L\approx1.0$ (sudden exit into a large reservoir), $K_L\approx0.3$–$0.9$ (90° elbow, depending on type), $K_L\approx0.2$ (fully open gate valve).

Total head loss in a piping system combines both types: $h_{L,\text{total}}=h_{L,\text{major}}+h_{L,\text{minor}}=f\dfrac LD\dfrac{V^2}{2g}+\sum K_L\dfrac{V^2}{2g}$ — this total (fed directly into the general energy equation, Week 7) is the complete quantity needed for any real pump-sizing or system-design calculation.

Key idea

$$h_{L,\text{minor}}=K_L\frac{V^2}{2g} \qquad h_{L,\text{total}}=f\frac LD\frac{V^2}{2g}+\sum K_L\frac{V^2}{2g}$$

Worked example

Example. A pipe system (diameter 80 mm, $f=0.02$, length 40 m) has a sharp-edged entrance ($K_L=0.5$), two $90\,{}^\circ$ elbows ($K_L=0.9$ each), and a fully open gate valve ($K_L=0.2$), with $V=2$ m/s. Find the total head loss.

Velocity head $=V^2/2g=4/19.62=0.2039$ m. Major loss: $h_{L,\text{major}}=f(L/D)(V^2/2g)=0.02(40/0.080)(0.2039)=0.02(500)(0.2039)=2.039$ m.

Minor loss: $\sum K_L=0.5+0.9+0.9+0.2=2.5$. $h_{L,\text{minor}}=2.5(0.2039)=0.5098$ m. Total: $h_{L,\text{total}}=2.039+0.510=2.549$ m.

Practice 10

A pipe entrance has $K_L=0.5$ and the flow velocity is 1.8 m/s. Find the minor head loss.

Solution

$h_L=0.5(1.8)^2/19.62=0.5(3.24)/19.62=0.0826$ m.

Practice 11

A pipe exit into a large tank has $K_L=1.0$, velocity 2.5 m/s. Find the minor head loss.

Solution

$h_L=1.0(2.5)^2/19.62=6.25/19.62=0.3186$ m.

Practice 12

A piping system has three fittings with $K_L$ values 0.3, 0.9, and 0.2, at a velocity of 1.5 m/s. Find the total minor head loss (summing all three coefficients first).

Solution

$\sum K_L=0.3+0.9+0.2=1.4$. $h_L=1.4(1.5)^2/19.62=1.4(2.25)/19.62=0.1606$ m.

Practice 13

A pipe system has major loss $h_{L,\text{major}}=1.8$ m and minor loss $h_{L,\text{minor}}=0.6$ m. Find the total head loss, and the percentage contributed by minor losses.

Solution

$h_{L,\text{total}}=1.8+0.6=2.4$ m. Minor loss percentage $=0.6/2.4=25\%$.

Practice 14

Explain why minor losses can sometimes EXCEED major (friction) losses in total magnitude, despite being called "minor," using the example of a short pipe run with many fittings.

Solution

Major (friction) losses scale with pipe length $L$ (via the $L/D$ term in the Darcy-Weisbach equation), so a SHORT pipe run inherently has a small major loss regardless of how many fittings it contains. Minor losses, by contrast, depend only on the number and type of fittings present, not on the pipe's length at all; a short pipe section connecting many valves, elbows, and other fittings in quick succession can accumulate a large total $\sum K_L$ even though the pipe itself is short, meaning the minor losses can genuinely dominate the total head loss in such systems — the "minor" label reflects the physical origin of the loss (localised fittings vs distributed friction), not a guarantee about its relative magnitude in every system.

Practice 15

A designer is choosing between a sharp-edged pipe entrance ($K_L=0.5$) and a smoothly rounded entrance ($K_L\approx0.04$, a typical value for a well-rounded entrance). Explain, in physical terms, why the rounded entrance has a much lower loss coefficient.

Solution

A sharp-edged entrance causes the flow to separate abruptly from the pipe wall just inside the entrance, forming a recirculating, turbulent separation region (a "vena contracta") that dissipates significant mechanical energy as the flow subsequently has to re-expand and reattach to the pipe walls farther downstream. A smoothly rounded entrance, by contrast, guides the flow gradually and gently into the pipe, allowing it to follow the wall's curvature without abrupt separation, dramatically reducing the turbulent mixing and associated energy dissipation — which is exactly why rounding pipe entrances (even modestly) is a simple, effective, low-cost way to reduce minor losses in a piping system.

3 Piping Networks: Series & Parallel Pipes

For pipes connected in series (one continuous flow path through multiple pipe segments of possibly different diameter/roughness), the same flow rate $Q$ passes through every segment (continuity, Week 6), and the total head loss is the sum of each segment's individual head loss: $Q_1=Q_2=\dots=Q$, $h_{L,\text{total}}=h_{L,1}+h_{L,2}+\dots$ — directly analogous to resistors in series in an electrical circuit.

For pipes connected in parallel (multiple alternative flow paths sharing the same start and end points), the head loss across each parallel branch must be equal (since they share the same start and end pressure/elevation), while the total flow rate splits among the branches: $h_{L,1}=h_{L,2}=\dots$, $Q_{\text{total}}=Q_1+Q_2+\dots$ — directly analogous to resistors in parallel. Since each branch's head loss depends on its own flow rate (via $f,K_L,V$), parallel pipe problems typically require iterative or simultaneous solution.

Key idea

$$\text{Series: } Q_1=Q_2=Q,\ h_{L,\text{total}}=h_{L,1}+h_{L,2} \qquad \text{Parallel: } h_{L,1}=h_{L,2},\ Q_{\text{total}}=Q_1+Q_2$$

Worked example

Example. Two pipes are connected in series, with head losses $h_{L,1}=2.5$ m and $h_{L,2}=1.8$ m at the (necessarily common) flow rate through both. Find the total head loss for the series system.

$h_{L,\text{total}}=h_{L,1}+h_{L,2}=2.5+1.8=4.3\ \text{m}$ (simply added, since the same flow rate passes through both segments in turn, and each contributes its own resistance along the single path).

Practice 16

Three pipe segments in series have head losses 1.2 m, 0.8 m, and 1.5 m (all at the common series flow rate). Find the total head loss.

Solution

$h_{L,\text{total}}=1.2+0.8+1.5=3.5$ m.

Practice 17

Two parallel pipe branches share the same total flow rate of 0.08 m$^3$/s. If branch 1 carries 0.05 m$^3$/s, find the flow rate in branch 2.

Solution

$Q_2=Q_{\text{total}}-Q_1=0.08-0.05=0.03\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$.

Practice 18

Two parallel pipe branches have head losses that must be equal. If branch 1's head loss is currently calculated as 3 m at its assumed flow rate, but branch 2's head loss (at ITS assumed flow rate) comes out to 5 m, explain what this mismatch indicates and what must be done to correctly solve the problem.

Solution

Since parallel branches must have EQUAL head loss (they share the same start and end points, hence the same available pressure/elevation difference to drive flow through either path), a mismatch (3 m vs 5 m) indicates the initially assumed flow-rate split between the two branches was incorrect. The correct solution requires adjusting the assumed flow rates in each branch (typically by iteration, or by solving the governing equations simultaneously) until the head loss calculated independently for each branch converges to the same value — only then does the flow-rate split represent the true, physically consistent solution.

Practice 19

Explain the general strategy (in words, no full calculation needed) for solving a parallel-pipe network problem where the total flow rate is known but the split between two branches is unknown.

Solution

Since head loss must be equal in both branches, and each branch's head loss depends on its own (initially unknown) flow rate through the Darcy-Weisbach and minor-loss formulas, the general strategy is: (1) assume a trial flow-rate split between the branches (that sums to the known total); (2) calculate the head loss in each branch using its assumed flow rate; (3) compare the two head losses — if they don't match, adjust the flow-rate split (sending relatively more flow to whichever branch currently has the lower head loss, since a higher flow rate would increase its head loss and help bring the two into balance); (4) repeat until both branches' head losses converge to the same value, at which point the true flow-rate split has been found.

Practice 20

Explain the direct analogy between series/parallel PIPE systems and series/parallel ELECTRICAL RESISTOR circuits, identifying which fluid-mechanics quantity plays the role of electrical current, and which plays the role of voltage/resistance.

Solution

Flow rate $Q$ plays the role of electrical current (the "through" quantity that must be conserved at any junction, and that adds up across parallel branches while remaining equal through series elements). Head loss $h_L$ plays the role of voltage drop (the "across" quantity that adds up along a series path, while being equal across parallel branches connecting the same two points) — and the general relationship between head loss and flow rate (via $f,L,D,K_L$) plays a role directly analogous to electrical resistance relating voltage drop to current, making the entire pipe-network analysis technique structurally identical to solving series/parallel electrical resistor circuits, just with different physical quantities and a nonlinear (rather than linear) relationship between the "current" and "voltage drop" analogues.
