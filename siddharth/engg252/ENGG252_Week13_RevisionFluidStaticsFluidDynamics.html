0 Review: Fluid Statics

Fluid statics (Weeks 1–4) rests on one core idea: a static fluid cannot sustain shear, so pressure varies only with depth ($P=P_0+\rho gh$) and acts equally in all directions at a point (Pascal's law). From this single equation flow all the practical results: manometry (trace the fluid path, add $\rho gh$ down, subtract going up), Archimedes' principle ($F_B=\rho_{\text{fluid}}gV_{\text{displaced}}$) with metacentric stability, and forces on submerged surfaces — $F_R=\rho gh_CA$ for plane surfaces (centre of pressure below the centroid), resolved into horizontal/vertical components for curved surfaces.

Also carried through from Weeks 1–2: dimensional homogeneity as a standing sanity check, density/specific weight/specific gravity, viscosity and Newton's law of viscosity ($\tau=\mu\,du/dy$), and vapor pressure/cavitation — all foundational properties that reappear constantly in the dynamics weeks that follow.

Key idea

$$P=P_0+\rho gh \qquad F_B=\rho_{\text{fluid}}gV_{\text{displaced}} \qquad F_R=\rho gh_CA \qquad \tau=\mu\frac{du}{dy}$$

Worked example

Example (mixed statics review). A vertical rectangular gate (width 2 m, height 4 m, top edge at the water surface) is hinged at its base. Find the resultant force and the depth of the centre of pressure.

$h_C=4/2=2$ m. $A=2(4)=8\ \text{m}^2$. $F_R=\rho gh_CA=1000(9.81)(2)(8)=156{,}960\ \text{N}=156.96$ kN.

Top-at-surface shortcut: $y_R=\dfrac23h=\dfrac23(4)=2.667\ \text{m}$ — combining the Week 4 force and centre-of-pressure results in a single problem, exactly the kind of integrated question a revision/exam setting favours.

Practice 1

A cube of side 0.4 m, density 750 kg/m$^3$, floats in water. Find the depth submerged.

Solution

$SG=0.75$, so 75% of the height is submerged (floating equilibrium shortcut): $0.75(0.4)=0.30$ m.

Practice 2

A mercury manometer ($SG=13.6$) shows a column height difference of 300 mm connected to a gas tank. Find the gauge pressure in the tank.

Solution

$P=\rho_{Hg}gh=13600(9.81)(0.300)=40{,}021\ \text{Pa}=40.0$ kPa.

Practice 3

A fluid layer 4 mm thick is sheared with the top plate moving at 0.6 m/s; $\mu=0.015\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$. Find the shear stress.

Solution

$\tau=\mu(du/dy)=0.015(0.6/0.004)=0.015(150)=2.25$ Pa.

Practice 4

A curved gate (quarter circle, radius 1.5 m, width 2 m) holds back water with the water on the concave side, top of the curve at the free surface. Find $F_H$ and $F_V$.

Solution

$F_H$: vertical projection is a rectangle 2 m × 1.5 m, centroid depth 0.75 m. $F_H=1000(9.81)(0.75)(2\times1.5)=22{,}073\ \text{N}=22.07$ kN. $F_V$: weight of the quarter-circle water volume: $V=\frac{\pi(1.5)^2}{4}(2)=3.534\ \text{m}^3$. $F_V=1000(9.81)(3.534)=34{,}670\ \text{N}=34.67$ kN.

Practice 5

Summarise, in a single sentence each, the core physical idea behind (a) the hydrostatic equation, (b) Archimedes' principle, and (c) the concept of centre of pressure.

Solution

(a) A static fluid cannot sustain shear, so a vertical force balance on a fluid element shows pressure depends only on depth. (b) The net upward pressure force on a submerged body equals the weight of the fluid it displaces, since the pressure difference between the body's top and bottom surfaces directly produces that buoyant force. (c) Because pressure increases with depth, the resultant hydrostatic force on a submerged surface acts at a point below the geometric centroid, where the deeper (higher-pressure) regions of the surface are effectively weighted more heavily.

1 Review: Continuity, Bernoulli & the Energy Equation

Fluid dynamics begins with two companion equations (Weeks 5–7): continuity ($A_1V_1=A_2V_2$ for incompressible flow — a purely geometric/kinematic bookkeeping statement) and Bernoulli's equation ($P+\frac12\rho V^2+\rho gz=\text{constant}$, valid only for steady, incompressible, frictionless flow along a streamline). The standard problem-solving pattern throughout is: apply Bernoulli between two points, use continuity to eliminate one velocity unknown, then solve.

When friction, pumps, or turbines are present, Bernoulli generalises to the energy equation: $\dfrac{P_1}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1+h_{\text{pump}}=\dfrac{P_2}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_{\text{turbine}}+h_L$ — the workhorse of virtually every real pipe-system and pump/turbine-sizing problem, feeding into the power chain $h\to\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=\rho gQh\to\dot W_{\text{shaft}}$ (via efficiency).

Key idea

$$A_1V_1=A_2V_2 \qquad P+\frac12\rho V^2+\rho gz=\text{const.} \qquad \frac{P_1}{\rho g}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1+h_{\text{pump}}=\frac{P_2}{\rho g}+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_{\text{turbine}}+h_L$$

Worked example

Example (mixed dynamics review). Water is pumped from a lower reservoir ($z_1=0$) to an upper tank ($z_2=18$ m), both atmospheric, negligible velocity, through a pipe with total head loss 2.5 m. Volume flow rate 0.06 m$^3$/s, pump efficiency 76%. Find the required motor shaft power.

Energy equation: $h_{\text{pump}}=z_2-z_1+h_L=18+2.5=20.5$ m.

$\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=\rho gQh_{\text{pump}}=1000(9.81)(0.06)(20.5)=12{,}066\ \text{W}=12.07$ kW. $\dot W_{\text{shaft}}=12.07/0.76=15.9\ \text{kW}$.

Practice 6

Water flows through a horizontal pipe narrowing from 120 mm to 60 mm diameter, $V_1=1.2$ m/s, $P_1=180$ kPa. Find $V_2$ and $P_2$.

Solution

$V_2=V_1(120/60)^2=1.2(4)=4.8\ \text{m/s}$. $P_2=180000+\frac12(1000)(1.44-23.04)=180000-10800=169{,}200\ \text{Pa}=169.2$ kPa.

Practice 7

A large tank has water 3.5 m deep above a small base outlet. Find the exit jet velocity (Torricelli).

Solution

$V=\sqrt{2(9.81)(3.5)}=8.28\ \text{m/s}$.

Practice 8

A turbine extracts a head of 12 m from water flowing at 0.4 m$^3$/s, with turbine efficiency 88%. Find the fluid power and the shaft power output.

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=1000(9.81)(0.4)(12)=47{,}088\ \text{W}=47.09$ kW. $\dot W_{\text{shaft}}=0.88(47.09)=41.4$ kW.

Practice 9

Explain, in one or two sentences, the KEY difference between when you can use Bernoulli's equation directly versus when you must use the general energy equation instead.

Solution

Bernoulli's equation applies only when the flow is steady, incompressible, and frictionless, with no pump or turbine present between the two points; as soon as any pump, turbine, or non-negligible friction loss is present along the path (essentially every real engineering pipe system), the general energy equation (which explicitly adds pump head, subtracts turbine head, and subtracts head loss) must be used instead.

Practice 10

A student sets up a Bernoulli equation between the free surface of a large tank and the outlet of a pump located partway down a long, narrow pipe. Explain what is wrong with this approach.

Solution

This setup violates two of Bernoulli's requirements simultaneously: a pump is present between the two chosen points (adding mechanical energy, which Bernoulli cannot account for), and a long, narrow pipe will have non-negligible friction losses (also not accounted for by Bernoulli). The correct approach is to use the general energy equation instead, explicitly including both the pump head term $h_{\text{pump}}$ and the head loss term $h_L$ between the two points.

2 Review: The Momentum Equation

The momentum equation (Week 8) is fluid mechanics' version of Newton's second law: $\sum\vec F=\sum_{\text{out}}\dot m\vec V-\sum_{\text{in}}\dot m\vec V$ — a vector equation, always solved component-by-component, where $\sum\vec F$ includes pressure forces, weight, and the unknown reaction force from any solid surface in contact with the fluid.

Two families of application recur: enclosed geometries (pipe bends, nozzles), where pressure forces at the inlet/outlet must be explicitly included alongside the unknown reaction force; and free jets on vanes (open to atmosphere), where pressure cancels and $F_x=\dot mV(1-\cos\theta)$ applies directly, maximised at $\theta=180\,{}^\circ$. For moving vanes, always use the relative velocity $V_r=V_{\text{jet}}-V_{\text{vane}}$.

Key idea

$$\sum\vec F=\sum_{\text{out}}\dot m\vec V-\sum_{\text{in}}\dot m\vec V \qquad F_x\text{ (free jet on vane)}=\dot mV(1-\cos\theta)$$

Worked example

Example (mixed momentum review). A water jet, $\dot m=7$ kg/s, $V=22$ m/s, strikes a stationary curved vane and is deflected through $120\,{}^\circ$ (frictionless, speed unchanged). Find the force on the vane in the original jet direction.

$F_x=\dot mV(1-\cos120\,{}^\circ)=7(22)(1-(-0.5))=154(1.5)=231\ \text{N}$.

Practice 11

Water flows through a nozzle, $\dot m=12$ kg/s, entering at 3 m/s and leaving at 15 m/s (straight nozzle, no direction change). Find the force needed to produce this momentum change.

Solution

$F=\dot m(V_{\text{out}}-V_{\text{in}})=12(15-3)=144$ N.

Practice 12

A jet, $\dot m=6$ kg/s, $V=18$ m/s, strikes a vane fully reversing it (180°). Find the force on the vane.

Solution

$F_x=\dot mV(1-\cos180\,{}^\circ)=6(18)(2)=216$ N.

Practice 13

A jet with $V_{\text{jet}}=28$ m/s strikes a single vane moving in the same direction at 9 m/s. Find the relative velocity used in the force calculation.

Solution

$V_r=28-9=19\ \text{m/s}$.

Practice 14

Explain why the force on a pipe bend calculation (Week 8, Section 1) requires explicitly including pressure forces at the inlet and outlet, while the free-jet-on-a-vane calculation (Week 8, Section 2) does not.

Solution

In an enclosed pipe bend, the fluid is under genuine internal gauge pressure needed to drive it through the pipe system, and this pressure (and the resulting pressure force $PA$ at each cross-section) generally differs between inlet and outlet, especially if the pipe diameter changes; these nonzero forces must be included in the momentum balance. A free jet exposed to the atmosphere throughout has only atmospheric (zero gauge) pressure acting on it at every point along its path, so the pressure-force terms are identically zero and drop out of the momentum equation entirely.

Practice 15

Summarise, in one sentence, why the momentum equation must always be solved as a VECTOR equation (component by component), unlike the (scalar) continuity and energy equations.

Solution

Force and momentum both have direction as well as magnitude, so correctly capturing effects like a fluid being deflected around a bend or fully reversed by a vane — where the momentum CHANGE depends critically on the angle through which the velocity vector turns, not just on speed — requires resolving the equation into perpendicular components (typically $x$ and $y$), whereas continuity (a statement about scalar mass/volume flow rate) and the energy equation (a statement about scalar energy) have no inherent directionality to track.

3 Review: Pipe Flow & Dimensional Analysis

Internal flow (Weeks 9–11) hinges on the Reynolds number $Re=\rho VD/\mu$ (laminar $Re<2300$, turbulent $Re>4000$), which determines everything downstream: for laminar flow, the exact Hagen–Poiseuille result ($f=64/Re$, parabolic profile, $V_{\text{avg}}=u_{\max}/2$); for turbulent flow, the empirical Colebrook/Haaland/Moody-chart friction factor (depending on both $Re$ and relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$). Both regimes share the same universal head-loss formula, the Darcy–Weisbach equation $h_L=f(L/D)(V^2/2g)$, to which minor losses ($K_LV^2/2g$) are added for a complete system head-loss calculation, and flow-measurement devices (Venturi/orifice meters, Pitot tubes, weirs) apply Bernoulli/continuity in specialised, calibrated forms.

Dimensional analysis (Week 12) sits conceptually above all of this: the Buckingham Pi theorem explains WHY relationships like $f$ vs $Re$ (at fixed $\varepsilon/D$) exist as clean, low-dimensional empirical curves in the first place — reducing what would otherwise be an intractable multi-variable experimental problem down to a manageable one- or two-dimensionless-group relationship, and underpinning the similarity criteria used to scale laboratory models up to full-size prototypes.

Key idea

$$Re=\frac{\rho VD}{\mu} \qquad h_L=f\frac LD\frac{V^2}{2g}+\sum K_L\frac{V^2}{2g} \qquad n-k=\text{number of dimensionless groups (Buckingham Pi)}$$

Worked example

Example (mixed pipe-flow/dimensional-analysis review). Water flows through a 60 mm diameter pipe at 1.8 m/s ($\rho=1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$, $\mu=0.001\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$). Classify the flow, and (if turbulent, smooth pipe) estimate $f$ via Haaland.

$Re=1000(1.8)(0.060)/0.001=108{,}000$ — turbulent (well above 4000).

Smooth pipe Haaland: $1/\sqrt f=-1.8\log_{10}(6.9/108000)=-1.8\log_{10}(6.389\times10^{-5})=-1.8(-4.195)=7.55\Rightarrow f=(1/7.55)^2=0.01754$ — a typical turbulent smooth-pipe friction factor, ready to feed directly into the Darcy-Weisbach head-loss calculation.

Practice 16

Oil ($\rho=880\ \text{kg/m}^3,\mu=0.6\ \text{Pa}\cdot\text{s}$) flows through a 25 mm pipe at 0.2 m/s. Classify the flow.

Solution

$Re=880(0.2)(0.025)/0.6=7.33$ — laminar.

Practice 17

For the laminar flow in the previous question, find the friction factor and, over a 5 m length, the head loss.

Solution

$f=64/7.33=8.73$. $h_L=f(L/D)(V^2/2g)=8.73(5/0.025)(0.04/19.62)=8.73(200)(0.002039)=3.560$ m (laminar flows at very low $Re$ can have surprisingly large friction factors and head losses, since $f=64/Re$ grows rapidly as $Re$ shrinks).

Practice 18

A pipe system has major head loss 2.2 m and three fittings with $K_L=0.5,0.9,0.3$ at $V=2$ m/s. Find the total head loss.

Solution

$\sum K_L=1.7$. $h_{L,\text{minor}}=1.7(4)/19.62=0.3466$ m. $h_{L,\text{total}}=2.2+0.347=2.547$ m.

Practice 19

A problem involves 5 dimensional variables and 3 fundamental dimensions. How many dimensionless groups does the Buckingham Pi theorem predict, and name the two groups that typically arise in a pipe-friction problem of this size.

Solution

$5-3=2$ groups — typically the friction factor $f$ (or an equivalent pressure-coefficient form) and the Reynolds number $Re$, exactly the two variables plotted against each other on the Moody chart (Week 10).

Practice 20

Summarise, in a short paragraph, how the Reynolds number acts as the connecting thread across Weeks 9-12: name at least three distinct places it appears or determines behaviour.

Solution

The Reynolds number first appears in Week 9 to classify laminar vs turbulent pipe flow (determining which friction-factor formula applies at all); it reappears in Week 10 as one axis of the Moody chart, governing the turbulent friction factor together with relative roughness; and it reappears again in Week 12 as one of the fundamental named dimensionless groups (alongside Froude, Weber, Euler, and Mach numbers), arising naturally from the Buckingham Pi theorem applied to almost any viscous-flow problem, and serving as the primary similarity criterion for scaling submerged/viscous-dominated model tests up to full-size prototypes — making it arguably the single most recurring and important quantity introduced anywhere in this subject.
