0 Lagrangian vs Eulerian Description; the Velocity Field

Fluid kinematics describes fluid motion without reference to the forces causing it (that comes later, in Weeks 6–8). The Lagrangian description follows individual fluid particles over time (as in Week 1's "system" concept) — conceptually natural but mathematically cumbersome for a continuously deforming fluid. The Eulerian description instead defines properties (velocity, pressure, etc.) as fields: functions of fixed position and time, $\vec V(x,y,z,t)$ — the standard approach in fluid mechanics, matching the control volume concept from Week 1.

The velocity field $\vec V=(u,v,w)$ gives the velocity of whichever fluid particle happens to be at position $(x,y,z)$ at time $t$. A flow is steady if the velocity field (and all other flow properties) does not change with time at any fixed point: $\dfrac{\partial\vec V}{\partial t}=0$ — note this does not mean individual fluid particles have constant velocity as they move (they can still speed up or slow down as they move to different locations), only that the field itself, viewed from a fixed point in space, doesn't change over time.

Key idea

$$\text{Lagrangian: follow individual particles} \qquad \text{Eulerian: } \vec V(x,y,z,t) \text{ as a field at fixed points} \qquad \text{Steady: } \frac{\partial\vec V}{\partial t}=0$$

Worked example

Example. A velocity field is given by $u=2x,\ v=-2y$ (steady, 2D). Find the velocity at the point $(3,1)$, and determine whether the flow is steady.

At $(3,1)$: $u=2(3)=6$, $v=-2(1)=-2$. Velocity $\vec V=(6,-2)$ m/s (assuming SI units and $x,y$ in metres).

Since neither $u$ nor $v$ depends on $t$ in the given expressions, $\partial\vec V/\partial t=0$ everywhere — the flow is steady (though clearly not uniform, since velocity still varies with position).

Practice 1

For the velocity field $u=3x,\ v=-3y$, find the velocity at the point $(2,4)$.

Solution

$u=3(2)=6$, $v=-3(4)=-12$. $\vec V=(6,-12)$ m/s.

Practice 2

A velocity field is given by $u=x^2t,\ v=0$. Is this flow steady or unsteady? Explain.

Solution

Unsteady, since $u$ explicitly depends on $t$: at a fixed point $(x,y)$, the velocity changes as time progresses (e.g. at $x=2$: $u=4t$, clearly increasing with $t$).

Practice 3

A velocity field is given by $u=5,\ v=0$ (constant everywhere). Classify this flow as steady/unsteady and uniform/non-uniform.

Solution

Steady (no time dependence) AND uniform (velocity is the same at every position) — the simplest possible flow field, e.g. representing ideal uniform flow far from any obstacles.

Practice 4

Explain the key practical reason the Eulerian description is almost always preferred over the Lagrangian description for analysing fluid flow through a fixed piece of equipment (e.g. a pump, valve, or pipe fitting).

Solution

Equipment like a pump, valve, or pipe fitting has a fixed physical geometry in space; the Eulerian description, which reports flow properties as functions of fixed spatial location, aligns naturally with measuring instruments (pressure gauges, velocity probes) that are themselves mounted at fixed locations within or around the equipment. The Lagrangian approach would instead require identifying and tracking specific fluid particles as they enter, pass through, and exit the equipment — a far more complex bookkeeping task with no natural correspondence to how real measurements are actually taken in practice.

Practice 5

Explain why a flow can be STEADY (no change with time at any fixed point) while individual fluid particles within that flow still ACCELERATE as they move, using the example of steady flow through a converging nozzle.

Solution

In steady flow through a converging nozzle, the velocity at each fixed spatial location within the nozzle does not change over time (e.g. the velocity exactly at the nozzle's midpoint is always the same value, at any instant) — this is what "steady" means in the Eulerian sense. However, any individual fluid particle, as it physically travels from the wider inlet toward the narrower outlet, moves through a sequence of different locations that each have progressively higher velocity (since the area is shrinking and, by continuity, velocity must increase); that particle therefore experiences genuine acceleration as it moves through the (unchanging-in-time) velocity field — steadiness refers to no change over time at a fixed point, not to individual particles having constant velocity as they move through varying conditions.

1 Streamlines, Streaklines & Pathlines

A streamline is a curve that is everywhere tangent to the velocity field at a given instant — a purely instantaneous, mathematical/geometric snapshot of the flow direction (not something a fluid particle necessarily follows, unless the flow is steady). A pathline is the actual trajectory traced out by one specific fluid particle over time (the Lagrangian trajectory). A streakline is the locus of all fluid particles that have previously passed through one fixed point (e.g. the visible trace of smoke or dye continuously released from a fixed injection point).

For steady flow, streamlines, pathlines, and streaklines all coincide (the flow pattern doesn't change over time, so the instantaneous tangent-curve, the trajectory a particle actually follows, and the accumulated trace from a fixed point are all identical). For unsteady flow, they generally differ — a frequent source of confusion in flow visualisation experiments, where a streakline is what is often actually captured (e.g. dye or smoke trails), even though the underlying flow itself may be unsteady.

Key idea

$$\text{Streamline: instantaneous tangent curve} \qquad \text{Pathline: one particle’s actual trajectory} \qquad \text{Streakline: trace from a fixed point} \qquad \text{Steady flow: all three coincide}$$

Worked example

Example. For the steady velocity field $u=2x,\ v=-2y$ (from Section 0), find the equation of the streamline passing through the point $(1,1)$.

Streamlines satisfy $\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{v}{u}=\dfrac{-2y}{2x}=\dfrac{-y}{x}$. Separating variables: $\dfrac{dy}{y}=-\dfrac{dx}{x}\Rightarrow\ln y=-\ln x+C\Rightarrow xy=\text{constant}$.

At $(1,1)$: constant $=1(1)=1$. Streamline equation: $xy=1$ (a hyperbola) — a classic "stagnation flow" pattern.

Practice 6

For the velocity field $u=y,\ v=x$, find the differential equation defining the streamlines.

Solution

$dy/dx=v/u=x/y$.

Practice 7

Solve the streamline equation from the previous question ($y\,dy=x\,dx$) to find the streamline passing through $(2,0)$.

Solution

Integrating: $y^2/2=x^2/2+C\Rightarrow y^2-x^2=\text{constant}$. At $(2,0)$: constant $=0-4=-4$. Streamline: $y^2-x^2=-4$, or $x^2-y^2=4$ (a hyperbola).

Practice 8

For a steady, uniform flow field $u=U_0$ (constant), $v=0$, describe the shape of all streamlines.

Solution

Since $dy/dx=v/u=0$ everywhere, every streamline is a horizontal straight line ($y=\text{constant}$) — the simplest possible flow pattern, all streamlines parallel to the direction of uniform flow.

Practice 9

Explain why, for STEADY flow, a streamline, a pathline (starting on that streamline), and a streakline (from a point on that streamline) must all coincide, using the definitions of each.

Solution

In steady flow, the velocity field itself does not change over time; a streamline at any given instant is therefore identical to the streamline at any other instant (since the field is unchanging), meaning the instantaneous tangent-curve a particle is following never changes shape or position — so the particle's actual trajectory (pathline) simply traces along this one fixed streamline shape over time. Likewise, every new particle released from the same fixed point will follow that identical, unchanging streamline path, so the accumulated trace of all such particles (the streakline) also coincides with that same curve. All three concepts collapse into the same single curve specifically because the underlying flow pattern itself is time-invariant.

Practice 10

Describe a physical scenario where streamlines, pathlines, and streaklines would all be DIFFERENT from each other, and briefly explain why.

Solution

Consider unsteady flow past a bluff body (e.g. an obstacle in a river where the flow direction and speed are actively changing over time, such as during the startup transient of a pump, or in vortex-shedding flow behind a cylinder). Since the velocity field itself is continuously changing with time, the instantaneous tangent-curve (streamline) at one moment differs from the streamline shape a moment later; a fluid particle's actual trajectory (pathline) reflects the cumulative effect of moving through this whole sequence of changing instantaneous streamline patterns, so it will generally not match any single instantaneous streamline. Similarly, a streakline (accumulated trace of particles released from one fixed point at different past times, each experiencing a different evolving flow pattern along the way) will generally trace out yet another distinct curve shape — all three genuinely differ whenever the flow field itself changes with time.

2 Acceleration Field & the Material Derivative

Since velocity is described as a field $\vec V(x,y,z,t)$ (Eulerian), finding the acceleration of a specific fluid particle requires the material (substantial) derivative $\dfrac{D\vec V}{Dt}=\dfrac{\partial\vec V}{\partial t}+(\vec V\cdot\nabla)\vec V$ — the total rate of change following a moving particle, combining the field's own time-dependence (local acceleration, $\partial\vec V/\partial t$) with the change the particle experiences purely from moving to a new location where velocity differs (convective acceleration, $(\vec V\cdot\nabla)\vec V$).

For 1D flow: $a_x=\dfrac{Du}{Dt}=\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial t}}_{\text{local}}+\underbrace{u\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}}_{\text{convective}}$. Even in a perfectly steady flow (where local acceleration is zero everywhere), a fluid particle can still experience significant convective acceleration simply by moving through a region where velocity varies with position (e.g. speeding up through a nozzle, as noted in Section 0) — this distinction is one of the most important and initially confusing concepts in fluid kinematics.

Key idea

$$\frac{D\vec V}{Dt}=\underbrace{\frac{\partial\vec V}{\partial t}}_{\text{local}}+\underbrace{(\vec V\cdot\nabla)\vec V}_{\text{convective}} \qquad a_x=\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}+u\frac{\partial u}{\partial x}+v\frac{\partial u}{\partial y}+w\frac{\partial u}{\partial z}$$

Worked example

Example. For the steady 1D flow $u=4x$ (e.g. representing flow accelerating through a converging channel), find the acceleration $a_x$ at $x=2$ m.

Local acceleration $=\partial u/\partial t=0$ (steady flow). Convective acceleration $=u\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}=(4x)(4)=16x$.

$a_x=0+16x$. At $x=2$: $a_x=16(2)=32\ \text{m/s}^2$ — a substantial acceleration, entirely convective in origin (from moving to a higher-velocity location), even though the flow is steady.

Practice 11

For the steady 1D flow $u=3x^2$, find $a_x$ as a function of $x$ (convective acceleration only, since the flow is steady).

Solution

$a_x=u\dfrac{\partial u}{\partial x}=(3x^2)(6x)=18x^3$.

Practice 12

For the flow field $u=2x,\ v=-2y$ (steady, from Section 0's example), find $a_x$ and $a_y$ at the point $(1,1)$.

Solution

$a_x=u\partial u/\partial x+v\partial u/\partial y=(2x)(2)+(-2y)(0)=4x$. At $(1,1)$: $a_x=4$. $a_y=u\partial v/\partial x+v\partial v/\partial y=(2x)(0)+(-2y)(-2)=4y$. At $(1,1)$: $a_y=4$. So $\vec a=(4,4)\ \text{m/s}^2$.

Practice 13

For the unsteady 1D flow $u=2xt$, find the local acceleration and the convective acceleration separately, at $x=3,t=1$.

Solution

Local: $\partial u/\partial t=2x$; at $x=3$: local $=6\ \text{m/s}^2$. Convective: $u\partial u/\partial x=(2xt)(2t)=4xt^2$; at $x=3,t=1$: convective $=12\ \text{m/s}^2$. Total $a_x=6+12=18\ \text{m/s}^2$.

Practice 14

A flow is described as "steady but non-uniform." Explain what this implies about the possible presence of local vs convective acceleration for a fluid particle in this flow.

Solution

"Steady" means the local acceleration is zero everywhere ($\partial\vec V/\partial t=0$, since the field doesn't change with time at any fixed point). "Non-uniform" means velocity varies with position, so the convective acceleration term (which depends on spatial velocity gradients) can be, and generally will be, nonzero. So a fluid particle in this flow experiences acceleration purely from the convective term — it changes speed and/or direction solely because it moves to different locations with different velocities, not because the flow pattern itself is evolving in time.

Practice 15

Explain, using the concept of convective acceleration, why a fluid particle moving through a garden hose nozzle (which narrows toward the outlet) speeds up even under perfectly steady flow conditions, connecting this to mass conservation (a preview of Week 6).

Solution

Under steady flow, mass conservation (Week 6) requires the volumetric flow rate to remain constant along the hose, meaning velocity must increase as the cross-sectional area decreases toward the narrower nozzle outlet (to keep $Q=AV$ constant). Since the velocity field is therefore genuinely a function of position (higher velocity near the narrow outlet, lower velocity near the wider inlet), even though it doesn't change over time at any fixed point (steady flow, zero local acceleration), a fluid particle physically travelling from the wide inlet toward the narrow outlet moves through this sequence of increasingly higher local velocities — producing a real, physically-experienced convective acceleration, exactly the mechanism responsible for the familiar increase in jet speed observed when you partially cover a garden hose outlet with your thumb.

3 Reynolds Transport Theorem (Introduction)

The Reynolds Transport Theorem (RTT) is the essential mathematical bridge connecting the Lagrangian "system" viewpoint (where fundamental physical laws like conservation of mass, momentum, and energy are naturally stated, since they describe fixed, identifiable matter) to the Eulerian "control volume" viewpoint (the practical framework for analysing flowing fluids, Week 1, Section 3). It converts a rate of change following a system into an equivalent expression involving a fixed control volume.

For any extensive property $B$ (with corresponding intensive property $b=B/m$ per unit mass): $\dfrac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt}=\underbrace{\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\displaystyle\int_{CV}\rho b\,dV}_{\text{rate of change within the CV}}+\underbrace{\displaystyle\int_{CS}\rho b(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA}_{\text{net flux out through the control surface}}$. This single unifying theorem, applied with $B=m$ (mass), $B=m\vec V$ (momentum), or $B=E$ (energy), directly generates the three fundamental conservation equations that occupy Weeks 6–8 — it is the conceptual foundation underlying essentially everything that follows in this subject.

Key idea

$$\frac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt}=\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\int_{CV}\rho b\,dV+\int_{CS}\rho b(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA \qquad \text{(system rate of change = CV storage rate + net CS flux)}$$

Worked example

Example. For steady flow, explain (conceptually) how the Reynolds Transport Theorem simplifies, and what this implies for the CV term.

For steady flow, nothing changes with time inside the control volume, so $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\displaystyle\int_{CV}\rho b\,dV=0$ — the entire system's rate of change of $B$ is then accounted for purely by the net flux through the control surface: $\dfrac{dB_{\text{sys}}}{dt}=\displaystyle\int_{CS}\rho b(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA$.

This is exactly the simplification used throughout Weeks 6–8 for steady-flow mass, momentum, and energy analysis — a dramatic simplification that makes most practical engineering flow problems tractable using only inlet/outlet conditions, without needing to track anything happening inside the control volume itself.

Practice 16

Identify the intensive property $b$ (i.e. $B/m$) corresponding to $B=m$ (mass) itself, and explain what value it must take.

Solution

$b=B/m=m/m=1$ — substituting $b=1$ into the RTT gives the conservation of mass equation directly (the subject of the first part of Week 6).

Practice 17

Identify the intensive property $b$ corresponding to $B=m\vec V$ (linear momentum).

Solution

$b=B/m=(m\vec V)/m=\vec V$ — substituting $b=\vec V$ into the RTT gives the linear momentum equation (Week 8).

Practice 18

Explain, in general terms, why the Reynolds Transport Theorem is described as the mathematical "bridge" between the Lagrangian and Eulerian viewpoints introduced in Section 0.

Solution

The fundamental conservation laws of physics (conservation of mass, Newton's second law, conservation of energy) are naturally and most simply stated for a fixed, identifiable quantity of matter (a Lagrangian "system") — e.g. "the mass of this specific system doesn't change." However, tracking a deforming system of fluid is impractical for engineering flow analysis (Week 1). The RTT provides an exact mathematical conversion formula, translating any system-based rate-of-change statement into an equivalent statement expressed purely in terms of a fixed control volume (storage plus flux terms) — allowing engineers to apply the fundamental system-based physical laws while working entirely within the much more practical, fixed-in-space Eulerian/control-volume framework.

Practice 19

For an UNSTEADY flow (e.g. a tank being filled), explain why the CV storage term $\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\displaystyle\int_{CV}\rho b\,dV$ generally cannot be neglected, in contrast to the steady-flow case.

Solution

In unsteady flow, properties within the control volume genuinely change over time (e.g. the total mass of water within a filling tank increases as time progresses), so the storage term $\partial/\partial t\int_{CV}\rho b\,dV$ is nonzero and represents a real, physically necessary contribution to the overall balance. Neglecting it would incorrectly assume the amount of the property $B$ contained within the fixed control volume never changes, which is only valid under steady-state conditions; for a filling or draining tank, ignoring this term would produce a physically wrong mass balance.

Practice 20

Explain, at a conceptual level, why applying the Reynolds Transport Theorem with $B=E$ (total energy) and the appropriate intensive property $b$ leads directly to the general energy equation used for pipe flow and turbomachinery analysis (a preview of Week 7).

Solution

The first law of thermodynamics (conservation of energy) is fundamentally a system-based statement (energy of a fixed quantity of matter changes only due to heat and work interactions with its surroundings). Applying the RTT with $B=E$ (and correspondingly $b=e$, energy per unit mass) converts this system-based energy balance into control-volume form, yielding a rate equation involving the rate of energy storage within a fixed control volume plus the net rate of energy flux carried by fluid crossing the control surface — which, once specialised for steady flow through a pipe, pump, or turbine (with appropriate work and heat transfer terms), becomes exactly the general energy equation used throughout Week 7 to analyse pumps, turbines, and pipe flow systems.
