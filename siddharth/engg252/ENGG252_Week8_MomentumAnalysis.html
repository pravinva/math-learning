0 The Linear Momentum Equation

Applying the Reynolds Transport Theorem (Week 5) with $B=m\vec V$ (linear momentum), $b=\vec V$, to a fixed control volume gives the linear momentum equation — the fluid-mechanics statement of Newton's second law: $\sum\vec F=\dfrac{\partial}{\partial t}\displaystyle\int_{CV}\rho\vec V\,dV+\displaystyle\int_{CS}\rho\vec V(\vec V\cdot\hat n)\,dA$. For steady flow with uniform properties across each inlet/outlet, this reduces to the practical working form: $$\sum\vec F=\sum_{\text{out}}\dot m\vec V-\sum_{\text{in}}\dot m\vec V$$

$\sum\vec F$ includes all forces acting on the fluid within the control volume: pressure forces at the inlets/outlets, weight, and the reaction force from any solid surfaces (pipe walls, vanes, nozzles) in contact with the fluid — it is precisely this last term, found by solving the momentum equation, that gives the force a flow exerts on a pipe bend, nozzle, or vane, the central application throughout this week.

Key idea

$$\sum\vec F=\sum_{\text{out}}\dot m\vec V-\sum_{\text{in}}\dot m\vec V \quad \text{(steady flow, uniform properties at each inlet/outlet)}$$

Worked example

Example. Water flows through a straight horizontal pipe of constant diameter at $\dot m=20$ kg/s, entering and leaving at the same velocity 3 m/s (no change in speed or direction). Find the net force the water exerts in the flow direction, purely from momentum change.

Since velocity doesn't change (same magnitude, same direction, constant-diameter straight pipe), $\sum\vec F=\dot m(V_{\text{out}}-V_{\text{in}})=20(3-3)=0$ — zero net force from momentum change alone (any actual force on the pipe wall here comes purely from pressure difference due to friction losses, not from momentum change, since the velocity itself is unchanged).

Practice 1

Water flows through a nozzle at $\dot m=15$ kg/s, entering at 2 m/s and leaving at 12 m/s (same direction, straight nozzle). Find the net force (in the flow direction) required to produce this momentum change.

Solution

$F=\dot m(V_{\text{out}}-V_{\text{in}})=15(12-2)=150$ N.

Practice 2

A jet of water, $\dot m=8$ kg/s, strikes a flat plate perpendicular to the jet and is deflected to zero velocity along the original jet direction (i.e. spreads out sideways). Find the force on the plate (in the original jet direction), if the jet enters at 15 m/s.

Solution

$F=\dot m(V_{\text{out}}-V_{\text{in}})=8(0-15)=-120$ N (on the water, in the jet direction) — meaning the plate exerts $-120$ N on the water (decelerating it), so by Newton's third law, the water exerts $+120$ N on the plate, in the original jet direction.

Practice 3

A water jet, $\dot m=5$ kg/s, enters a control volume at 10 m/s and exits at 10 m/s but in the exact opposite direction (a full $180\,{}^\circ$ reversal, as at a curved vane). Find the magnitude of the force required (using a 1D sign convention along the jet's original direction).

Solution

Taking the original direction as positive: $V_{\text{in}}=+10$, $V_{\text{out}}=-10$ (reversed). $F=\dot m(V_{\text{out}}-V_{\text{in}})=5(-10-10)=-100$ N — a much larger magnitude force than a simple deceleration-to-zero case, since the momentum change spans the full reversal (from $+10$ to $-10$, a change of 20 m/s, not just 10 m/s).

Practice 4

Explain why the force found from the momentum equation, $\sum\vec F=\dot m(V_{\text{out}}-V_{\text{in}})$, represents the TOTAL force on the fluid (including pressure, weight, AND the reaction force from any solid surface), not just the reaction force alone.

Solution

The momentum equation is derived directly from Newton's second law applied to the fluid within the control volume; $\sum\vec F$ on the left-hand side is, by definition, the sum of ALL external forces acting on that fluid, regardless of their physical origin (pressure pushing on the fluid at the inlet/outlet cross-sections, the fluid's own weight, and any contact/reaction force from solid boundaries like pipe walls or vanes). To isolate specifically the reaction force from a solid surface (which is usually the quantity of practical engineering interest, e.g. the force on a pipe bend), one must separately account for and subtract the pressure and weight contributions from the total force found via the momentum equation — a step demonstrated explicitly in the pipe-bend application (Section 1).

Practice 5

Explain why the momentum equation is stated in terms of $\dot m\vec V$ (a vector quantity, with the sign/direction of $\vec V$ mattering) rather than simply $\dot m|\vec V|$ (treating speed alone), using the 180-degree reversal example from this section as illustration.

Solution

Momentum is fundamentally a vector quantity (it has both magnitude and direction), and Newton's second law relates the vector sum of forces to the vector rate of change of momentum; simply using speed (magnitude only, always treated as positive) would completely fail to capture situations like the 180° reversal example, where the fluid's speed is unchanged (10 m/s throughout) but its direction has fully reversed — a huge vector change in momentum despite zero change in speed. Only by correctly treating velocity (and momentum) as a signed/directional vector quantity does the momentum equation correctly predict the very large force required to fully reverse a jet's direction, compared to the much smaller force needed merely to decelerate the same jet to rest.

1 Momentum Equation Applications: Pipe Bends & Nozzles

For a pipe bend or reducing nozzle, the fluid's direction and/or speed changes between inlet and outlet, requiring a net force — supplied by the pipe wall's anchoring/reaction force $\vec R$ on the fluid (and, by Newton's third law, the fluid exerts an equal and opposite force on the pipe/bend itself, which anchor bolts or thrust blocks must be designed to resist). Applying the momentum equation component-by-component (typically $x$ and $y$ separately) with all forces (pressure at inlet $P_1A_1$, pressure at outlet $P_2A_2$, and the unknown reaction $\vec R$) explicitly included: $$\sum F_x=P_1A_1-P_2A_2\cos\theta+R_x=\dot m(V_2\cos\theta-V_1)$$

Standard solution procedure: (1) sketch the control volume and define a consistent sign convention; (2) find $V_1,V_2$ from continuity (Week 6); (3) find $P_1,P_2$ from Bernoulli or the energy equation (Weeks 6–7) if not directly given; (4) apply the momentum equation in each coordinate direction, solving for the unknown reaction force components $R_x,R_y$; (5) combine into a resultant force magnitude and direction, and note that the force on the bend is equal and opposite to $\vec R$ (the force on the fluid).

Key idea

$$\sum F_x=P_1A_1-P_2A_2\cos\theta+R_x=\dot m(V_2\cos\theta-V_1) \qquad \text{Force on the bend } = -\vec R \ \text{(Newton’s third law)}$$

Worked example

Example. Water flows through a 90° horizontal reducing pipe bend: inlet diameter 200 mm, outlet diameter 100 mm, $\dot m=100$ kg/s, $P_1=150$ kPa (gauge). Find $V_1,V_2$, and $P_2$ (using Bernoulli, horizontal, negligible loss for this idealised case), then set up the momentum equations (values only, full solve omitted for brevity in this preview).

$A_1=\pi(0.100)^2=0.03142\ \text{m}^2$, $A_2=\pi(0.050)^2=7.854\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$. $V_1=\dot m/(\rho A_1)=100/(1000\times0.03142)=3.183\ \text{m/s}$. $V_2=\dot m/(\rho A_2)=100/(1000\times7.854\times10^{-3})=12.73\ \text{m/s}$.

Bernoulli: $P_2=P_1+\frac12\rho(V_1^2-V_2^2)=150000+\frac12(1000)(10.13-162.1)=150000-75{,}990=74{,}010\ \text{Pa}$. These values ($V_1,V_2,P_1,P_2$) are exactly what feed into the $x$- and $y$-momentum equations to solve for $R_x,R_y$ (a full 90°-bend solve is worked through in the practice problems).

Practice 6

Water flows through a straight, horizontal, constant-diameter pipe section (100 mm diameter) at $\dot m=30$ kg/s. $P_1=200$ kPa, $P_2=190$ kPa (a pressure drop due to friction, Week 9-10). Find the net anchoring force $R_x$ needed in the flow direction (no direction change, so $V_1=V_2$, momentum change is zero).

Solution

$A=\pi(0.050)^2=7.854\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$. $P_1A_1-P_2A_2+R_x=\dot m(V_2-V_1)=0\Rightarrow R_x=(P_2-P_1)A=(190000-200000)(7.854\times10^{-3})=-78.5$ N (the pipe must be anchored to resist this net force, arising purely from the pressure difference, since momentum itself doesn't change here).

Practice 7

A jet of water, $\dot m=10$ kg/s, $V=20$ m/s, strikes a stationary flat plate held perpendicular to the jet, and splatters sideways (final velocity component in the original jet direction $=0$). Find the force on the plate.

Solution

$F_{\text{on fluid}}=\dot m(0-20)=-200$ N (in the jet direction). By Newton's third law, force ON the plate $=+200$ N, in the original jet direction.

Practice 8

A jet of water, $\dot m=6$ kg/s, $V=15$ m/s, strikes a curved vane and is deflected through 60° from its original direction, leaving at the same speed (15 m/s, frictionless vane assumption). Find the $x$-component (original jet direction) of the force on the vane.

Solution

$F_x$ on fluid $=\dot m(V\cos60\,{}^\circ-V)=6(15\times0.5-15)=6(7.5-15)=6(-7.5)=-45$ N. Force ON the vane (Newton's third law) $=+45$ N, in the original jet direction.

Practice 9

For the same vane in the previous question, find the $y$-component (perpendicular to the original jet direction) of the force on the vane.

Solution

$F_y$ on fluid $=\dot m(V\sin60\,{}^\circ-0)=6(15\times0.866)=6(12.99)=77.9$ N. Force ON the vane $=-77.9$ N (opposite sign, Newton's third law) in that same perpendicular direction.

Practice 10

A fire hose nozzle reduces the pipe diameter from 80 mm to 25 mm, delivering water at $\dot m=25$ kg/s. Find $V_1$ and $V_2$ using continuity, then explain (without full pressure/force calculation) why the firefighter holding the nozzle must brace against a substantial reaction force.

Solution

$A_1=\pi(0.040)^2=5.027\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$, $A_2=\pi(0.0125)^2=4.909\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $V_1=25/(1000\times5.027\times10^{-3})=4.97\ \text{m/s}$. $V_2=25/(1000\times4.909\times10^{-4})=50.9\ \text{m/s}$. The large increase in velocity (roughly a 10-fold speed increase) represents a substantial momentum change of the water as it passes through the nozzle; by the momentum equation, this requires a correspondingly large force from the nozzle (and the firefighter's grip) acting on the water to accelerate it — and by Newton's third law, the water exerts an equal and opposite (substantial) reaction force back on the nozzle and the person holding it, which is exactly the "kick" firefighters must brace against when operating a fire hose.

Practice 11

Explain why a 180-degree pipe bend (U-bend) experiences a LARGER anchoring force than a 90-degree bend, for the same inlet/outlet velocity magnitudes and mass flow rate, referencing the vector nature of the momentum change.

Solution

In a 90° bend, the velocity vector changes direction by 90°, producing a moderate net vector change in momentum. In a 180° bend, the velocity vector reverses direction entirely, meaning the outlet momentum vector points in essentially the exact opposite direction from the inlet momentum vector — the vector difference (change) between two opposite-pointing vectors of the same magnitude is exactly double the magnitude of either vector alone (whereas a 90° direction change produces a vector difference smaller than a full reversal, specifically related to $\sqrt2$ times the magnitude for equal speeds). Since the required force is directly proportional to this vector momentum change, a 180° U-bend requires substantially more anchoring force than a 90° bend under otherwise identical flow conditions.

2 Momentum Equation for Jets Striking Surfaces

A free jet striking a stationary flat plate or curved vane (open to atmosphere, unlike the enclosed pipe-bend case) is a classic and instructive momentum-equation application, directly relevant to devices like Pelton wheel turbines (water jets striking curved buckets to extract mechanical energy, connecting directly back to Week 7's turbine concepts). Since the jet and its deflected flow are both exposed to atmospheric pressure throughout, the pressure terms in the momentum equation cancel (atmospheric pressure acts uniformly on both the incoming jet and outgoing deflected flow), leaving a comparatively simple pure-momentum-change calculation.

For a jet deflected through angle $\theta$ by a frictionless vane (speed unchanged, only direction changes): force component along the original jet direction $F_x=\dot m V(1-\cos\theta)$ (using the sign convention that $F_x$ is the force on the vane, in the jet's original direction) — maximum ($F_x=2\dot mV$) when $\theta=180\,{}^\circ$ (full reversal, as found in Section 0), and zero when $\theta=0$ (no deflection at all, as expected).

Key idea

$$F_x \text{ (on vane, frictionless, speed unchanged)}=\dot mV(1-\cos\theta) \qquad \theta=180\,{}^\circ: F_x=2\dot mV \ \text{(maximum)} \qquad \theta=0: F_x=0$$

Worked example

Example. A water jet, $\dot m=4$ kg/s, $V=25$ m/s, strikes a curved vane that deflects it through $135\,{}^\circ$ (frictionless, speed unchanged). Find the force on the vane in the original jet direction.

$F_x=\dot mV(1-\cos135\,{}^\circ)=4(25)(1-(-0.707))=100(1.707)=170.7\ \text{N}$.

Practice 12

A jet, $\dot m=3$ kg/s, $V=18$ m/s, strikes a flat plate perpendicular to the jet ($\theta=90\,{}^\circ$ deflection, splattering sideways). Find the force on the plate.

Solution

$F_x=\dot mV(1-\cos90\,{}^\circ)=3(18)(1-0)=54$ N.

Practice 13

A jet, $\dot m=5$ kg/s, $V=20$ m/s, strikes a vane that fully reverses it ($\theta=180\,{}^\circ$, as in a Pelton wheel bucket). Find the force on the vane, and compare to a flat plate case ($\theta=90\,{}^\circ$) with the same $\dot m,V$.

Solution

$F_{180}=\dot mV(1-\cos180\,{}^\circ)=5(20)(1-(-1))=100(2)=200$ N. $F_{90}=5(20)(1-0)=100$ N. The full-reversal vane produces exactly double the force of the flat-plate case — explaining why Pelton wheel buckets are curved to nearly reverse the jet, maximising force (and hence power extraction) for a given jet.

Practice 14

A jet, $\dot m=2$ kg/s, $V=30$ m/s, is deflected through $45\,{}^\circ$ by a vane. Find the force on the vane in the original jet direction.

Solution

$F_x=2(30)(1-\cos45\,{}^\circ)=60(1-0.707)=60(0.293)=17.6$ N.

Practice 15

Explain why the pressure terms cancel out of the momentum equation for a free jet striking a vane in open air, but do NOT cancel for an enclosed pipe bend (Section 1), even though both involve fluid changing direction.

Solution

For a free jet exposed to the atmosphere both before and after striking the vane, the pressure acting on the jet at both the inlet and outlet cross-sections is simply atmospheric pressure (gauge pressure zero) — there is no pressure buildup or pressure difference driving the flow through an enclosed geometry, so the pressure-force terms in the momentum equation vanish identically. In an enclosed pipe bend, by contrast, the fluid is under genuine internal gauge pressure (needed to push it through the pipe system against friction and other resistances), and this internal pressure differs from atmospheric and generally differs between the inlet and outlet cross-sections (especially if the pipe diameter changes) — these nonzero, generally unequal pressure forces must be explicitly included and cannot be assumed to cancel.

Practice 16

Explain, using the formula $F_x=\dot mV(1-\cos\theta)$, why Pelton wheel turbine buckets are deliberately shaped to deflect the water jet through nearly (but not exactly) 180°, rather than exactly 180°, referencing a practical engineering constraint.

Solution

The formula shows that force (and hence power extraction) is maximised exactly at $\theta=180\,{}^\circ$ (full reversal); however, a real Pelton bucket moving past the fixed jet nozzle must allow the deflected water to clear the bucket and flow away without striking the back of the FOLLOWING bucket (which is moving into position immediately behind it) — a physical/geometric necessity given the turbine wheel's continuous rotation. Deflecting the jet through very nearly (but slightly less than) 180° (typically around 165°–170° in real Pelton wheel designs) sacrifices only a small amount of theoretical force/power (since $\cos\theta$ changes very slowly near $\theta=180\,{}^\circ$) while providing the practical clearance needed for the deflected water to exit the bucket cleanly.

3 Moving Control Volumes & an Introduction to Angular Momentum

For a moving vane (e.g. a series of buckets on a rotating turbine wheel, or a single vane moving at constant velocity $\vec V_{\text{vane}}$), the momentum equation must be applied using the relative velocity $\vec V_r=\vec V_{\text{jet}}-\vec V_{\text{vane}}$ (the velocity of the fluid as seen by an observer moving with the vane) — this is the velocity that actually determines the force the vane experiences, since it reflects the fluid's true velocity change relative to the (moving) surface deflecting it.

Angular momentum (a natural extension of linear momentum for rotating systems, e.g. sprinklers, pumps, turbines with rotating blades) follows the same Reynolds Transport Theorem logic with $B=\vec r\times m\vec V$ (moment of momentum): $\sum\vec M=\sum_{\text{out}}\dot m(\vec r\times\vec V)-\sum_{\text{in}}\dot m(\vec r\times\vec V)$, directly relating the torque on a rotating fluid machine to the change in the fluid's angular momentum as it passes through — the fundamental principle behind turbomachinery (pumps, turbines, compressors) design, though full development of this topic is left for further study beyond this subject.

Key idea

$$\vec V_r=\vec V_{\text{jet}}-\vec V_{\text{vane}} \ \text{(use relative velocity for a moving vane)} \qquad \sum\vec M=\sum_{\text{out}}\dot m(\vec r\times\vec V)-\sum_{\text{in}}\dot m(\vec r\times\vec V) \ \text{(angular momentum)}$$

Worked example

Example. A water jet, $V_{\text{jet}}=25$ m/s, strikes a single flat vane moving away from the nozzle at $V_{\text{vane}}=8$ m/s (same direction as the jet). If the jet's own mass flow rate (from the stationary nozzle) is $\dot m_{\text{jet}}=10$ kg/s, find the relative velocity, and note the important subtlety that the EFFECTIVE mass flow rate striking the moving vane is reduced (since the vane is "running away" from the jet).

$V_r=V_{\text{jet}}-V_{\text{vane}}=25-8=17\ \text{m/s}$ — this is the velocity actually used in the force calculation (deflected relative to the vane, e.g. $F_x=\dot m_{\text{eff}}V_r(1-\cos\theta)$ for a single vane).

Effective mass flow rate onto the moving vane: $\dot m_{\text{eff}}=\rho A_{\text{jet}}V_r$ (using the relative velocity, not the original jet velocity, since only the "catching up" portion of the jet actually reaches and interacts with the receding vane) — a subtlety worth remembering whenever a single vane, rather than a full series of vanes on a wheel, is being analysed.

Practice 17

A jet with $V_{\text{jet}}=30$ m/s strikes a single vane moving in the same direction at $V_{\text{vane}}=10$ m/s. Find the relative velocity.

Solution

$V_r=30-10=20\ \text{m/s}$.

Practice 18

A jet with $V_{\text{jet}}=20$ m/s strikes a vane moving TOWARD the nozzle (opposite direction) at $V_{\text{vane}}=5$ m/s. Find the relative velocity (careful with signs: use consistent sign convention where the jet direction is positive, so the vane's opposing velocity is negative).

Solution

$V_r=V_{\text{jet}}-V_{\text{vane}}=20-(-5)=25\ \text{m/s}$ (larger than the jet speed alone, since the vane is approaching the oncoming jet).

Practice 19

Explain why the relative velocity, rather than the jet's original (absolute) velocity, must be used when calculating the force on a MOVING vane.

Solution

The force a vane experiences depends on the actual velocity change of the fluid AS EXPERIENCED BY THE VANE ITSELF (i.e. observed from a reference frame moving with the vane), since it is this relative motion that determines how the fluid is genuinely being deflected and decelerated/accelerated by the vane's surface. Using the jet's absolute (ground-frame) velocity would incorrectly ignore the fact that a receding vane is being "caught up to" more slowly (reducing the effective impact), or that an approaching vane experiences a faster, more forceful impact than the jet's raw ground-frame speed alone would suggest — the relative velocity correctly captures this vane-frame perspective.

Practice 20

Explain, in general conceptual terms, why the angular momentum equation (rather than the simpler linear momentum equation) is the appropriate tool for analysing torque and rotational effects in devices like lawn sprinklers, pump impellers, or turbine rotors.

Solution

Linear momentum and the associated linear momentum equation relate net force to the rate of change of straight-line (translational) momentum, which is the natural tool for analysing devices that produce or resist translational forces (like pipe bends or vanes moving in a straight line). Devices like sprinklers, pump impellers, or turbine rotors, however, involve fluid entering and leaving at different radial positions relative to a rotation axis, producing (or being driven by) a TORQUE (rotational effect) rather than simply a translational force; the angular momentum equation, which relates net torque to the rate of change of the moment of momentum ($\vec r\times m\vec V$), is the directly analogous and appropriate tool for capturing this rotational, rather than purely translational, physical behaviour.

Practice 21

Summarising the arc of Weeks 6-8: explain how mass conservation (continuity), the energy equation, and the momentum equation each answer a DIFFERENT fundamental question about the same flowing fluid, using a single example (e.g. flow through a pump) to illustrate all three.

Solution

For flow through a pump: continuity (Week 6) answers "how much fluid passes through, and how does velocity change with cross-sectional area?" (a purely geometric/kinematic bookkeeping question). The energy equation (Week 7) answers "how much mechanical energy (head) does the pump add to the fluid, and what shaft power is required, accounting for efficiency and losses?" (a scalar energy-accounting question). The momentum equation (Week 8) answers "what net force does the fluid exert on the pump casing/mounting bolts as its momentum changes passing through?" (a vector force-and-reaction question). All three principles apply simultaneously to the very same physical flow, but each extracts fundamentally different, complementary information — together forming the complete analytical toolkit for any flow system, exactly the progression this subject has built across these three weeks.
