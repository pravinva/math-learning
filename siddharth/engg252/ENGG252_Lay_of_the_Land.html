01 The one idea behind the subject

You already know one accounting problem, and you have known it since your first job costing a project: money enters an account, money leaves it, and whatever is left over is what accumulated. Fluid mechanics runs the identical argument on physical quantities instead of dollars. Draw a boundary — a control volume — around some region of interest, whether that is a length of pipe, a pump casing, or a block of still water. Then track one ledger at a time: how much mass crosses the boundary, how much momentum crosses it and what force that requires, or how much energy crosses it and what work or friction is spent along the way.

Every method in this subject is that same balance, applied to a different ledger, in a system with a different amount of motion. Hydrostatics is the ledger with velocity set to zero, so nothing crosses the boundary and only pressure and weight remain to balance each other. Bernoulli's equation is the energy ledger for a system with no friction and no machinery. The general energy equation puts the pump work and the friction losses back in. The momentum equation is the same boundary, but tracking a vector instead of a scalar, which is what lets you compute a force instead of a pressure. Nothing new is invented after Week 6; the same drawing of a boundary is reused on a different quantity, again and again.

The idea in one paragraph

A bank statement balances what came in against what went out. In fluid mechanics the same balance is written for mass ($\dot m_{in}=\dot m_{out}$ for steady flow), for energy ($p/\rho g + V^2/2g + z$, plus pump work, minus losses, is conserved along the system), and for momentum ($\sum F = \dot m(V_{out}-V_{in})$). Which ledger you open depends only on what the question is actually asking for: a flow rate, a pressure or a head, or a force.

02 Five things every fluid system does

This is the mental model worth carrying into every week. Any real fluid system — a dewatering sump, a slurry pipeline, a water cannon, a flotation feed line — does five things, and ENGG252 gives you one body of technique for each.

One

It sits still

Even with zero velocity, pressure builds with depth and pushes on every wall, gate and dam face it touches. This is the momentum ledger with the flow terms switched off.

Two

It moves

Before you can balance anything you need language for how a fluid is moving: velocity fields, streamlines, and how fast a property changes for a particle that is itself travelling.

Week 5 · kinematics, the material derivative
Three

It conserves mass

What enters a control volume must leave it, or the level inside it must change. That single statement is the continuity equation, and it is the first ledger you will ever write.

Week 6 · continuity, $A_1V_1=A_2V_2$
Four

It exchanges energy and momentum

Height, speed and pressure trade off against each other along a streamline, and any change in direction or speed of the flow requires a force to produce it.

Weeks 6–8 · Bernoulli, energy, momentum
Five

It loses energy to friction, and it can be scaled

Real pipes are not frictionless, and the correlations you use to price that friction — and to build a small-scale model of anything — come from the same technique.

Weeks 9–13 · pipe flow, measurement, dimensional analysis
Why the topics come in this order

You cannot describe a moving fluid until you have language for motion, which puts kinematics before any of the balance equations. You cannot write the momentum or energy ledger until continuity has told you how the velocity changes as the pipe changes size, so continuity comes first among the three conservation laws. Bernoulli is introduced before the general energy equation because it is the friction-free special case, and seeing the ideal answer first is what lets you recognise, later, exactly what pipe friction subtracts from it. Dimensional analysis is saved for last because it explains where the friction factor and the discharge coefficients you were told to look up in Weeks 9–11 actually came from.

03 Weeks 1–2: fluid properties and the meaning of pressure

Before any balance can be written you need the vocabulary that describes a fluid at a point: density $\rho$, specific weight $\gamma=\rho g$, and specific gravity relative to water. Viscosity $\mu$ is the property that makes a fluid a fluid rather than a solid — it relates a shear stress to a rate of angular deformation through Newton's law of viscosity, $\tau=\mu\,du/dy$, and it is what makes pumping a thick slurry a different problem to pumping water even at the same flow rate.

Pressure gets a careful definition here that matters for the rest of the subject: at a point in a fluid at rest, pressure is the same in every direction (Pascal's law), which is exactly why it can be treated as a scalar rather than a vector for the rest of Weeks 1–11. You also meet the distinction between absolute and gauge pressure, vapour pressure and the cavitation it causes when local pressure falls to that level, and surface tension. Compressibility and the bulk modulus close out the week — for almost everything else in this subject, liquids are treated as incompressible, and this is the week that tells you why that assumption is safe.

Quick example

A tailings slurry has a plastic viscosity roughly ten times that of water. If everything else about the pipeline were unchanged, the wall shear stress — and therefore the pressure drop the pump has to overcome — scales up by close to the same factor. That single number, read off a rheology test in Week 2, is what a pump selection in Week 9 is actually built around.

Engineering application
  • Slurry and paste viscosity (mining). Tailings and paste-fill lines are non-Newtonian; the apparent viscosity used in every later pipe-flow calculation for that line comes straight out of this week's definitions.
  • Cavitation at a pump inlet (mining and general). A dewatering or process-water pump starved on suction side drops local pressure toward vapour pressure; cavitation damage on the impeller is a direct consequence of the vapour-pressure concept introduced here.
  • Barometric pressure underground (mining). Absolute vs gauge pressure matters directly when ventilation and compressed-air calculations are referenced to a shaft bottom rather than to sea level.
  • Water hammer and hydraulic fracturing (general). The bulk modulus you compute here is the same property that governs pressure-wave speed in a rapidly closed valve and the stiffness of water used in high-pressure jetting or fracturing.
04 Weeks 3–4: hydrostatics, the zero-velocity limit

With velocity set to zero, the momentum ledger from Section 01 collapses to a single statement: pressure increases with depth exactly enough to support the weight of fluid above it, $dp/dz=-\rho g$. Integrating that gives $p=p_0+\rho g h$, the equation behind every manometer reading you will take in the lab. Week 3 also covers buoyancy (Archimedes' principle, which is this same pressure integral applied to a submerged or floating body) and fluids undergoing rigid-body motion — a tank accelerating in a truck, or a cyclone spinning a slurry — where the free surface tilts or curves to a shape you can find from the same balance with an inertial term added.

Week 4 turns the same integral into force calculations on real surfaces. The force on a submerged plane surface is $F_R=\rho g h_c A$, acting not at the centroid but at a centre of pressure found from the second moment of area of the surface, $y_{cp}=y_c+I_{xc}/(y_c A)$. Curved surfaces are handled by resolving the resultant into horizontal and vertical components separately: the horizontal component equals the force on the vertical projection of the surface, and the vertical component equals the weight of the real or imaginary fluid above it.

Engineering application
  • Tailings dam and retaining wall design (mining and general). The force and overturning moment on an embankment or bulkhead face is a direct plane-surface hydrostatics calculation, centre of pressure included.
  • Dewatering sump and pontoon buoyancy (mining). Sizing a floating pump platform or checking whether a submerged structure will lift is Archimedes' principle applied without approximation.
  • Cyclone and centrifuge free surface (mining). The paraboloid free surface inside a spinning classifier is the rigid-body-rotation result from Week 3, and it is why the core of a cyclone is air rather than slurry.
  • Curved gates and pipeline end caps (general). Any curved bulkhead, gate or pipe cap is sized using the horizontal/vertical resolution method rather than a direct integral, because the direct integral over a curved surface is usually harder than it needs to be.
05 Week 5: kinematics — describing motion before you balance it

Kinematics is the week that gives you a language for motion with no forces involved yet. A velocity field $\mathbf{V}(x,y,z,t)$ describes speed and direction everywhere in the fluid at every instant; streamlines are curves everywhere tangent to that field at one instant, and for steady flow they coincide with the path any particle actually follows. The trickiest idea in the week is the material derivative, $\dfrac{D\phi}{Dt}=\dfrac{\partial\phi}{\partial t}+\mathbf{V}\cdot\nabla\phi$: the rate of change felt by a particle riding along with the flow, made of a local part (does the field itself change with time at a fixed point) and a convective part (does the particle move into a region where the property is different). Applied to velocity, this is exactly how a fluid can accelerate even in a steady flow — a particle speeding up simply because the pipe is narrowing.

The week closes with the Reynolds Transport Theorem, the formal bridge between a physical law written for a fixed collection of matter (a "system", the language physics uses) and the fixed-boundary "control volume" view this subject actually calculates with. Every balance equation from Week 6 onward is this theorem, applied once to mass, once to momentum, once to energy.

Why this week feels like a pause

No forces, no pressures, no answers you can quote in a report appear this week, and that is deliberate. Weeks 6–8 need convective acceleration and the Reynolds Transport Theorem as machinery, the same way Weeks 6–7 of MATH283 need the multivariable calculus built in its own first three weeks. Treat this week as infrastructure, not filler.

Engineering application
  • Feed acceleration into a cyclone or mill (mining). Slurry accelerating into a narrowing feed chamber is convective acceleration made visible; it is why inlet erosion concentrates exactly where the geometry narrows fastest.
  • CFD models of a thickener or launder (mining and general). Any velocity field you are shown from a computational fluid dynamics study is exactly the Eulerian field this week defines; being able to read streamlines off such a plot is a direct use of this material.
  • Traffic and stormwater network modelling (general). The same local/convective split appears whenever a quantity is tracked through a network with changing cross-section, well beyond fluids narrowly defined.
06 Weeks 6–7: mass and energy — continuity and Bernoulli

Continuity is the mass ledger from Section 01 made concrete: for steady flow through a control volume with one inlet and one outlet, $\rho_1A_1V_1=\rho_2A_2V_2$, and for an incompressible liquid the density cancels, leaving $A_1V_1=A_2V_2$. This single equation is why velocity increases wherever a pipe narrows, and it is the first thing you check in almost every later question, because it is usually what lets you eliminate one unknown before the harder ledger is even opened.

The Bernoulli equation is the energy ledger for the special, friction-free, no-machinery case: along a streamline, $\dfrac{p}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V^2}{2g}+z=\text{constant}$. It is not a separate law — it is what the general energy equation reduces to once pump work, turbine work and head loss are all set to zero. Week 7 puts those terms back in: $\dfrac{p_1}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1+h_p=\dfrac{p_2}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_t+h_L$, where $h_p$ is the head added by a pump, $h_t$ the head extracted by a turbine, and $h_L$ the head lost to friction and fittings between the two points. Pump and turbine efficiency converts between this hydraulic head and the electrical or mechanical power actually consumed or produced.

Quick example

Water leaves a reservoir and drops 40 m in elevation to a turbine, with 6 m of that head lost to friction in the penstock along the way. Bernoulli by itself would credit the turbine with the full 40 m; the energy equation from Week 7 correctly leaves only $40-6=34$ m of head available to extract, and that six-metre difference is exactly what the friction calculations of Weeks 9–10 exist to quantify.

Engineering application
  • Slurry and dewatering pump sizing (mining). Selecting a pump for a tailings line or a dewatering circuit is solving the energy equation for $h_p$, then reading the corresponding duty point off a pump curve.
  • Site water reticulation head loss (mining and general). Any elevation profile — water pumped up a haul road, or a municipal supply climbing a hill — is the same energy-equation bookkeeping between two elevations.
  • Venturi feeders on a flotation circuit (mining). A venturi accelerates flow by narrowing the pipe, and continuity is the equation that tells you by how much before Bernoulli tells you the resulting pressure drop.
  • Hydro turbines and pico-hydro on site (general). Any small hydro installation, on or off a mine site, is sized using exactly the head-loss-corrected energy equation of Week 7.
07 Week 8: momentum — the vector ledger

Continuity and the energy equation are scalar ledgers: they balance a quantity that has a size but no direction. The linear momentum equation, $\sum\mathbf{F}=\dot m(\mathbf{V}_{out}-\mathbf{V}_{in})$, is the same Reynolds Transport Theorem from Week 5 applied to a vector instead, and it is the only one of the three ledgers that can hand you a force directly. Wherever a flow changes speed or direction — a bend, a reducer, a nozzle, a jet striking a plate or a turbine vane — the fluid has had its momentum changed, and something had to supply the force to change it. Solving for that force, rather than assuming it, is the entire content of the week.

The practical difficulty is bookkeeping rather than concept: choosing a control volume that cuts cleanly through the pipe (so the entering and leaving momentum are easy to write down), resolving everything into components before applying the equation, and being careful that the force the fluid exerts on a solid surface is the reaction to, and therefore opposite in sign to, the force the control-volume analysis directly returns.

Engineering application
  • Thrust blocks on a pipeline bend (mining and general). A slurry or water pipeline elbow tries to straighten itself under flow; the momentum equation gives the force a thrust block or anchor must resist so the joint does not fail.
  • Hydraulic monitors and water cannons (mining). The reaction force on a monitor nozzle used for hydraulic mining or hosing down a stockpile is a direct jet-momentum calculation, and it is what sizes the monitor's mounting.
  • Pelton wheels and small hydro on site (mining and general). The force — and hence torque and power — a jet delivers to a turbine bucket is the momentum equation applied to a moving vane.
  • Launder and chute flushing (mining). The force a flushing jet exerts to clear a blocked launder, and the reaction it imposes on the structure, follow the same analysis as the monitor example above.
08 Weeks 9–10: pipe flow, where friction enters the ledger

Everything up to Week 8 could be solved with algebra once the right ledger was opened. Pipe flow is where the subject admits that real pipes are not frictionless, and the head-loss term $h_L$ left as a placeholder since Week 7 finally gets a formula. The Reynolds number, $Re=\rho VD/\mu$, decides whether the flow is smooth and orderly (laminar, $Re\lesssim2300$) or chaotic (turbulent), and the friction behaviour is qualitatively different in each regime. Major losses along a straight pipe are computed from the Darcy–Weisbach equation, $h_L=f\dfrac{L}{D}\dfrac{V^2}{2g}$, where the friction factor $f$ is read from the Moody chart or computed from the Colebrook equation as a function of $Re$ and relative roughness $\varepsilon/D$. Minor losses at bends, valves and fittings are added the same way, using a loss coefficient $K$ in place of $f L/D$.

Weeks 9–10 also connect this back to Week 7: with $h_L$ now computable, the energy equation can be solved fully for a real pipeline, including finding the operating point where a given pump curve intersects a given system's head-loss curve, and analysing simple pipe networks in series and in parallel.

Engineering application
  • Slurry pipeline design (mining). Long-distance tailings and concentrate pipelines are sized almost entirely from this material, with the friction factor adjusted for non-Newtonian rheology on top of the standard Moody-chart value.
  • Mine dewatering pipeline and pump selection (mining). Matching a pump curve to a rising main's system curve, including static lift and friction loss, is the direct output of Weeks 9–10 combined with Week 7.
  • Ventilation ducting (mining). Airflow through underground ducting follows the same Darcy–Weisbach analysis, with air properties in place of water, and is central to fan selection.
  • Site and municipal water reticulation (general). Any branched water-supply network on or off a mine site is solved with the same series/parallel pipe-network methods introduced here.
09 Week 11: flow measurement — turning Bernoulli into an instrument

Every device in this week is Bernoulli plus continuity, run in reverse: instead of using a known flow rate to find a pressure, you measure a pressure difference and solve for the flow rate. A venturi meter, flow nozzle or orifice meter narrows the pipe deliberately, and continuity guarantees the velocity rises there; Bernoulli then converts that velocity rise into a pressure drop you can measure. Because a real fitting has some friction and some non-uniformity that the ideal Bernoulli result ignores, every one of these devices is corrected with an empirical discharge coefficient $C_d$, so that $Q=C_d A_2\sqrt{\dfrac{2\Delta p}{\rho(1-(A_2/A_1)^2)}}$.

For flow with a free surface rather than in a closed pipe, a weir plays the same role: the height of water flowing over a rectangular or V-notch weir is related to discharge by a formula derived, again, from the same energy argument, and is the standard way open-channel flow is measured on site.

Engineering application
  • Custody and process metering on a tailings line (mining). Venturi and orifice meters are the standard instrument for reporting slurry throughput on a mine site, and the discharge coefficient used is exactly the one derived here.
  • Compliance discharge monitoring (mining and general). A V-notch weir monitoring water leaving a site, for environmental reporting to a regulator, is read directly off the weir formula from this week.
  • Process water flow control (general). Orifice plates used to trim or measure flow in a plant water line follow the identical $C_d$-corrected Bernoulli relationship.
10 Week 12: dimensional analysis — where the correlations came from

Two questions have been quietly deferred since Week 9: where did the Moody chart come from, and where did the discharge coefficients of Week 11 come from? Both are answers to the same underlying problem — friction factor and discharge coefficient cannot be derived from first principles for turbulent flow, so they were measured, and dimensional analysis is what tells you how to plot those measurements so a handful of experiments cover every pipe size, every fluid and every speed at once.

The Buckingham Pi theorem reduces a physical relationship among many dimensional variables to a smaller relationship among dimensionless groups. Applied to pipe flow, it is exactly this theorem that tells you $f$ can only depend on $Re$ and $\varepsilon/D$, and nothing else — which is precisely the two axes the Moody chart is drawn on. The same logic produces the Reynolds number itself, the Froude number (inertia versus gravity, relevant for anything with a free surface), and the drag coefficient. Once two systems match on every relevant dimensionless group — geometric, kinematic and dynamic similarity — a small scale model is guaranteed to behave, dimensionlessly, exactly like the full-size system, which is what makes physical model testing meaningful at all.

Engineering application
  • Physical hydraulic modelling of a spillway or tailings dam (mining and general). A scaled physical model of a spillway or emergency spillway is only meaningful once Froude similarity has been checked, which is a direct application of this week.
  • Cyclone and flotation cell scale-up (mining). Predicting full-scale cyclone or flotation performance from a pilot-scale test relies on matching the dimensionless groups this week defines.
  • Pump testing on water before commissioning on slurry (mining). Pump curves are usually generated on water; converting that curve to predict slurry performance uses similarity and correction factors built on this material.
  • Wind and drag loading on structures (general). Drag coefficients quoted for stockpile covers, conveyor structures or vehicles are the same dimensionless group defined here.

12 What MATH141/142 gave you, and where you will spend it

ENGG252's formal prerequisite is first-year engineering mathematics (MATH142, or an equivalent such as MATH162/MATH188). Nothing new in the way of mathematical technique is introduced this session — the subject is entirely about applying calculus and algebra you already have to a new physical setting, plus one genuinely new skill, dimensional analysis, that belongs to engineering rather than to mathematics proper.

The practical consequence

If solving two simultaneous equations, integrating a linear function of depth, or resolving a vector into components is still slow, ENGG252 will feel harder than it is, because the fluid-mechanics idea in each question is usually simple and the algebra around it is where marks and time are actually lost. Keeping those skills fluent is the highest-value preparation available before Week 1.

13 What you will be able to solve

By the end of the session, given a realistic engineering scenario and no instruction about which method to apply, you should be able to do all of the following.

  • Recognise whether a system is static or moving, and open the correct ledger — mass, momentum or energy — for the quantity actually being asked for.
  • Compute the pressure at a depth, read a manometer, and find the force and centre of pressure on a plane or curved submerged surface.
  • Describe a velocity field, identify streamlines, and compute the acceleration of a fluid particle including its convective part.
  • Apply continuity and the energy equation together to size a pump, including elevation change and friction losses, and state the efficiency required.
  • Apply the momentum equation to find the force a bend, nozzle, jet or vane exerts, or must resist.
  • Compute the friction factor for a given pipe and flow rate, find the head loss, and locate the operating point of a pump against a system curve.
  • Convert a measured pressure drop or weir height into a flow rate using the correct discharge coefficient.
  • Use dimensional analysis to identify the relevant dimensionless groups for a new problem, and judge whether a scale model will predict full-scale behaviour.

Together those steps amount to identifying the right ledger, closing it with the right empirical correction where one is needed, and solving for the quantity a real engineering decision depends on. That is what this subject is training you to do.

Six things to carry into Week 1
  • Everything is the same balance. Draw a control volume, decide whether the ledger is mass, momentum or energy, and the rest is algebra.
  • Hydrostatics is dynamics with the velocity set to zero. It is not a separate topic; it is the special case that shows up whenever nothing is flowing.
  • Bernoulli is the friction-free special case of the energy equation. Learn it first so you can see exactly what Weeks 9–10 add back in.
  • Continuity almost always goes first. It is usually the fastest way to eliminate one unknown before the harder ledger is opened.
  • Momentum is the only ledger that returns a force. If a question asks for a force, you are in Week 8's territory even if it appears later in the session.
  • Dimensional analysis explains the whole subject's empirical constants. The friction factor and every discharge coefficient you used from Week 9 onward exist because of the technique you meet last, in Week 12.
