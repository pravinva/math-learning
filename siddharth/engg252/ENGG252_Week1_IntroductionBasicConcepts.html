0 What Is a Fluid? The Continuum Assumption

A fluid (liquid or gas) is a substance that deforms continuously under any applied shear stress, however small — it cannot sustain a shear stress at rest, unlike a solid, which deforms a fixed amount and then stops. This is the fundamental distinction: pour a liquid into a container of any shape and it keeps flowing until it fills the container's base; a solid retains its own shape.

Liquids have a definite volume but take the shape of their container (nearly incompressible, held together by strong intermolecular forces); gases expand to fill their entire container (compressible, weak intermolecular forces). Fluid mechanics treats fluids as a continuum — a smooth, continuous distribution of matter with no gaps, ignoring the fact that fluids are actually made of discrete molecules. This is valid as long as the length scale of interest is far larger than the mean free path between molecules (true for essentially all engineering-scale flows).

Key idea

$$\text{Fluid: deforms continuously under any shear stress} \qquad \text{Continuum assumption: valid when length scale} \gg \text{molecular mean free path}$$

Worked example

Example. Classify each of the following as exhibiting fluid or solid behaviour under an applied shear force: (a) a steel beam under a side load; (b) water being stirred in a cup; (c) honey slowly flowing off a spoon.

(a) Solid: deforms a small, fixed amount and stops (elastic deformation), returning to its original shape when the load is removed. (b) and (c): Fluid: both continue to deform (flow) for as long as the shear force is applied, never reaching a static deformed shape — honey simply flows more slowly than water due to its much higher viscosity (Week 2), but it is still unambiguously a fluid.

Practice 1

Classify each as a fluid or solid: (a) a rubber band being stretched; (b) air being compressed in a syringe; (c) toothpaste being squeezed from a tube.

Solution

(a) Solid (elastic, returns to shape). (b) Fluid (gas, continues to flow/compress under the applied force, no fixed final shape from the pressure alone). (c) Fluid (a viscous fluid, though a very thick one, it does continuously deform/flow when squeezed).

Practice 2

Explain why a block of steel and a puddle of water on a flat surface behave completely differently under gravity alone, in terms of the fluid/solid distinction.

Solution

Gravity acting on the water's own weight creates a shear stress within the puddle (the fluid's weight tends to spread it sideways along the flat surface), and since a fluid cannot sustain any shear stress at rest, it continues to spread/flow until it reaches a state of zero shear (a thin, flat layer). The steel block, being a solid, can sustain internal shear stresses without deforming, so it simply sits in its original block shape under gravity.

Practice 3

Explain, in terms of intermolecular spacing and forces, why liquids are nearly incompressible while gases are highly compressible.

Solution

In a liquid, molecules are packed closely together, held by strong intermolecular attractive forces, leaving very little free space between them — applying pressure has almost nowhere to "push" the molecules closer together, so volume barely changes (nearly incompressible). In a gas, molecules are widely spaced with weak intermolecular forces, so there is abundant free space between them; applying pressure can significantly reduce the average spacing (and hence the volume) with comparatively little resistance, making gases highly compressible.

Practice 4

A fluid mechanics problem involves flow through a pipe of diameter 20 mm. Explain why the continuum assumption remains valid here, even though the fluid is technically made of discrete molecules with gaps between them.

Solution

The continuum assumption requires the length scale of interest (here, 20 mm) to be vastly larger than the molecular mean free path (the average distance a molecule travels between collisions, which is on the order of nanometres for a liquid and around 68 nm for air at standard conditions). Since 20 mm is many orders of magnitude larger than any molecular length scale, treating the fluid as a smooth, continuous medium (ignoring its discrete molecular nature) introduces negligible error and is entirely appropriate for this problem.

Practice 5

Describe a physical scenario (in general terms) where the continuum assumption might start to break down, even in an engineering context.

Solution

The continuum assumption becomes questionable for flows at extremely small length scales approaching the molecular mean free path — for example, gas flow through nanoscale channels (as in some microelectromechanical systems, MEMS) or extremely low-pressure/rarefied gas flow (as in high-altitude aerodynamics or vacuum systems), where the gap between molecules becomes comparable to the physical dimensions of the flow passage itself.

1 Dimensions, Units & Dimensional Homogeneity

Every physical quantity has a dimension (a fundamental type of measurement, e.g. length $[L]$, mass $[M]$, time $[T]$) and a unit (a specific scale for measuring that dimension, e.g. metres, kilograms, seconds in SI). This subject uses SI units throughout: length in m, mass in kg, time in s, force in N ($=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$, from Newton's second law), pressure in Pa ($=\text{N/m}^2$), and energy in J ($=\text{N}\cdot\text{m}$).

Dimensional homogeneity: every valid physical equation must have the same dimensions on both sides (and every term added together must share the same dimensions) — a powerful, quick way to check whether a derived or remembered formula could possibly be correct, and a starting point for dimensional analysis (Week 12).

Key idea

$$[F]=[M][L][T]^{-2} \quad (\text{N}=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2) \qquad \text{Dimensional homogeneity: every additive term in a valid equation shares the same dimensions}$$

Worked example

Example. Check the dimensional homogeneity of Bernoulli's equation (a preview of Week 6): $P+\dfrac12\rho V^2+\rho gz=\text{constant}$.

$[P]=\text{Pa}=\text{N/m}^2=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$. $[\rho V^2]=(\text{kg/m}^3)(\text{m/s})^2=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$. $[\rho gz]=(\text{kg/m}^3)(\text{m/s}^2)(\text{m})=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$.

All three terms share the same dimensions ($\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$, equivalent to Pa) — the equation is dimensionally homogeneous, as any correct physical equation must be.

Practice 6

State the SI base units for mass, length, and time.

Solution

Mass: kilogram (kg). Length: metre (m). Time: second (s).

Practice 7

Show that 1 Pa $=$ 1 N/m$^2$ is dimensionally equivalent to kg$\cdot$m$^{-1}\cdot$s$^{-2}$.

Solution

$1\ \text{N}=1\ \text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2$ (from $F=ma$). So $1\ \text{Pa}=1\ \text{N/m}^2=\dfrac{\text{kg}\cdot\text{m/s}^2}{\text{m}^2}=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$.

Practice 8

Check whether the formula $Q=A\sqrt{2g h}$ (a flow-rate formula previewing Week 11, with $Q$ in m$^3$/s, $A$ in m$^2$, $g$ in m/s$^2$, $h$ in m) is dimensionally homogeneous.

Solution

$[A\sqrt{2gh}]=\text{m}^2\cdot\sqrt{(\text{m/s}^2)(\text{m})}=\text{m}^2\cdot\sqrt{\text{m}^2/\text{s}^2}=\text{m}^2\cdot(\text{m/s})=\text{m}^3/\text{s}=[Q]$. Dimensionally homogeneous. ✓

Practice 9

A student proposes the formula $\Delta P=\rho V$ (density times velocity) for a pressure drop. Check whether this is dimensionally homogeneous with $[\Delta P]=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$.

Solution

$[\rho V]=(\text{kg/m}^3)(\text{m/s})=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-2}\cdot\text{s}^{-1}$, which does NOT match $[\Delta P]=\text{kg}\cdot\text{m}^{-1}\cdot\text{s}^{-2}$. The formula is dimensionally inconsistent and therefore cannot be a correct general physical relationship as written.

Practice 10

Convert a pressure of 2.5 bar to Pa (1 bar $=10^5$ Pa), then to kPa.

Solution

$2.5\ \text{bar}=2.5\times10^5\ \text{Pa}=250\ \text{kPa}$.

Practice 11

A flow rate is given as 500 L/min. Convert this to m$^3$/s (1 L $=10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$).

Solution

$500\ \text{L/min}=500\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3/60\ \text{s}=0.5/60=8.33\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3/\text{s}$.

Practice 12

Explain why dimensional homogeneity is a necessary (but not sufficient) condition for an equation to be physically correct, giving an example of a dimensionally consistent but physically wrong equation.

Solution

Dimensional homogeneity only confirms that the units/dimensions match up correctly on both sides of an equation — it says nothing about whether the numerical coefficients or the underlying physics are actually correct. For instance, the equation $F=2ma$ is dimensionally homogeneous (both sides have dimensions of force), yet it is physically wrong (missing the correct coefficient of 1, per Newton's second law $F=ma$) — dimensional homogeneity is a useful sanity check that can catch certain errors, but passing it does not guarantee an equation is correct.

2 Density, Specific Weight & Specific Gravity

Density $\rho=\dfrac{m}{V}$ (kg/m$^3$) is mass per unit volume — the single most fundamental fluid property, appearing in nearly every formula in this subject. Water at 4°C has $\rho=1000\ \text{kg/m}^3$ (the reference value used throughout this subject unless stated otherwise); air at standard conditions has $\rho\approx1.2\ \text{kg/m}^3$, roughly 800 times less dense.

Specific weight $\gamma=\rho g$ (N/m$^3$) is weight per unit volume — simply density scaled by gravitational acceleration. Specific gravity (relative density) $SG=\dfrac{\rho}{\rho_{\text{water}}}$ is a dimensionless ratio comparing a fluid's density to that of water at 4°C — a convenient way to quickly judge whether a substance will float or sink in water ($SG<1$: floats; $SG>1$: sinks).

Key idea

$$\rho=\frac{m}{V} \qquad \gamma=\rho g \qquad SG=\frac{\rho}{\rho_{\text{water}}} \qquad \rho_{\text{water}}=1000\ \text{kg/m}^3\ \text{(at 4°C, reference value)}$$

Worked example

Example. Mercury has $SG=13.6$. Find its density and specific weight.

$\rho_{\text{Hg}}=SG\times\rho_{\text{water}}=13.6\times1000=13600\ \text{kg/m}^3$.

$\gamma_{\text{Hg}}=\rho g=13600(9.81)=133{,}416\ \text{N/m}^3\approx133.4\ \text{kN/m}^3$.

Practice 13

A fluid has a mass of 850 kg occupying a volume of 1 m$^3$. Find its density and specific gravity.

Solution

$\rho=850/1=850\ \text{kg/m}^3$. $SG=850/1000=0.85$.

Practice 14

A tank holds 2 m$^3$ of oil with $SG=0.88$. Find the mass of the oil.

Solution

$\rho=0.88\times1000=880\ \text{kg/m}^3$. $m=\rho V=880(2)=1760$ kg.

Practice 15

Find the specific weight of a fluid with density 950 kg/m$^3$ (use $g=9.81\ \text{m/s}^2$).

Solution

$\gamma=950(9.81)=9319.5\ \text{N/m}^3\approx9.32\ \text{kN/m}^3$.

Practice 16

A substance has specific gravity 0.7. Will it float or sink in water? Find its density.

Solution

$SG<1\Rightarrow$ floats. $\rho=0.7\times1000=700\ \text{kg/m}^3$.

Practice 17

A container of volume 0.5 m$^3$ is filled with a liquid weighing 4000 N. Find the liquid's density.

Solution

$m=W/g=4000/9.81=407.75$ kg. $\rho=m/V=407.75/0.5=815.5\ \text{kg/m}^3$.

Practice 18

Two liquids, A ($SG=0.8$) and B ($SG=1.2$), are placed in the same container without mixing. Explain which liquid will be on top and why, referencing density.

Solution

Liquid A (lower density, $SG=0.8<1.2$) will float on top of liquid B, since a lower-density fluid experiences a smaller downward gravitational force per unit volume than the denser fluid below it, so under gravity the system settles into the stable configuration with the lighter fluid on top — the same principle underlying buoyancy and stratification (explored further in Week 3).

Practice 19

Explain why specific gravity is a more convenient quantity than density alone for quickly comparing many different fluids or materials.

Solution

Specific gravity is a dimensionless ratio, always referenced to the same standard (water at 4°C), so a single number (e.g. $SG=13.6$ for mercury, $SG=0.7$ for petrol) immediately conveys how much denser or lighter a substance is relative to water, without needing to recall or look up water's exact density value each time, and without carrying along units. This makes specific gravity especially convenient for quick mental comparisons and for engineering tables that list many different fluids side by side.

3 System vs Control Volume; the Engineering Problem-Solving Approach

A system is a fixed, identifiable quantity of matter that you "follow" as it moves and deforms (the Lagrangian viewpoint) — useful conceptually but awkward for flowing fluids, since the matter is constantly moving through space. A control volume is instead a fixed (or sometimes moving) region of space through which fluid flows freely across its boundary, the control surface (the Eulerian viewpoint) — almost always the more practical choice for analysing fluid flow, and the basis for the mass, momentum, and energy equations covered in Weeks 6–8.

A consistent engineering problem-solving approach throughout this subject: (1) sketch the physical situation and define an appropriate system or control volume; (2) list known and required quantities with correct units; (3) state governing equations and assumptions explicitly; (4) solve algebraically before substituting numbers; (5) check the answer's units, order of magnitude, and physical reasonableness.

Key idea

$$\text{System: fixed matter, moves with the flow (Lagrangian)} \qquad \text{Control volume: fixed region of space, fluid flows through it (Eulerian)}$$

Worked example

Example. Water flows steadily through a garden hose. Describe an appropriate control volume for analysing the flow rate through the hose, and contrast this with the (impractical) system approach.

Control volume approach: define a fixed region of space bounded by the hose's inner wall and two cross-sections (inlet and outlet) — water flows freely in one end and out the other, and we track properties (velocity, pressure) at those two fixed cross-sections without needing to follow any particular water molecule.

System approach (impractical here): would require identifying and tracking a specific, fixed parcel of water molecules as it travels the entire length of the hose, continuously deforming and mixing with other parcels — far more complex to define and track than simply monitoring two fixed cross-sections, which is exactly why the control volume approach is standard for essentially all flowing-fluid problems.

Practice 20

Classify each analysis as most naturally suited to a system or control volume approach: (a) tracking a specific weather balloon as it rises through the atmosphere; (b) analysing steady flow through a section of pipe.

Solution

(a) System (following one specific, identifiable object). (b) Control volume (fluid flows freely through a fixed region of space, the pipe section).

Practice 21

Explain why the control volume (Eulerian) approach is almost always preferred over the system (Lagrangian) approach for analysing fluid flow through a pump or turbine.

Solution

A pump or turbine has a fixed physical geometry (housing, blades) through which fluid continuously flows in and out; defining a control volume that coincides with this fixed geometry allows straightforward application of the mass, momentum, and energy equations at the inlet and outlet, without ever needing to track which specific fluid parcels are inside the device at any given instant — tracking a "system" of fluid through a pump would require following it as it deforms, splits, and mixes internally, an unnecessarily complex bookkeeping task compared to simply monitoring the fixed control surface.

Practice 22

List, in order, the five steps of the recommended engineering problem-solving approach for this subject.

Solution

(1) Sketch the situation and define the system/control volume. (2) List known and required quantities with units. (3) State governing equations and assumptions. (4) Solve algebraically before substituting numbers. (5) Check units, order of magnitude, and physical reasonableness of the final answer.

Practice 23

Explain why solving a problem algebraically first (before substituting numerical values) is generally recommended, rather than substituting numbers into each equation as soon as they become available.

Solution

Solving algebraically first often reveals that certain quantities cancel out entirely (simplifying the final formula and reducing the amount of arithmetic and rounding-error accumulation), makes the underlying physical relationship and its dependence on each variable clearer and easier to check for errors or sanity, and makes it far easier to verify dimensional homogeneity of the final symbolic expression before any specific numbers are involved — catching an error at the algebraic stage is much easier than tracing it back through a chain of already-substituted numerical calculations.

Practice 24

A student calculates a pipe flow velocity and obtains an answer of 4500 m/s. Explain why this result should immediately be flagged as suspicious, using the "check physical reasonableness" step of the problem-solving approach.

Solution

Typical liquid flow velocities in engineering piping systems are on the order of 1–10 m/s (limited by practical considerations like erosion, pressure loss, and pump capability); a result of 4500 m/s (faster than the speed of sound in many gases, and utterly unrealistic for liquid pipe flow) is many orders of magnitude outside any physically sensible range, strongly suggesting an arithmetic error, a units conversion mistake, or an incorrect formula was used somewhere in the solution, and should prompt the student to re-check their work before accepting the result.
