0 Mechanical Energy & Efficiency

Mechanical energy is the portion of a fluid's total energy that can be freely converted to useful mechanical work by an ideal device (pump or turbine): $e_{\text{mech}}=\dfrac{P}{\rho}+\dfrac{V^2}2+gz$ (per unit mass) — exactly the three terms appearing in Bernoulli's equation (Week 6), now interpreted explicitly as flow, kinetic, and potential energy respectively, rather than pressure/velocity/elevation.

A pump adds mechanical energy to a fluid (using shaft work input, from an electric motor); a turbine extracts mechanical energy from a fluid (converting it to shaft work output, e.g. driving a generator). Real machines are never perfectly efficient: pump efficiency $\eta_{\text{pump}}=\dfrac{\dot W_{\text{fluid}}}{\dot W_{\text{shaft,in}}}$ (useful mechanical energy actually gained by the fluid, divided by the shaft power supplied) is always $<1$ (some input energy is lost to friction, heat, etc. within the pump itself); turbine efficiency $\eta_{\text{turbine}}=\dfrac{\dot W_{\text{shaft,out}}}{\dot W_{\text{fluid}}}$ (useful shaft power extracted, divided by the mechanical energy actually given up by the fluid) is likewise always $<1$.

Key idea

$$e_{\text{mech}}=\frac{P}{\rho}+\frac{V^2}2+gz \qquad \eta_{\text{pump}}=\frac{\dot W_{\text{fluid}}}{\dot W_{\text{shaft,in}}}<1 \qquad \eta_{\text{turbine}}=\frac{\dot W_{\text{shaft,out}}}{\dot W_{\text{fluid}}}<1$$

Worked example

Example. A pump receives 15 kW of shaft power from a motor and has an efficiency of 78%. Find the mechanical power actually delivered to the fluid.

$\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=\eta_{\text{pump}}\dot W_{\text{shaft,in}}=0.78(15)=11.7\ \text{kW}$ (the remaining $15-11.7=3.3$ kW is lost, mostly to internal friction and turbulence within the pump, ultimately dissipated as heat).

Practice 1

A pump has shaft power input of 20 kW and efficiency 82%. Find the mechanical power delivered to the fluid.

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=0.82(20)=16.4$ kW.

Practice 2

A turbine receives 50 kW of mechanical energy from the fluid, and delivers 44 kW of shaft power. Find the turbine efficiency.

Solution

$\eta_{\text{turbine}}=44/50=0.88=88\%$.

Practice 3

A pump delivers 12 kW to the fluid and has an efficiency of 75%. Find the required shaft power input.

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{shaft,in}}=\dot W_{\text{fluid}}/\eta=12/0.75=16$ kW.

Practice 4

A hydroelectric turbine extracts 200 kW of mechanical energy from flowing water and has an efficiency of 90%. Find the electrical power output (assuming the generator itself is perfectly efficient, for simplicity).

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{shaft,out}}=0.90(200)=180$ kW.

Practice 5

A pump-motor combination has overall efficiency 70% (motor efficiency 90%, pump efficiency unknown). Find the pump's own efficiency.

Solution

Overall $=\eta_{\text{motor}}\times\eta_{\text{pump}}\Rightarrow0.70=0.90\times\eta_{\text{pump}}\Rightarrow\eta_{\text{pump}}=0.70/0.90=0.778=77.8\%$.

Practice 6

Explain, in terms of where energy actually goes, why a pump's efficiency is always less than 100%, referencing internal friction and heat.

Solution

As fluid passes through a real pump, it inevitably experiences internal friction (between the fluid and the pump's internal surfaces, and internal viscous friction within the fluid itself as it is churned and redirected by the impeller), as well as other losses such as leakage and mechanical friction in bearings/seals. All of these effects convert a portion of the shaft work input into heat (and other non-useful forms) rather than useful mechanical energy delivered to the fluid stream, meaning the fluid always receives strictly less mechanical energy than the shaft work supplied — making $\eta_{\text{pump}}<1$ an unavoidable physical reality for any real machine, not merely an engineering imperfection that could theoretically be eliminated.

Practice 7

Explain why mechanical energy, as defined in this section (pressure/flow, kinetic, and potential energy), specifically EXCLUDES internal (thermal) energy, even though total energy conservation (the first law of thermodynamics) does include it.

Solution

Mechanical energy is specifically defined as the portion of a fluid's energy that is directly and freely convertible to useful shaft work by an ideal, frictionless device — pressure, kinetic, and potential energy can all, in principle, be fully converted to mechanical work with no fundamental thermodynamic restriction (unlike, say, converting heat to work, which is fundamentally limited by the second law of thermodynamics/Carnot efficiency). Internal (thermal) energy, by contrast, cannot be freely and completely converted to useful mechanical work even in an idealised device; it represents energy that has effectively been "downgraded" (via friction, viscous dissipation) into a form no longer available for useful mechanical work, which is precisely why mechanical energy losses (like pump/turbine inefficiency and pipe friction losses, Weeks 9-10) actually represent mechanical energy converting into this excluded internal-energy category.

1 The General Energy Equation

The general energy equation extends Bernoulli's equation (Week 6) to real flows, explicitly including pump work added, turbine work extracted, and irreversible head loss due to friction: $$\dfrac{P_1}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1+h_{\text{pump}}=\dfrac{P_2}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_{\text{turbine}}+h_L$$ (head form, all terms in metres), where $h_{\text{pump}}=\dot W_{\text{pump,fluid}}/(\dot mg)$, $h_{\text{turbine}}=\dot W_{\text{turbine,fluid}}/(\dot mg)$, and $h_L$ is the total head loss (Weeks 9–10) between points 1 and 2.

This single equation is the workhorse of virtually all practical pipe-system and pump/turbine-selection problems: it reduces exactly to Bernoulli's equation when $h_{\text{pump}}=h_{\text{turbine}}=h_L=0$ (Week 6's idealised, frictionless, no-machine case), and it is simply an energy balance (Week 1's problem-solving approach) applied along a flow path between any two points of interest.

Key idea

$$\frac{P_1}{\rho g}+\frac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1+h_{\text{pump}}=\frac{P_2}{\rho g}+\frac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_{\text{turbine}}+h_L$$

Worked example

Example. Water is pumped from a lower reservoir (surface at $z_1=0$, atmospheric, negligible velocity) to a higher reservoir (surface at $z_2=25$ m, atmospheric, negligible velocity) through a pipe with a total head loss of 3 m. Find the pump head required.

Both surfaces: $P_1=P_2=P_{\text{atm}}$ (cancel), $V_1\approx V_2\approx0$ (large reservoir surfaces). Energy equation: $z_1+h_{\text{pump}}=z_2+h_L\Rightarrow h_{\text{pump}}=z_2-z_1+h_L=25-0+3=28\ \text{m}$.

Practice 8

Water is pumped from a reservoir at $z_1=0$ to a tank at $z_2=15$ m, both surfaces atmospheric with negligible velocity, total head loss 2 m. Find the required pump head.

Solution

$h_{\text{pump}}=z_2-z_1+h_L=15+2=17$ m.

Practice 9

Find the pump power required for the previous question, if the volume flow rate is 0.05 m$^3$/s (use $\dot W_{\text{pump}}=\rho gQh_{\text{pump}}$).

Solution

$\dot W=1000(9.81)(0.05)(17)=8335\ \text{W}=8.34$ kW.

Practice 10

If the pump in the previous two questions has an efficiency of 75%, find the required shaft power input.

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{shaft}}=\dot W_{\text{fluid}}/\eta=8.34/0.75=11.1$ kW.

Practice 11

Water flows from a tank ($z_1=10$ m, atmospheric, negligible velocity) through a turbine to a downstream point ($z_2=0$, atmospheric, velocity 2 m/s), with a head loss of 1 m. Find the turbine head extracted.

Solution

Energy equation: $z_1=\dfrac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2+h_{\text{turbine}}+h_L\Rightarrow h_{\text{turbine}}=z_1-z_2-\dfrac{V_2^2}{2g}-h_L=10-0-\dfrac{4}{19.62}-1=10-0.204-1=8.80$ m.

Practice 12

For the turbine in the previous question, find the power extracted if the volume flow rate is 0.3 m$^3$/s.

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{turbine,fluid}}=\rho gQh_{\text{turbine}}=1000(9.81)(0.3)(8.80)=25{,}890\ \text{W}=25.9$ kW.

Practice 13

Explain why the general energy equation reduces exactly to Bernoulli's equation when there is no pump, no turbine, and no friction loss between the two points of interest, referencing the terms that vanish.

Solution

Setting $h_{\text{pump}}=0$ (no pump work added), $h_{\text{turbine}}=0$ (no turbine work extracted), and $h_L=0$ (no friction/irreversible losses) in the general energy equation leaves exactly $\dfrac{P_1}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_1^2}{2g}+z_1=\dfrac{P_2}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V_2^2}{2g}+z_2$ — precisely Bernoulli's equation (in head form) from Week 6. This confirms that Bernoulli's equation is simply the special, idealised case of the more general energy equation, valid only when no machines are present and friction losses are genuinely negligible between the two points being compared.

Practice 14

Explain, conceptually, why the general energy equation places $h_{\text{pump}}$ on the LEFT side (with point 1) and $h_{\text{turbine}}$ and $h_L$ on the RIGHT side (with point 2), rather than all being on the same side.

Solution

The energy equation is fundamentally an energy balance stating that the total mechanical energy at the starting point PLUS any energy added along the way must equal the total mechanical energy at the ending point PLUS any energy removed or lost along the way. A pump ADDS energy to the fluid as it travels from point 1 toward point 2, so it belongs conceptually with the "energy going in" (point 1) side; a turbine REMOVES useful energy, and friction losses also remove/dissipate energy, so both belong with the "energy leaving or being lost" (point 2) side of the balance — the equation's structure directly mirrors the physical direction of each energy transfer as the fluid flows from point 1 to point 2.

2 Head Loss & the Energy/Hydraulic Grade Lines

Head loss $h_L$ represents mechanical energy irreversibly converted to heat by friction (viscous effects at pipe walls and fittings) — always positive, always reduces the total available mechanical energy as flow progresses along a pipe (detailed calculation methods for $h_L$ are the subject of Weeks 9–10; here, the focus is on how to use $h_L$ once known, within the energy equation framework).

The Energy Grade Line (EGL) plots the total head $\dfrac{P}{\rho g}+\dfrac{V^2}{2g}+z$ along the flow path — it always slopes downward in the direction of flow (reflecting continuous head loss), except for a sudden jump up at a pump or down at a turbine. The Hydraulic Grade Line (HGL) plots just $\dfrac{P}{\rho g}+z$ (i.e. EGL minus the velocity head) — physically, the height water would rise to in a small vertical tube (piezometer) tapped into the pipe at that point. HGL is always below EGL by exactly the velocity head $V^2/2g$, and the two coincide only where velocity is negligible (e.g. at a large reservoir surface).

Key idea

$$\text{EGL}=\frac{P}{\rho g}+\frac{V^2}{2g}+z \quad \text{(always slopes down with flow, except jumps at pumps/turbines)} \qquad \text{HGL}=\frac{P}{\rho g}+z=\text{EGL}-\frac{V^2}{2g}$$

Worked example

Example. A pipe carries water at a constant velocity of 3 m/s. At a certain point, the EGL is at elevation 40 m. Find the HGL elevation at that same point.

Velocity head $=\dfrac{V^2}{2g}=\dfrac{3^2}{2(9.81)}=0.459\ \text{m}$.

HGL $=$ EGL $-\ V^2/2g=40-0.459=39.54\ \text{m}$.

Practice 15

A pipe carries water at 4 m/s. At a point where the EGL is at elevation 55 m, find the HGL elevation.

Solution

$V^2/2g=16/19.62=0.815$ m. HGL $=55-0.815=54.19$ m.

Practice 16

Between two points along a pipe (no pump or turbine present), the EGL drops from 60 m to 57 m. Find the head loss between these points.

Solution

$h_L=60-57=3$ m (the EGL drop directly equals the head loss, whenever no pump/turbine is present between the two points).

Practice 17

A pipe has constant diameter throughout its length (so velocity, and hence velocity head, is constant everywhere). Explain what this implies about the relationship between the EGL and HGL slopes.

Solution

Since the velocity head $V^2/2g$ is constant everywhere along a constant-diameter pipe, HGL $=$ EGL $-$ (a constant amount) at every point; this means the HGL and EGL are always parallel to each other along such a pipe, separated everywhere by exactly that same constant vertical distance, and both slope downward at the identical rate (matching the rate of head loss per unit length).

Practice 18

At a pump located along a pipeline, the EGL jumps sharply upward by 20 m. Explain what physical quantity this jump represents.

Solution

The upward jump in the EGL at a pump represents the pump head $h_{\text{pump}}=20$ m — the mechanical energy (per unit weight of fluid) added to the flow by the pump, exactly cancelling and then exceeding the head loss and elevation gain that would otherwise cause the EGL to only decrease.

Practice 19

Explain why the HGL can sometimes dip BELOW the physical pipe centreline elevation at certain points along a pipeline (e.g. where the pipe rises over a hill), and why this situation is a potential concern for engineers.

Solution

The HGL represents $P/(\rho g)+z$; if the HGL at some point falls below the actual pipe elevation $z$ at that point, this would require $P/(\rho g)$ to be negative, i.e. the local absolute pressure would need to drop below atmospheric (a vacuum condition) or even become physically impossible (below zero absolute) if the deficit were large enough. This is a genuine concern in pipeline design — if the HGL dips too far below the pipe's physical elevation at a high point (e.g. where a pipeline crests a hill), it can indicate sub-atmospheric pressure in the pipe there, risking air/vapor release, pipe collapse (for thin-walled pipes not designed for external vacuum loading), or cavitation-type issues, and must be checked carefully during pipeline route and pump design.

3 Applications: Pump & Turbine Power Calculations

Combining every tool from this week: the general energy equation (Section 1) determines the required pump head or available turbine head for a given piping system; multiplying by $\rho gQ$ converts head to fluid power ($\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=\rho gQh$); and dividing (for a pump) or multiplying (for a turbine) by the appropriate efficiency (Section 0) gives the actual shaft power required or delivered — the complete chain of calculation used to specify a pump motor size or predict a turbine's electrical output in real engineering practice.

This full chain — energy equation $\to$ head $\to$ fluid power $\to$ shaft power (via efficiency) — represents the practical culmination of everything covered in Weeks 6–7, and is exactly the calculation sequence used in real pump/turbine selection and system design throughout the engineering industry.

Key idea

$$h \ \text{(energy equation)} \to \dot W_{\text{fluid}}=\rho gQh \to \dot W_{\text{shaft}}=\frac{\dot W_{\text{fluid}}}{\eta_{\text{pump}}} \ \text{or} \ \dot W_{\text{shaft}}=\eta_{\text{turbine}}\dot W_{\text{fluid}}$$

Worked example

Example. A pump moves water at 0.08 m$^3$/s from a lower reservoir ($z_1=0$) to an upper reservoir ($z_2=20$ m), both atmospheric with negligible velocity, total head loss 4 m. Pump efficiency is 72%. Find the required electric motor (shaft) power.

Energy equation: $h_{\text{pump}}=z_2-z_1+h_L=20+4=24$ m. Fluid power: $\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=\rho gQh_{\text{pump}}=1000(9.81)(0.08)(24)=18{,}829\ \text{W}=18.83$ kW.

Shaft power: $\dot W_{\text{shaft}}=\dot W_{\text{fluid}}/\eta_{\text{pump}}=18.83/0.72=26.2\ \text{kW}$ — this is the motor size the engineer would need to specify.

Practice 20

A pump moves water at 0.1 m$^3$/s, required pump head 15 m, efficiency 80%. Find the fluid power and the required shaft power.

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=1000(9.81)(0.1)(15)=14{,}715\ \text{W}=14.7$ kW. $\dot W_{\text{shaft}}=14.7/0.80=18.4$ kW.

Practice 21

A hydroelectric turbine operates with a net head of 30 m, volume flow rate 5 m$^3$/s, and efficiency 92%. Find the fluid power and the electrical (shaft) power output.

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=1000(9.81)(5)(30)=1{,}471{,}500\ \text{W}=1471.5$ kW. $\dot W_{\text{shaft}}=0.92(1471.5)=1353.8$ kW.

Practice 22

A pump system requires a shaft power of 25 kW, and the pump has efficiency 70%. Find the mechanical power actually delivered to the fluid, and then (if the flow rate is 0.06 m$^3$/s) the resulting pump head.

Solution

$\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=0.70(25)=17.5$ kW. $h_{\text{pump}}=\dot W_{\text{fluid}}/(\rho gQ)=17500/(1000\times9.81\times0.06)=29.7$ m.

Practice 23

A water supply system pumps 0.15 m$^3$/s from a reservoir (elevation 0) to a tank (elevation 35 m), with a total head loss of 6 m. If the pump efficiency is 75% and electricity costs $0.25/kWh, find the required shaft power and the hourly running cost.

Solution

$h_{\text{pump}}=35+6=41$ m. $\dot W_{\text{fluid}}=1000(9.81)(0.15)(41)=60{,}327\ \text{W}=60.33$ kW. $\dot W_{\text{shaft}}=60.33/0.75=80.4$ kW. Hourly cost $=80.4\times0.25=\$20.11$/hour.

Practice 24

Explain the complete calculation chain (in words, listing each step) an engineer would follow to determine the required electric motor size for a new pump installation, starting from knowing the piping layout, flow rate requirement, and estimated head loss.

Solution

(1) Apply the general energy equation between the source and destination points (accounting for elevation change, any pressure difference, and the estimated head loss) to find the required pump head $h_{\text{pump}}$. (2) Multiply by $\rho gQ$ (using the required flow rate) to find the fluid power $\dot W_{\text{fluid}}$ that must actually be delivered to the water. (3) Divide by the pump's known (or estimated, from manufacturer data) efficiency $\eta_{\text{pump}}$ to find the required shaft power $\dot W_{\text{shaft}}$. (4) Select an electric motor rated at or above this shaft power (often with an additional safety margin), completing the sizing process — exactly the chain demonstrated in this section's worked example.
