0 Hydrostatic Force on a Plane Surface

A submerged (or partially submerged) flat surface experiences a distributed hydrostatic pressure that increases linearly with depth (Week 3). The resultant force equals the pressure at the centroid of the surface, multiplied by the surface's total area: $F_R=P_Ch_C A=\rho gh_CA$ (gauge pressure), where $h_C$ is the vertical depth of the surface's centroid below the free surface.

This works because the average of a linearly varying quantity over a plane area equals the value at the centroid — a direct consequence of the definition of a centroid, regardless of the surface's shape or orientation (vertical, horizontal, or inclined).

Key idea

$$F_R=\rho gh_CA \qquad h_C=\text{vertical depth of the surface’s centroid below the free surface}$$

Worked example

Example. A vertical rectangular gate, width 2 m, height 3 m, has its top edge at the free surface of water. Find the resultant hydrostatic force on the gate.

Centroid depth $h_C=3/2=1.5$ m (midheight of the gate, since the top is at the surface). $A=2(3)=6\ \text{m}^2$.

$F_R=\rho gh_CA=1000(9.81)(1.5)(6)=88{,}290\ \text{N}=88.29$ kN.

Practice 1

A vertical circular gate, diameter 1.2 m, has its centre 2 m below the water surface. Find the resultant force.

Solution

$A=\pi(0.6)^2=1.131\ \text{m}^2$. $F_R=1000(9.81)(2)(1.131)=22{,}190\ \text{N}=22.19$ kN.

Practice 2

A vertical rectangular gate, width 1.5 m, height 2 m, has its top edge 1 m below the water surface. Find the resultant force.

Solution

Centroid depth $h_C=1+2/2=2$ m. $A=1.5(2)=3\ \text{m}^2$. $F_R=1000(9.81)(2)(3)=58{,}860\ \text{N}=58.86$ kN.

Practice 3

A horizontal rectangular plate, 2 m × 3 m, lies at a depth of 4 m in water. Find the resultant force (note: for a horizontal surface, pressure is uniform, so $h_C$ is simply the plate's own depth).

Solution

$A=6\ \text{m}^2$. $F_R=1000(9.81)(4)(6)=235{,}440\ \text{N}=235.4$ kN.

Practice 4

An inclined rectangular gate (width 2 m, length along the incline 3 m) is set at 40° to the horizontal, with its centroid at a vertical depth of 2.5 m. Find the resultant force.

Solution

Note that the inclination angle affects the area's geometry but the force formula still only needs the vertical centroid depth: $A=2(3)=6\ \text{m}^2$. $F_R=1000(9.81)(2.5)(6)=147{,}150\ \text{N}=147.15$ kN.

Practice 5

A dam face is vertical, 20 m wide, holding back water to a depth of 8 m. Find the resultant hydrostatic force on this section of the dam.

Solution

Centroid depth $h_C=8/2=4$ m. $A=20(8)=160\ \text{m}^2$. $F_R=1000(9.81)(4)(160)=6{,}278{,}400\ \text{N}\approx6.28$ MN.

Practice 6

Explain why the resultant hydrostatic force on a submerged plane surface can be computed using only the centroid depth (a single point), even though pressure varies continuously (linearly) across the entire surface.

Solution

The resultant force is the integral of pressure over the entire area, $F_R=\int P\,dA$. Since gauge pressure varies linearly with depth ($P=\rho gh$), and the centroid of an area is defined precisely as the point where the average value of a linear function integrated over that area equals the function's value at that single point, the integral $\int\rho gh\,dA$ simplifies exactly to $\rho gh_CA$ — the centroid depth captures the true area-averaged pressure automatically, without needing to perform the full integral explicitly for each new problem.

Practice 7

Explain why the resultant force formula $F_R=\rho gh_CA$ works identically for vertical, horizontal, AND inclined submerged plane surfaces, as long as $h_C$ is correctly measured as the VERTICAL depth of the centroid (not the distance along an inclined surface).

Solution

Pressure at any point in the fluid depends only on the point's true vertical depth below the free surface (the hydrostatic equation, Week 3), regardless of the orientation of any submerged surface passing through that point. So regardless of whether the plane surface itself is vertical, horizontal, or tilted at any angle, the pressure distribution across it is still governed purely by each point's vertical depth, and the resultant force still equals that same area-averaged pressure (evaluated using the vertical centroid depth $h_C$) times the total area — the surface's own orientation affects how the area and centroid are measured geometrically, but not the fundamental force formula itself.

1 Centre of Pressure

The resultant force does not act at the centroid itself, but at the centre of pressure — located below the centroid (deeper), since pressure (and hence the "weighting" of the force distribution) is greater at greater depth. For a plane surface inclined at angle $\theta$ to the horizontal: $y_R=y_C+\dfrac{I_C}{y_CA}$, where $y$ is measured along the inclined surface from the free-surface level, $y_C$ is the centroid's position along the surface, and $I_C$ is the surface's own second moment of area about its centroidal axis (parallel to the free surface line).

For a vertical rectangular surface, this simplifies (using $I_C=\dfrac{bh^3}{12}$ for a rectangle) to a clean, frequently-used result: the centre of pressure is always below the centroid by $\dfrac{I_C}{y_CA}$, with the offset becoming smaller (as a fraction of depth) the deeper the surface is submerged — deeply submerged surfaces have an almost uniform pressure distribution, so the centre of pressure approaches the centroid.

Key idea

$$y_R=y_C+\frac{I_C}{y_CA} \qquad \text{(centre of pressure always below the centroid, along the inclined/vertical surface)}$$

Worked example

Example. For the vertical rectangular gate from Section 0's worked example (width 2 m, height 3 m, top edge at the surface), find the location of the centre of pressure.

$y_C=1.5$ m (measured down the vertical surface, same as $h_C$ here since the surface is vertical). $I_C=\dfrac{bh^3}{12}=\dfrac{2(3)^3}{12}=4.5\ \text{m}^4$. $A=6\ \text{m}^2$.

$y_R=1.5+\dfrac{4.5}{1.5(6)}=1.5+0.5=2.0\ \text{m}$ — the centre of pressure is 2.0 m down the gate (0.5 m below the centroid), i.e. two-thirds of the way down the 3 m gate.

Practice 8

For the vertical circular gate from Section 0 (diameter 1.2 m, centroid depth 2 m), find the centre of pressure depth. ($I_C=\pi d^4/64$ for a circle.)

Solution

$I_C=\pi(1.2)^4/64=0.1018\ \text{m}^4$. $A=1.131\ \text{m}^2$. $y_R=2+\dfrac{0.1018}{2(1.131)}=2+0.0450=2.045$ m.

Practice 9

For the vertical rectangular gate from Section 0 (width 1.5 m, height 2 m, top edge 1 m deep, centroid depth 2 m), find the centre of pressure depth.

Solution

$I_C=\dfrac{1.5(2)^3}{12}=1.0\ \text{m}^4$. $A=3\ \text{m}^2$. $y_R=2+\dfrac{1.0}{2(3)}=2+0.1667=2.167$ m.

Practice 10

For the dam face from Section 0 (vertical, 20 m wide, 8 m deep, top edge at the free surface), find the centre of pressure depth using the shortcut $y_R=\dfrac23h$ for a surface with its top at the free surface.

Solution

$y_R=\dfrac23(8)=5.33$ m (this shortcut, derived in the next practice problem, is faster than the general formula whenever the surface's top edge is exactly at the free surface).

Practice 11

For a vertical rectangular surface with its top edge exactly at the free surface (height $h$, so $y_C=h/2$), show that the general formula reduces to the well-known shortcut $y_R=\dfrac23h$.

Solution

$I_C=\dfrac{bh^3}{12}$, $A=bh$, $y_C=h/2$. $y_R=\dfrac h2+\dfrac{bh^3/12}{(h/2)(bh)}=\dfrac h2+\dfrac{h}{6}=\dfrac{3h+h}6=\dfrac{4h}6=\dfrac{2h}3$. ✓ Matches the well-known shortcut for this common special case.

Practice 12

Explain why the centre of pressure is always located BELOW the centroid (never above, and never exactly at the centroid) for any submerged plane surface with a free surface above it.

Solution

Since gauge pressure increases with depth, the lower portions of any submerged surface experience higher local pressure than the upper portions; when computing the effective "pressure-weighted average" location (the centre of pressure), the higher-pressure (deeper) regions contribute disproportionately more to the resultant force's location than the geometric centroid (which weights every part of the area equally, regardless of pressure). This asymmetric weighting always pulls the true force-location downward relative to the purely geometric centroid, which is exactly why the correction term $I_C/(y_CA)$ in the formula is always positive (added to $y_C$), placing the centre of pressure strictly below the centroid.

Practice 13

Explain why the offset between the centre of pressure and the centroid becomes proportionally smaller as a surface is submerged to a much greater depth (i.e. for a very deeply submerged surface, the centre of pressure nearly coincides with the centroid).

Solution

The offset $I_C/(y_CA)$ has $y_C$ in the denominator, so as the centroid depth $y_C$ increases (while $I_C$ and $A$, which depend only on the surface's own fixed geometry, remain unchanged), the offset shrinks. Physically, this reflects that for a very deeply submerged surface, the pressure difference between its top and bottom edges (a fixed geometric height difference) becomes a very small fraction of the very large absolute pressure at that depth — the pressure distribution across the surface becomes nearly uniform in relative terms, so the pressure-weighted centre of pressure converges toward the simple geometric centroid.

2 Hydrostatic Force on Curved Surfaces

For a curved submerged surface, the simple $F_R=\rho gh_CA$ formula (valid only for flat surfaces) no longer applies directly, since the pressure force at each point acts perpendicular to the local surface, which continuously changes direction along a curve. Instead, resolve into horizontal and vertical components separately.

Horizontal component $F_H$: exactly equal to the hydrostatic force on the flat vertical projection of the curved surface (as if the curved surface were "flattened" onto a vertical plane) — compute it using the plane-surface methods from Sections 0–1. Vertical component $F_V$: exactly equal to the weight of fluid (real or imaginary) directly above the curved surface, up to the free surface — acting through the centroid of that volume. The resultant is $F_R=\sqrt{F_H^2+F_V^2}$, at angle $\theta=\tan^{-1}(F_V/F_H)$ from horizontal.

Key idea

$$F_H=\text{force on the vertical projection (flat-surface method)} \qquad F_V=\text{weight of fluid above the curved surface} \qquad F_R=\sqrt{F_H^2+F_V^2}$$

Worked example

Example. A quarter-circle curved gate (radius 2 m) holds back water, with the curve running from the free surface down to the tank floor, curving outward (water on the concave side). Gate width 3 m. Find $F_H$ and $F_V$.

$F_H$: vertical projection is a rectangle, width 3 m, height 2 m (equal to the radius), top at the free surface. Centroid depth $=1$ m. $F_H=\rho gh_CA=1000(9.81)(1)(3\times2)=58{,}860\ \text{N}=58.86$ kN.

$F_V$: weight of the (real, in this concave-toward-water case) water volume directly above the curved surface, which is the quarter-circle cross-section times the width: $V=\dfrac{\pi r^2}{4}\times\text{width}=\dfrac{\pi(2)^2}{4}(3)=9.425\ \text{m}^3$. $F_V=\rho gV=1000(9.81)(9.425)=92{,}460\ \text{N}=92.46$ kN.

Practice 14

For the curved gate in the worked example, find the resultant force magnitude $F_R$.

Solution

$F_R=\sqrt{58.86^2+92.46^2}=\sqrt{3464.5+8548.9}=\sqrt{12{,}013.4}=109.6$ kN.

Practice 15

For the same curved gate, find the angle of the resultant force from the horizontal.

Solution

$\theta=\tan^{-1}(92.46/58.86)=\tan^{-1}(1.571)=57.5\,{}^\circ$.

Practice 16

A curved dam spillway section (quarter-circle, radius 3 m, width 5 m) has water on the convex (outer) side, with the free surface at the top of the curve. Find the horizontal force component (using the vertical projection method, identical logic regardless of concave/convex).

Solution

Vertical projection: rectangle, width 5 m, height 3 m, top at the free surface, centroid depth $1.5$ m. $F_H=1000(9.81)(1.5)(5\times3)=220{,}725\ \text{N}=220.7$ kN.

Practice 17

For a curved surface where water is on the CONVEX side (curving away from the water, an "outward bulging" shape as seen from the water), explain how the vertical force component's DIRECTION differs from the concave case in the worked example, referencing the imaginary vs real fluid volume.

Solution

When water is on the convex side of a curved surface, there is no real fluid directly above the surface within the curved region itself (the surface bulges away from the water); instead, the vertical force component is computed using the weight of an imaginary volume of fluid that would occupy the space between the curved surface and the free surface if that space actually contained fluid. Crucially, the resulting vertical force in this case acts downward on the surface from the fluid's perspective (or equivalently, the surface pushes up on the fluid) — the opposite sense to the concave case, where the real overlying fluid weight pushes directly down on the surface.

Practice 18

A semicircular curved gate (radius 1.5 m, width 4 m) has its diameter along the free surface and curves downward into the water (water on the concave/underside). Find the vertical force component (weight of the real water volume above the curve, a half-cylinder shape).

Solution

$V=\dfrac12\pi r^2\times\text{width}=\dfrac12\pi(1.5)^2(4)=14.14\ \text{m}^3$. $F_V=1000(9.81)(14.14)=138{,}700\ \text{N}=138.7$ kN.

Practice 19

Explain why the horizontal force component on a curved surface can always be found using the SAME flat-plane methods from Sections 0-1 (applied to the vertical projection), regardless of how curved or complex the actual surface shape is.

Solution

The horizontal component of the total hydrostatic force is, by definition, the sum of all the horizontal components of the pressure force acting at each point on the curved surface. Since pressure always acts perpendicular to the local surface, and the horizontal projection of any such perpendicular force depends only on the local surface's vertical projected area (not its specific curved shape), summing all these horizontal contributions across the entire curved surface gives exactly the same result as computing the force on a flat vertical surface that has the identical vertical projected shape and area — the curved surface's true 3D shape becomes irrelevant to the horizontal component once you account for its vertical "shadow" or projection.

3 Applications: Dams, Gates & Design

Practical design of dams, sluice gates, and tank walls requires not just the resultant force, but also checking stability against overturning (does the structure's own weight and any anchoring keep the resultant hydrostatic force's overturning moment, taken about a likely tipping point, safely counteracted?) and structural adequacy at the location of maximum local pressure (usually the very bottom of the submerged surface).

A common design check for a dam or retaining wall: compute the resultant hydrostatic force and its centre of pressure (line of action), then take moments about the toe (the downstream bottom edge, the likely pivot point for overturning) to compare the overturning moment (from the water force) against the restoring moment (from the structure's own weight acting through its centroid) — the structure is safe against overturning only if the restoring moment sufficiently exceeds the overturning moment, typically by a specified factor of safety.

Key idea

$$\text{Overturning moment (hydrostatic force} \times \text{lever arm to pivot)} < \text{Restoring moment (weight} \times \text{lever arm to pivot)} \quad \text{(with a safety factor)}$$

Worked example

Example. A concrete gravity dam section (per metre width) weighs 800 kN, acting through a point 3 m from the downstream toe (the likely overturning pivot). The resultant hydrostatic force is 450 kN, acting at a height of 2.5 m above the base (toe). Check overturning stability (find the factor of safety against overturning).

Overturning moment (about the toe) $=F_R\times(\text{height of action})=450(2.5)=1125\ \text{kN}\cdot\text{m}$.

Restoring moment $=W\times(\text{horizontal lever arm})=800(3)=2400\ \text{kN}\cdot\text{m}$. Factor of safety $=2400/1125=2.13>1$ — stable against overturning, with a reasonable margin.

Practice 20

A retaining wall (per metre width) has a resultant hydrostatic force of 200 kN acting at a height of 1.8 m above the base pivot point. The wall's weight is 350 kN, acting 2 m horizontally from the pivot. Find the factor of safety against overturning.

Solution

Overturning moment $=200(1.8)=360\ \text{kN}\cdot\text{m}$. Restoring moment $=350(2)=700\ \text{kN}\cdot\text{m}$. $FS=700/360=1.94$.

Practice 21

A dam section has an overturning moment of 900 kN·m and a restoring moment of 1200 kN·m. Find the factor of safety against overturning, and comment on whether this would typically be considered adequate (design codes often require $FS\ge1.5$).

Solution

$FS=1200/900=1.33<1.5$ — below the commonly required threshold, so this design would typically be considered inadequate and would need a heavier structure, wider base, or other stabilising modification.

Practice 22

A vertical sluice gate (width 2 m, height 2.5 m, top at the water surface) must be designed to resist the resultant hydrostatic force. Find $F_R$ and the depth of the centre of pressure, using the methods from Sections 0-1.

Solution

$h_C=1.25$ m. $F_R=1000(9.81)(1.25)(2\times2.5)=61{,}313\ \text{N}=61.3$ kN. $y_R=\frac23(2.5)=1.667$ m (using the free-surface-at-top shortcut).

Practice 23

Explain why the depth of the centre of pressure (not just the resultant force magnitude) is critical information for designing a gate's supports (e.g. hinges or anchor points).

Solution

The resultant force's magnitude alone tells you the total load the supports must resist, but the location where that force effectively acts (the centre of pressure) determines the moment arm and hence the actual bending moments/reactions at each support point. A gate hinged at the wrong location relative to the centre of pressure could experience a large unbalanced moment even if the total force is correctly resisted, potentially causing the gate to rotate open (or requiring an additional latch/support specifically positioned to counter that moment) — both the magnitude and the precise location of the resultant force are needed for a complete, safe structural design.

Practice 24

A dam's design is modified to increase the water depth it holds back from 8 m to 10 m (a 25% increase). Explain, using the resultant force formula, why the hydrostatic force increases by MORE than 25%, and find the actual percentage increase.

Solution

For a surface with its top at the free surface, $F_R\propto h_C\times A\propto h\times h=h^2$ (both the centroid depth and the submerged area scale with $h$ for a fixed-width surface extending the full depth). So $F_R\propto h^2$, meaning a 25% increase in depth ($h\to1.25h$) increases the force by a factor of $1.25^2=1.5625$, i.e. a 56.25% increase — substantially more than the 25% depth increase itself, illustrating why dam design is extremely sensitive to water depth and why taller dams require disproportionately much stronger structures.
