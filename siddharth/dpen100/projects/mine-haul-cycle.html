MINE TRUCK PROJECT

A truck driving out of a hole: kinematics in MATLAB

Here is a situation you can hold entirely in your head. A truck full of rock drives up a road out of a very large hole, tips its load out at the top, and rolls back down empty to be filled again. Every single thing you build over the next forty days comes from one question about that picture: how long does one loop take? The pleasure of the project is watching a question that simple turn into five stages of code, a few surprises, and one disagreement with reality that you get to explain.

The situation, and the words that go with it

Let's build up the picture slowly, because there are about a dozen mining words scattered through this project, and every one of them is a plain idea wearing a jacket. Take them one at a time and by the end of this section you will own all of them.

Start with the hole. Some minerals sit close enough to the surface that the cheapest way to reach them is to dig a giant pit and work downwards. That is an open-pit mine — a few hundred metres across, tens of metres deep, and shaped roughly like a cone with its point cut off. Everyone who works there just calls it "the pit".

Now, if you dig a hole with smooth steep walls, the walls slide in on you. So the sides get cut as a giant staircase instead, and each step is called a bench. That is why photographs of these mines always look terraced. The flat area right at the bottom, where the digging is happening, is the pit floor.

Down on the floor there is a large digging machine that scoops broken rock into a truck. Its formal name is an excavator; around a mine you will hear it called a shovel. The rock it is scooping comes in two flavours, and the distinction matters commercially even though it makes zero difference to our physics: ore is rock with enough valuable mineral in it to be worth processing, and waste rock is everything else, which still has to be lifted out of the way.

The truck, once full, has to get from the floor up to the surface. It drives on a dirt road cut into the pit wall, called the haul road — "haul" meaning nothing more than carrying. Here is a question worth pausing on: the wall is steep, but a loaded truck cannot climb anything steep. So how does the road get up?

The same way a mountain road does. It wraps around the inside of the pit, and where it runs out of wall it doubles back on itself in a hairpin bend. That hairpin has a name: a switchback. Our road has one, right in the middle, and it will cause a specific and rather satisfying bug in your code if you are not watching for it.

How steep is "not steep"? Steepness gets quoted as a percentage called the grade. A grade of 8% means that for every 100 metres you travel horizontally, you rise 8 metres. As an angle that is only about 4.6°, which sounds gentle right up until you try to drive 180 tonnes of rock up it. Mine roads live around 8–10%, and ours tops out at 8.24%.

At the top the truck reaches the tipping point — the flat area on the surface where it raises its tray and the rock slides out. From there the rock is often carried onwards by a conveyor, a long rubber belt running on rollers. At the far end of the belt the rubber wraps around a roller and turns back underneath, and the rock, having no reason to turn with it, keeps going and flies off through the air into a steel box called a bin. That flight is Stage 4 of this project, and it is an ordinary projectile problem in a hard hat.

Then the empty truck drives back down and lines up under the shovel again. One complete lap — up full, tip, down empty, get filled — is a haul cycle, and its duration is the number the entire mine cares about, because it decides how much rock gets moved in a day. The mass of rock carried in one trip is the payload; ours is 180 tonnes, which is normal for a large mine truck. Combine payload with cycle time and you get tonnes per hour, which is the score.

One last pair of words comes from the survey data. Surveyors record positions as an easting and a northing — metres east and metres north of some agreed starting point. They are ordinary \(x\) and \(y\) coordinates with a compass bolted on. In this project easting plays the role of \(x\), northing plays \(y\), and height above sea level is \(z\).

That is the whole vocabulary. Here it is on one picture.

Three things worth noticing before you write any code

Sit with the picture for a minute and three features of it start to stand out. Each one ends up driving a whole stage of the project.

  • The trip up and the trip down are different journeys on the same road. Loaded, the truck tops out around 25 km/h. Empty, around 40. Same 545 m, same bends, two quite different speed profiles. A surprising amount of what makes this project interesting hangs off that asymmetry, including a result in Stage 3 that catches most people out.
  • The road is long and shallow. 545 m of driving to gain 35 m of height. If you imagine a truck heaving up a cliff face you have the wrong picture; imagine a long ramp in a multi-storey car park, and you have close to the right one.
  • A lot of the cycle is spent standing still. Lining up under the shovel and waiting to be filled costs about 50 seconds. Tipping costs about 40. That is 90 seconds of a cycle that turns out to last around 260. Any model that counts only the driving will be cheerfully optimistic, and part of your job is to know by how much.
Worth a moment. That falling easting is the first thing in this project that will quietly break code. If you write something that measures how far east the truck went and adds it up, the switchback contributes a negative amount and your road comes out too short. If you patch that by wrapping it in an absolute value, the road comes out too long. Have a think now about what you should be adding up instead — the answer arrives in Stage 1, and it is nicer if you get there first.

What lives inside this model, and what stays outside

Everything here is kinematics: positions, speeds, accelerations, distances, times. There are no forces anywhere in the first forty days — no engine power, no tyre friction, no air resistance, no fuel. That boundary is chosen deliberately. A model where every coefficient is something you can see and check will teach you far more MATLAB than one where half the inputs came from a table you had to trust.

The data you start with

A surveyor rode up the haul road once and recorded where the truck was every 40 seconds. Eight readings, each one a time, an easting, a northing and a height. East and north are measured from the loading point, so the first reading is zeros apart from its height.

Notice what these timings are: one particular careful trip that happened on one particular day. In Stage 1 you use them to measure the road itself. In Stage 2 you set the timings aside and work out from scratch how long the climb ought to take, and then the two answers get to argue with each other. That argument is the most interesting moment in the project.

The truck's fixed numbers

These describe the truck itself, so they hold still all the way through a run. Put them in one function that hands back a struct, which is MATLAB's way of bundling named values into a single object. The discipline to keep is that each number appears once in the entire repository. If you later decide the truck carries 220 tonnes, exactly one line should need touching.

function p = truck_params()
% Fixed numbers for the mine truck model. Metres, seconds, tonnes.
p.g            = 9.81;      % m/s^2   gravity
p.v_loaded_max = 25/3.6;    % m/s     top speed climbing, full   (25 km/h)
p.v_empty_max  = 40/3.6;    % m/s     top speed descending, empty (40 km/h)
p.a_accel      = 0.35;      % m/s^2   how gently it picks up speed
p.a_brake      = -0.50;     % m/s^2   normal braking; negative, since it slows
p.t_dump       = 40;        % s       stopped at the top, tipping
p.t_load       = 50;        % s       stopped at the bottom, being filled
p.payload_t    = 180;       % t       rock carried per trip
end

Look at that acceleration for a second: 0.35 m/s². A family car manages around 3, so this truck picks up speed roughly ten times more lazily. Multiply it out and you find it needs about twenty seconds to reach 25 km/h. Whenever a number in this project looks strange, do that — turn it into something you can picture. Half of engineering judgement is the habit of asking whether a number is the right size.

The five stages

Each stage below says why it exists, what to calculate, what the finished graph has to show, the answers you should land on, and the places where things quietly go wrong. Those target answers exist so you can catch a mistake the same afternoon you make it. Leave them out of your code and let the script earn them.

Stage 1 — Measure the road from eight points

You have coordinates. You want the things an engineer would quote out loud: how long is this road, how steep is the worst of it, how fast was the truck actually going. The same shape of analysis as Project 1 Parts A–B, with different numbers.

Of everything in the project, this stage transfers the furthest. A GPS trace from a bike ride, a temperature log, an accelerometer file from a phone — they all arrive as a column of times and a column of values, and they all get processed with the moves you are about to learn.

1a — Draw the road in three dimensions

Plot the road with plot3(E,N,z). Put visible markers on the eight survey points so a reader can see how sparse the data is, and give the start and the end a distinct marker plus a text label. Label all three axes with their units, turn on grid on, and title it after the thing it shows: "Haul road, pit floor to tipping point" tells someone more than "plot of E N z".

1b — Grade, section by section

Grade is height gained divided by the horizontal distance covered while gaining it. Pause on which distance that is. Between two survey points the truck travels along a slope, and that slope distance is slightly longer than the horizontal distance underneath it. Grade wants the horizontal one, the flat shadow of the journey. For each gap:

\[\Delta s_{xy,i}=\sqrt{(\Delta E_i)^2+(\Delta N_i)^2},\qquad G_i = 100\,\frac{\Delta z_i}{\Delta s_{xy,i}}\ \ (\%)\]

Eight readings leave seven gaps, so you get seven grades. Notice what that means: a grade describes a stretch of road, and asking for the grade at an instant is asking a question the data cannot answer. So plot each value at the midpoint of its interval, or use a stair-step. Put height and grade on one figure with yyaxis left and yyaxis right, and the coincidence between the steepest section and the switchback jumps straight off the page.

1c — Distance and speed

Here is the answer to the question posed after Diagram 2. What you add up is the length of each little movement in three dimensions, which is always positive whichever direction the truck happened to face:

\[\Delta s_i=\sqrt{(\Delta E_i)^2+(\Delta N_i)^2+(\Delta z_i)^2},\qquad s_k=\sum_{i=1}^{k}\Delta s_i\]

cumsum does the running total. One subtlety: distance travelled starts at zero, so stick a zero on the front to keep the vector the same length as your survey points. Convert speeds to km/h before plotting, since that is the unit any reader thinks in. Make two plots — speed against time, and speed against distance travelled. The second is the one a road engineer would ask for, because it tells you where the truck is slow.

1d — Print a summary

Use fprintf with sensible spacing and a unit on every line. Report total road length in metres and kilometres, total height gained, average speed in km/h, and the steepest grade along with which section it belongs to.

Hand in. s1_path3d.m, s1_grade.m, s1_distance_speed.m, a reusable functions/path_metrics.m taking E,N,z and returning section lengths, running distance and grades, plus four figures saved into figures/.
Your answers: road length 545.0 m, height gained 35 m, average speed 7.0 km/h — about the pace of a decent jog, which is a good thing to know about mine trucks — and steepest grade 8.24% on the switchback, between 259 m and 332 m along. Section speeds spread between 4.9 and 8.3 km/h. Two useful diagnostics: 543.9 m means horizontal distance crept into the sum somewhere, and anything near 546 m means an extra element joined in.
Where this quietly goes wrong. Almost all of it is off-by-one. diff hands back one fewer value than you gave it, so plotting 7 grades against 8 times throws an error — and padding with a zero to make the error go away invents a flat piece of road that was never there. The switchback punishes any code using \(|\Delta E|\) or assuming east increases. And a subtle one: differentiating east, north and height separately and adding the three results gives a number that is too big, because those three movements happen at right angles to each other. Find the length of the movement first, then divide by time.

Stage 2 — Predict the climb from first principles

Stage 1 described a trip that happened. Stage 2 asks a different flavour of question entirely, the design question: suppose the truck pulls away gently, runs at its safe top speed, then brakes to a stop at the top. How long does that take?

This is where the constant-acceleration formulas from school stop being exam furniture and start producing arrays of numbers you can plot. Take the road length \(S = 545\) m from Stage 1 and let go of east and north completely — the road becomes one number \(s\) running from 0 to \(S\), like distance along a piece of string. The climb then has three phases.

\[t_{\text{acc}}=\frac{v_{\max}}{a_{\text{acc}}},\quad s_{\text{acc}}=\frac{v_{\max}^2}{2a_{\text{acc}}},\qquad t_{\text{br}}=\frac{v_{\max}}{|a_{\text{br}}|},\quad s_{\text{br}}=\frac{v_{\max}^2}{2|a_{\text{br}}|}\]

\[s_{\text{steady}}=S-s_{\text{acc}}-s_{\text{br}},\qquad t_{\text{steady}}=\frac{s_{\text{steady}}}{v_{\max}}\]

What to calculate

  1. The six quantities above, printed with units.
  2. A guard in the code for short roads. If \(s_{\text{acc}}+s_{\text{br}} > S\) there is no room to reach top speed at all, and the truck picks up speed then immediately brakes — a triangle in place of a trapezium, peaking at \(v_{\text{peak}}=\sqrt{2Sa_{\text{acc}}|a_{\text{br}}|/(a_{\text{acc}}+|a_{\text{br}}|)}\). Write the if even though our road never triggers it, then prove it works by calling your function with \(S=80\) m and watching the triangle appear.
  3. Build the arrays: time, distance, speed, acceleration. linspace across each phase, then join them, keeping one consistent clock so the joins line up.
  4. One figure with three stacked subplot panels on a shared time axis: distance, speed, acceleration. The acceleration panel should come out as a staircase — a positive block, a zero, a negative block — which is exactly the assumption you fed in, now visible.
Hand in. functions/piecewise_motion.m with signature [t,s,v,a] = piecewise_motion(S, vmax, a_acc, a_brake), and scripts/s2_loaded_climb.m calling it with Stage 1's road length.
Your answers: picking up speed takes 19.8 s over 68.9 m; steady running lasts 61.6 s over 427.9 m; braking takes 13.9 s over 48.2 m. Climb time 95.3 s. Then check yourself with the area idea: trapz(t,v) has to give 545 m, and the last entry of your distance array has to be exactly \(S\).
The interesting disagreement — this is the good bit. The surveyed climb took 280 seconds. Your model says 95. That is a factor of three, which is far too large to be rounding, and the temptation is to go hunting for the bug. There is no bug. What you have found is one of your own assumptions failing, and finding those is most of what modelling is.

Which assumption? You told the model the truck holds 25 km/h all the way up. Look back at Diagram 3: three of the seven sections are steeper than 7.5%, and 180 tonnes on a 7.5% slope does not hold 25 km/h. On top of that, the real driver slowed right down through the switchback and may have queued somewhere. Write two or three sentences in your memo laying this out, and then say what you would change: making the top speed depend on the local grade is the natural next model. Spotting that a tidy model disagrees with the world, and being able to say which assumption caused it, is the whole skill this project is here to teach.
Where the code quietly goes wrong. Sign errors on braking — pass in \(-0.50\) and use \(|a|\) inside the distance formula. Joining phases without shifting each one's start time, which turns the trapezium into a sawtooth. And building distance with cumsum(v)*dt when your time steps are unequal; use the algebraic formula for each phase.

Stage 3 — The full cycle, and the number the mine cares about

Now assemble the whole lap and turn it into productivity. This is where the project stops being a plotting exercise and starts answering a question somebody would pay to have answered.

What to calculate

  1. The empty descent, over the same 545 m, at the empty top speed, with the same acceleration and braking rates. Call piecewise_motion again with different arguments and change nothing inside it. If you catch yourself copying the function and editing a number in the copy, that is a signal the function is missing an input.
  2. Insert the two waits as real stretches in the arrays where position holds constant and speed sits at zero. Adding 90 seconds to the total at the end would give the same cycle time, but the flat sections need to physically exist in the speed array, because you are about to integrate it as a check.
  3. Total cycle time, in seconds and in minutes.
  4. Productivity: \(\text{tonnes per hour} = 3600\,\times\,\text{payload}/T_{\text{cycle}}\), and loads per hour, which is just \(3600/T_{\text{cycle}}\).
  5. One figure matching Diagram 4: speed against time across the whole cycle, waits shaded and labelled, legend distinguishing full from empty.
Hand in. functions/cycle_time.m returning a struct of the four phase durations plus the total, scripts/s3_full_cycle.m, and the labelled cycle figure.
Your answers: the empty descent takes 76.0 s, made of 31.7 s picking up speed, 22.1 s steady, and 22.2 s braking. Cycle time \(= 95.3 + 40 + 76.0 + 50 = \mathbf{261.4\ \text{s}}\), or 4.36 minutes, giving about 13.8 loads an hour and roughly 2 480 tonnes an hour.

Now look at those two trip times again. The empty truck is allowed to go 1.6 times faster, so you might reasonably expect it to take 1.6 times less time — around 60 seconds. It takes 76. Have a look at the phase breakdown and see whether you can explain the gap before reading on. The reason is that going faster costs you time at both ends: reaching 11 m/s takes 31.7 s where the loaded climb needed only 19.8, and shedding it again takes 22.2 s against the loaded 13.9. Those two phases eat 54 of the 76 seconds, leaving barely 22 seconds of actual top speed. On a short road, a faster vehicle spends most of its trip getting up to speed and coming back down again. That is a result worth a line in your memo, and it is entirely visible in the shape of your own graph.
How much to believe 2 480 tonnes an hour. It assumes the shovel is always waiting, the road is always clear, nobody stops for a shift change, and the weather cooperates. Mines handle this by multiplying by the fraction of the time the truck is really working. Add one line to the memo giving your figure at 80% working time, and you have turned a fantasy number into a usable estimate.

Stage 4 — Rock leaving the end of a conveyor

Back to the top of the pit. The tipped rock goes onto a conveyor, and at the far end of the belt the rubber wraps around a roller and heads back underneath. The rock carries straight on and falls into the bin.

What makes projectile motion pleasant is that the horizontal and vertical stories never speak to each other. Sideways, nothing pushes the rock, so it drifts at a constant speed forever. Vertically, gravity pulls it down at a steady 9.81 m/s² regardless of how fast it is drifting sideways. The two motions share only a clock, and writing them as two independent one-line formulas is the entire technique.

The rock leaves at \(v_0 = 3.5\) m/s along a belt tilted \(\theta = 18^\circ\) above horizontal, from a lip \(h = 4.0\) m above the bin floor, with \(g = 9.81\) m/s². Air resistance is left out, which for fist-sized lumps of rock changes the answer by less than the precision of anything else here.

\[x(t) = (v_0\cos\theta)\,t,\qquad y(t) = h + (v_0\sin\theta)\,t - \tfrac12 g t^2\]

It lands when \(y=0\), the positive root of that quadratic:

\[t_f = \frac{v_0\sin\theta + \sqrt{(v_0\sin\theta)^2 + 2gh}}{g}\]

What to calculate

  1. Solve for the landing time with the quadratic formula, then build tv = linspace(0,tf,200) and get both \(x\) and \(y\) in one line each. A for loop here would work and would also be a missed opportunity: array arithmetic is the habit this stage exists to train.
  2. Plot \(y\) against \(x\) with axis equal, so the shape on screen matches the shape in the air. Mark the launch and landing points and draw the bin floor as a horizontal line at \(y=0\).
  3. Print time in the air, horizontal distance travelled, and highest point, with units.
  4. Cross-check your arrays against the algebra: the largest value in your \(y\) array should agree with \(h+(v_0\sin\theta)^2/(2g)\) to within the coarseness of a 200-point grid. Print both and the difference. Doing this a few times is how you come to trust numbers your own code produced.
  5. Run it again with a faster belt, say 4.5 m/s, and draw both flights on one set of axes with a legend. Then answer the practical question underneath: would a bin positioned for the slower belt still catch this?
Hand in. functions/projectile_xy.m taking \((v_0,\theta,h,g)\) and returning \(t,x,y\) plus a struct of flight time, distance and highest point; and scripts/s4_conveyor.m with the two-speed comparison.
Your answers: horizontal speed 3.329 m/s, initial upward speed 1.082 m/s, time in the air 1.020 s, lands 3.40 m from the lip, highest point 4.060 m reached after only 0.37 m of travel. At 4.5 m/s the landing moves out to about 4.4 m. A whole metre further for a fairly modest speed change, which is why anyone designing a real transfer point runs this calculation before pouring concrete.
Where this quietly goes wrong. MATLAB's cos and sin want radians, so reach for cosd(18) and sind(18), or convert with deg2rad. The quadratic has two roots and the negative one describes a time before the rock existed on the belt. And the classic: starting the rock at \(y=0\). It leaves the belt 4 m up, and dropping that term turns a correct 3.40 m into a puzzling 0.7 m.

Stage 5 — Separate the data, make it run in one command, write it up

This stage turns a folder of scripts that happen to work into something a stranger can run, where the stranger in question is you in six months. Everything about the long keep-warm period gets easier or harder depending on how carefully you do this.

What to build

  1. Write the survey readings out to data/ramp_climb.csv with a header row t_s,E_m,N_m,z_m using writematrix. Then delete the typed-in vectors from your scripts and read the file with readmatrix, handling the header row deliberately.
  2. Save every figure into figures/ with saveas, or exportgraphics, which trims the white margins better. Keep names consistent: fig_s1_path3d.png and so on.
  3. run_all.m at the top level: add the function folder to the path, then run Stages 1 to 4 in order, regenerating every figure and printing every summary. It has to survive a completely fresh MATLAB after clear all; close all.
  4. memo.md, about a page. Think of it as a short list of statements you are willing to defend out loud.

What the memo covers

  • Road length, height gained, and the steepest section with its location.
  • Predicted climb time, descent time, cycle time, the tonnes-per-hour figure, and what that figure leaves out.
  • The 95-versus-280-second disagreement, which assumption caused it, and the change you would make next.
  • How far the rock flies off the conveyor, how long it is airborne, and how precisely the bin needs placing.
  • One measurement you would take on a real road to improve the model, and your reason for choosing that one.
Hand in. run_all.m, the CSV, every figure on disk, and memo.md, all committed.

The six months after — one addition a month

Once the forty days are done the model is finished. Through the long gap you add exactly one feature a month, in two short sessions a week. The single hard rule is that run_all.m still works at the end of every month.

  1. Full and empty on one graph. Both speed profiles on shared axes with a legend, and print the difference in time. Cheap to do, and it walks you back through your own function signatures.
  2. A second, gentler road. Longer, never as steep. A new data file, and zero new functions. Compare tonnes per hour and decide which road the mine should have built. Needing to edit path_metrics.m to load it tells you Stage 5 has a little more work in it.
  3. A second conveyor elsewhere on site, with its own speed, angle and height, calling projectile_xy.m untouched.
  4. Twenty cycles in a row, each with a random extra delay of 0 to 15 seconds at the top, using rand. Histogram the resulting tonnes per hour and report the average and the spread. Your first engineering answer that arrives as a range.
  5. An animation: a marker for the truck moving along the map view, driven by your Stage 2 clock, so the marker travels the way your model says it should.
  6. A month with no new features. Run run_all.m in a clean MATLAB. If it fails, the whole month's job is making it work again.
Still hungry after that? The most worthwhile next step is letting the truck's top speed depend on the local grade in place of one fixed 25 km/h. Your predicted climb time would slide up from 95 s towards the 280 s that was actually measured, and it uses nothing you have not already built. It is also the moment the project starts to resemble the simulations that real mine planners run.
