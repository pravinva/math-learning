Lay of the Land: what this subject is really about

Read this before Week 1. MATH283 can look like six unrelated topics stapled together: multivariable calculus, Laplace transforms, Fourier series, Gamma and Beta functions, the error function, and statistics. These topics form one connected story. The first three weeks build the calculus of quantities that depend on several things at once, and the rest of the subject uses that calculus to move difficult problems into a setting where they can be solved, then brings the answers back. 01 The one idea behind the subject

You already know one transform, and you learned it years ago. It is the logarithm. Multiplying large numbers by hand is slow and error-prone, so you take logarithms, add, and then undo the logarithm to return to ordinary numbers. The difficult operation is exchanged for an easy one by working somewhere else for a while.

MATH283 applies that strategy on a much larger scale. Differential equations are hard to solve directly, so you apply a Laplace transform, do ordinary algebra, and then invert the transform to recover the answer as a function of time. Signals with sudden switches and sharp corners are hard to handle directly, so a Fourier series rewrites them as a list of sine and cosine amplitudes that can be examined one at a time. Factorials only make sense for whole numbers, so the Gamma function extends them to every real number and keeps the standard formulas working on problems where the powers are fractional.

Almost every technique in the first half of this subject is a version of that detour.

The idea in one paragraph

A logarithm converts multiplication into addition. In the same way, a Laplace transform converts differentiation into multiplication by $s$, and a Fourier series converts an awkward repeating shape into a list of coefficients. Each one exchanges an operation you find difficult for one you already know how to perform, at the price of one extra step at the end to convert the result back.

02 Five things every engineered system does

Why the topics come in this order

Each block depends on the one before it. You cannot describe a switching pump until you can handle a quantity that depends on both time and position, which puts the multivariable calculus first. You cannot analyse a repeating signal until you can integrate the switched pieces it is built from. You cannot state how confident you are in a result until you have a method for producing the result, which puts statistics last.

03 Weeks 1–2: the calculus of several variables

Everything in MATH141 and MATH142 concerned a single input and a single output, written $y=f(x)$. Physical quantities rarely respect that restriction. Ore grade varies through a deposit as $g(x,y,z)$. Rock temperature near a heat source varies as $T(x,t)$. The net present value of a project depends jointly on price, grade and recovery. Before you can transform anything, you need calculus that copes with more than one input, and the first two weeks build it piece by piece.

Domain, range and level curves Week 1

A function of two variables $z=f(x,y)$ describes a surface. Its domain is the set of input pairs for which the formula makes sense, and domain restrictions usually encode a physical constraint. A square root that requires a non-negative argument, or a denominator that must not vanish, is the mathematics marking the point where your model stops being valid.

A level curve is the set of points satisfying $f(x,y)=k$ for a fixed value $k$. This is a horizontal slice through the surface, and it is an object you have been reading your whole life without naming it: a contour line on a topographic plan, a grade shell on a block model, an isobar on a weather map. Week 1 supplies the algebra behind a picture you already understand.

Limits and continuity in two dimensions Week 1

In one variable there are two ways to approach a point, from the left and from the right. In two variables there are infinitely many paths of approach, and $\lim_{(x,y)\to(a,b)}f(x,y)=L$ has to hold along all of them. The practical consequence is the two-path test: find two routes to the same point that give different limiting values and you have shown the limit does not exist.

A model whose predicted value depends on the direction you approach an operating point from will behave badly at that point. Finding those points before equipment is designed around them is part of the work.

Partial derivatives Week 2

A partial derivative holds every variable fixed except one and differentiates with respect to that one. Written $f_x$ or $\partial f/\partial x$, it answers the question every project engineer eventually has to answer: if this one input moves slightly and nothing else changes, how much does the output move? This is the mathematics of sensitivity, and it is why feasibility studies contain tornado charts.

The chain rule and implicit differentiation Week 2

Variables in real systems depend on each other. Grade depends on position, and position depends on time as a shovel advances through a bench, so the grade arriving at the crusher changes with time through a chain of dependencies. The multivariable chain rule is the bookkeeping that handles this correctly, and implicit differentiation extends it to relationships defined by an equation you cannot rearrange into the form $z=f(x,y)$.

Linear approximation and error estimation Week 2

This is the most immediately practical topic in the first half of the subject. The total differential

$$dz=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$$

says that a small change in the output is approximately the sum of each input's change multiplied by the corresponding sensitivity. Turn it around and it becomes a statement about measurement error: if each input is known only to within a tolerance, this formula tells you how much uncertainty the answer inherits. Every measured quantity in mining carries a tolerance, so every calculated quantity carries one too, and this is the link between the two.

Second-order partials, and the partial differential equations behind them Week 2

Differentiating twice produces $f_{xx}$, $f_{yy}$ and the mixed partials $f_{xy}$ and $f_{yx}$, which for well-behaved functions are equal whichever order you take them in. Second-order partial derivatives matter because they are the ingredients of the partial differential equations that govern continuous physical processes:

$$\underbrace{\frac{\partial T}{\partial t}=\alpha\frac{\partial^2T}{\partial x^2}}_{\text{heat / diffusion}}\qquad\qquad \underbrace{\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial t^2}=c^2\frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}}_{\text{waves}}$$

The scope here shapes how to study it. In MATH283 you are asked to recognise these equations and to verify that a proposed function satisfies one, by differentiating it and checking that both sides agree. General solution methods such as separation of variables sit outside the syllabus. Week 7 is the point where a partial differential equation gets solved: a diffusion problem is pushed through a transform and the answer comes back as the error function. Later subjects covering heat transfer, fluid flow and groundwater will solve these equations in full, and they will assume you already speak this language.

Mining application
  • Grade contours. A grade shell on a block model is a level curve of a function of position, and reading how tightly the contours are spaced is reading the size of a partial derivative.
  • Survey volume tolerance. A stockpile volume computed from measured dimensions inherits the error in each measurement. The total differential converts three tolerances into a single uncertainty figure on the reported tonnes.
  • Feasibility sensitivity. Comparing $\partial V/\partial(\text{grade})$ against $\partial V/\partial(\text{price})$ shows which assumption justifies more drilling and which one moves the answer very little.
  • Changing feed. As a shovel advances, the head grade reaching the plant changes through position, which changes through time. The chain rule produces that rate.
  • Ground temperature. Rock temperature depends on depth and time together, and the equation relating them is the heat equation you meet as a verification exercise in Week 2 and solve in Week 7.
04 Weeks 2–3: adding things up over a region

A single integral accumulates a quantity along a line. A double integral $\iint_R f(x,y)\,dA$ accumulates it over a two-dimensional region. Use it whenever the quantity you are totalling is spread across an area.

The difficulty in double integration lies in describing the region. Most of the marks lost in this topic are lost in the limits of integration, so sketch the region before writing anything down.

Reversing the order of integration Week 3

The same region can be swept in two directions, by vertical strips or by horizontal ones, and the two descriptions give different-looking integrals with identical values. One order often produces an inner integral with no elementary antiderivative while the other order is a routine calculation, so swapping the order turns an impossible problem into a manageable one. As you will see in Section 05, this manipulation is the key step in proving one of the most useful results in the transform half of the subject.

Areas, volumes and double integrals in polar coordinates Week 3

Setting $f=1$ makes the double integral compute the area of the region itself, and integrating the height between two surfaces computes a volume. Where the region is circular or annular, Cartesian limits become unpleasant and polar coordinates become the natural choice, with the area element becoming $dA=r\,dr\,d\theta$. The extra factor of $r$ says that a small polar patch grows wider the further it sits from the origin.

Polar double integrals also deliver one specific result that echoes through the rest of the subject, the value of the Gaussian integral $\int_0^\infty e^{-x^2}dx=\tfrac{\sqrt{\pi}}{2}$. There is no elementary antiderivative for $e^{-x^2}$, so the trick is to square the integral, read the product as a double integral over the plane, and convert to polar coordinates, at which point the extra factor of $r$ collapses the whole thing to something you can integrate in one line. That result is where $\Gamma(1/2)=\sqrt{\pi}$ comes from, and it supplies the constant sitting in front of both the error function and the normal distribution.

Mining application
  • Overburden volume. With surface elevation $z_s(x,y)$ and ore roof $z_r(x,y)$, the waste to be moved over a footprint $R$ is $\iint_R (z_s-z_r)\,dA$.
  • Contained metal. Grade times density, integrated over an area and a thickness, gives contained tonnes, and the integral form is what makes irregular pit outlines tractable.
  • Dewatering drawdown. Pumping from a bore produces a cone of depression that is radial, so the volume of water removed integrates cleanly in polar coordinates.
  • Blast influence zones. The region affected by a charge is roughly circular around the hole, which again makes polar the sensible coordinate system.

06 Heaviside, Laplace, and switching

In the 1880s Oliver Heaviside was working on the equations governing telegraph lines and grew impatient with solving them by conventional means. He replaced the operator $\tfrac{d}{dt}$ with an ordinary algebraic symbol $p$, performed straightforward algebra, and read the answer back out. The method produced correct results consistently, he could not supply a rigorous justification for why it should, and the mathematicians of the day dismissed it accordingly.

Some decades later his shortcut was shown to be the Laplace transform in different notation, with his $p$ playing the role of the variable now written $s$. That history reframes the topic. Laplace transforms are the formalisation of a shortcut invented by a working engineer who wanted to stop doing calculus.

Why the step function and the transform are inseparable

The Heaviside step function $u(t)$ is a switch, taking the value $0$ before $t=0$ and $1$ afterwards. The one-sided Laplace transform integrates from $0$ to $\infty$, so every function it processes is multiplied by that switch. The transform is built on the assumption that the world begins when you press the start button.

$$\mathcal{L}\{f\}=\int_0^\infty f(t)e^{-st}\,dt,\qquad \mathcal{L}\{f'\}=sF(s)-f(0)$$

Read the second formula again. A derivative has become a multiplication, and the initial condition is carried inside the result. That single line is the reason the entire method exists.

Once the transform is available, three further tools follow from it:

  • The shift theorems, which handle a system that starts at some time $t=a$, written using $u(t-a)f(t-a)$.
  • Convolution, $(f*g)(t)=\int_0^t f(\tau)g(t-\tau)\,d\tau$, which constructs the response of a system to an arbitrary input out of its response to a single instantaneous kick. Multiplication in the $s$-domain corresponds to convolution in time, and the proof of that correspondence is a Week 3 double integral.
  • The transform of a periodic function, where one cycle of a repeating input contains enough information to describe the whole of it. This is the doorway into Fourier series.
Mining application
  • Dewatering pump start-up. The pump switches on at a set time and the water level responds as a first-order system, a step input solved algebraically through the transform.
  • Motor starting transients. The inrush and settling behaviour of the RL circuits you meet in your electrical subject is this calculation, and the time-constant formulas quoted there are Laplace results in disguise.
  • Blast response. A detonation is well approximated by an instantaneous impulse, and convolution then predicts the ground response to an entire delayed blast sequence from the measured response to a single hole.
  • Mill residence time. Inject a tracer into a grinding circuit and the output concentration is the convolution of the input with the circuit's residence-time distribution.
07 Fourier series and repetition

A Fourier series makes a claim that is hard to believe the first time you meet it: any reasonable repeating signal, however jagged, can be written as a sum of pure sines and cosines whose frequencies are whole-number multiples of one base frequency.

$$f(t)=\frac{a_0}{2}+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left[a_n\cos\frac{n\pi t}{L}+b_n\sin\frac{n\pi t}{L}\right]$$

The square wave you can assemble from Heaviside steps is the standard example, and reproducing its sharp corners takes infinitely many harmonics. This is the link between Weeks 4 and 6: a signal with sudden edges in time carries significant content at high frequencies, which is why fast switching creates interference and why sharp mechanical impacts excite high-frequency vibration.

Let the period grow without bound and the discrete harmonics move closer together until they merge into a continuous spectrum. That limiting process converts the Fourier series into the Fourier transform, which is what vibration analysers and power-quality meters compute internally.

Mining application
  • Condition monitoring. A failing gearbox or a cracking screen deck announces itself as a new peak at a particular harmonic long before it announces itself as a breakdown, and reading that spectrum is applied Fourier analysis.
  • Blast vibration compliance. Ground vibration near a community is assessed on its frequency content as well as its peak amplitude, because structures respond most strongly to particular frequencies.
  • Power quality. Variable-speed drives on a mine site inject harmonics onto the site network, and the harmonic numbers listed in a power-quality report are Fourier coefficients of the kind you will compute by hand in Week 6.
  • Half-range expansions. When a process is defined over only half a domain you choose an even or an odd extension, which is the mathematical form of choosing sensible behaviour at a boundary.
08 Gamma, Beta, and the error function

These three functions are the shapes that answers take once you start transforming realistic problems. Each one arrives as the output of work you will already have done.

Gamma: extending the factorial to every real number

$$\Gamma(x)=\int_0^\infty t^{x-1}e^{-t}\,dt,\qquad \Gamma(n+1)=n!\ \text{for whole numbers } n$$

The definition is itself an integral transform, which is the first sign that it belongs in this part of the subject. Take the Laplace transform of a power of $t$ and the Gamma function appears without being invited:

$$\mathcal{L}\{t^{n}\}=\frac{\Gamma(n+1)}{s^{n+1}}$$

For whole-number $n$ this reduces to the familiar $n!/s^{n+1}$. For a power such as $t^{1.5}$, the kind of exponent that arises in diffusion, consolidation and creep problems, there is no factorial available and the Gamma function is what keeps the formula usable.

Beta: the natural function for proportions

$$B(p,q)=\frac{\Gamma(p)\Gamma(q)}{\Gamma(p+q)}$$

The Beta function is closely related to Gamma and is the natural language for quantities confined between zero and one: a recovery fraction, a moisture content, the proportion of a batch meeting specification. It also tidies up the products of Gamma functions that appear when inverting transforms in creep and viscoelastic problems, such as paste backfill or tailings deforming slowly under their own weight over months.

The error function: what a sharp switch becomes once it has spread

$$\operatorname{erf}(x)=\frac{2}{\sqrt{\pi}}\int_0^x e^{-u^2}\,du$$

Apply heat, moisture or a contaminant suddenly at a boundary, which is a Heaviside step imposed in space or time, and let diffusion act. Transform the heat equation, solve it in the transform domain, and invert the result, and what comes back is an error function. It describes how an initially sharp front smooths into a gradual one as time passes.

The constant $2/\sqrt{\pi}$ in front is the Gaussian integral from Week 3, evaluated with a polar double integral. The same integral gives the area under the normal curve, which is why the error function reappears in the statistics half of the subject. Diffusion problems and probability problems rest on the same integral.

Mining application
  • Spontaneous combustion in a coal stockpile. Heat generated within the pile diffuses outward, the temperature profile takes the form of an error-function front, and the timing of that front is the basis of a safety decision.
  • Seepage from a tailings storage facility. A contaminant released at a boundary migrates through the profile as a diffusion front described by the same function.
  • Curing paste fill and shotcrete. Transient heat generation and strength development follow diffusion-type solutions containing fractional powers of time, where the Gamma function earns its place.
  • Equipment reliability. The Weibull distribution used to model truck and pump life has a mean expressed in terms of a Gamma function, so a special function from Part A appears inside the reliability work of Part B.
09 Why statistics shares the subject

Part A produces exact answers to idealised problems. Part B addresses the fact that every input fed into those idealised problems was measured, and every measurement carries uncertainty. That is why the two halves are taught as one subject.

Weeks 8 to 13 form one continuous argument:

  • First you describe the data you have, using probability and exploratory data analysis.
  • Then you model it with an appropriate distribution, whether binomial, Poisson, exponential, uniform or normal.
  • Then you examine sampling behaviour, where the Central Limit Theorem explains why averages behave predictably even when the individual values do not.
  • Then you infer, using confidence intervals and hypothesis tests to decide whether an apparent effect sits outside the range of ordinary variation.
  • Finally you compare, through two-sample tests, $F$-tests, correlation, regression, and analysis of variance for three or more groups.
Mining application
  • Assay quality control. Duplicates, standards and blanks are judged using the hypothesis-testing logic of Week 11, so a failed check comes with a probability attached to it.
  • Grade estimation. A block grade is an average of samples, the Central Limit Theorem is what allows a confidence interval to be attached to it, and the resulting standard error shows whether additional drilling is worth its cost.
  • Drill and blast performance. Regression relates powder factor and burden to measured fragmentation, and reports how much of the observed variation the model explains.
  • Comparing crews and fleets. Deciding whether one crew is more productive than another, or has simply had a good fortnight, is what two-sample $t$-tests and analysis of variance are for.
The link back to Part A

Probabilities under the normal curve are error-function integrals. Reliability calculations use Gamma functions. Measurement error propagates through calculations using the total differential from Week 2. Part B applies the machinery of Part A to measured data.

12 What you will be able to solve

By the end of the session, given a realistic engineering scenario and no instruction about which method to apply, you should be able to do all of the following.

  • Take a quantity that depends on two or three variables, work out how sensitive it is to each one, carry the measurement uncertainty in the inputs through to the answer, and integrate the quantity over a real region to obtain a total.
  • Recognise the partial differential equation governing a diffusion or wave process and verify whether a proposed function satisfies it.
  • Take a system that is switched on, switched off or delayed, express the input using Heaviside functions, transform it, solve the resulting algebra, and invert the result to obtain the behaviour over time.
  • Take a repeating signal, decompose it into its harmonics, and explain what the appearance of a new peak in that spectrum means physically.
  • Recognise when an answer has to be expressed using the Gamma, Beta or error function, and evaluate it.
  • Take a set of measurements and state, at a stated confidence level, whether an observed difference is real.

Together those steps amount to modelling a situation, solving the model, and reporting how much confidence the answer carries. That is what engineering analysis consists of, and it is why this subject sits where it does in the degree.

Six things to carry into Week 1
  • Most methods here are detours. You transform the problem, solve something easier, and convert the answer back. Logarithms taught you the pattern years ago; Laplace and Fourier apply it at a larger scale.
  • The first three weeks get used throughout. Reversing the order of integration proves the convolution theorem, and the polar double integral produces the constant that appears in both the error function and the normal distribution.
  • Partial derivatives lead to partial differential equations. Here you learn to recognise and verify the heat and wave equations; Week 7 is where one of them is solved, using a transform.
  • Heaviside and Laplace are a single idea. The step function is the reason the transform integrates from zero.
  • Fourier is the periodic counterpart of Laplace. Sharp edges in time always require high-frequency content to reproduce them.
  • Statistics completes the argument. Part A computes the number and Part B establishes how much of it you can believe.
