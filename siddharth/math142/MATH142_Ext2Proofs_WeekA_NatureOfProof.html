The goal is not to memorise ceremonial words. It is to make every claim traceable: what is assumed, what must be shown, why each step follows, and where the conclusion is reached.

01 Statements, implication and quantifiers

A statement is a sentence that is either true or false. A conditional $P\Rightarrow Q$ says that whenever $P$ is true, $Q$ must follow. It does not automatically give $Q\Rightarrow P$.

OriginalRelated statementEquivalent?
$P\Rightarrow Q$Converse: $Q\Rightarrow P$No
$P\Rightarrow Q$Inverse: $\neg P\Rightarrow\neg Q$No
$P\Rightarrow Q$Contrapositive: $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$Yes
Negating quantifiers

The negation of “for every $x$, $P(x)$” is “there exists an $x$ for which $P(x)$ is false”. The negation of “there exists $x$ such that $P(x)$” is “for every $x$, $P(x)$ is false”.

02 Direct proof and definitions

A direct proof starts with the hypothesis and uses definitions and known results until the conclusion appears. For integers, definitions are your algebraic handles:

  • even: $n=2k$;
  • odd: $n=2k+1$;
  • $a\mid b$: $b=ak$ for some integer $k$;
  • rational: $x=p/q$ for integers $p,q$, $q\ne0$.

Example — the square of an odd integer is odd

Let $n=2k+1$. Then $$n^2=(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1.$$ The bracket is an integer, so $n^2$ has the form $2m+1$ and is odd.

Contrapositive: to prove $P\Rightarrow Q$, prove the equivalent statement $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$. This is ideal when the negation of $Q$ has a useful algebraic form.

Contradiction: assume the claim is false, then derive an impossibility: a number is both odd and even, a reduced fraction has a common factor, or a quantity is simultaneously $<a$ and $\ge a$.

Do not confuse them

Contrapositive begins with $\neg Q$ and aims for $\neg P$. Contradiction assumes enough to negate the whole target and aims for any contradiction.

04 Equivalence, existence and disproof

To prove $P\Leftrightarrow Q$, prove both directions separately. To prove existence, construct one object or show why one must exist. To disprove a universal statement, give one valid counterexample and check it explicitly.

Example — disprove a tempting pattern

The claim “$n^2-n+11$ is prime for every positive integer $n$” is false. Set $n=11$: $11^2-11+11=121=11^2$.

05 Practice — write complete arguments
Proof checklist

Declare variables and domains; name the method; state assumptions; justify each implication; use full sentences; finish with the exact claim.

P01
Negate: “For every real number $x$, $x^2+1>0$.”
Worked solution

The negation of “for every” is “there exists”, and $>0$ becomes $\le0$:

$$\boxed{\text{There exists }x\in\mathbb R\text{ such that }x^2+1\le0.}$$

P02
Write the converse and contrapositive of: “If $n$ is divisible by $6$, then $n$ is even.”
Worked solution

Converse: If $n$ is even, then $n$ is divisible by $6$ (false: $n=2$).

Contrapositive: If $n$ is not even, then $n$ is not divisible by $6$ (true and equivalent to the original).

P03
Prove directly that the sum of two odd integers is even.
Worked solution

Let the odd integers be $2a+1$ and $2b+1$, where $a,b\in\mathbb Z$. Then

$$(2a+1)+(2b+1)=2(a+b+1).$$

Since $a+b+1\in\mathbb Z$, the sum is twice an integer and is therefore even.

P04
Prove directly: if $3\mid a$ and $3\mid b$, then $9\mid ab$.
Worked solution

Write $a=3r$ and $b=3s$ for integers $r,s$. Then $ab=9rs$. Since $rs\in\mathbb Z$, $9\mid ab$.

P05
Prove by contraposition: if $n^2$ is divisible by $3$, then $n$ is divisible by $3$.
Worked solution

Prove the contrapositive. If $3\nmid n$, then $n=3q+1$ or $n=3q+2$. Squaring gives

$$(3q+1)^2=3(3q^2+2q)+1,\qquad(3q+2)^2=3(3q^2+4q+1)+1.$$

In either case $3\nmid n^2$. Therefore, by contraposition, $3\mid n^2\Rightarrow3\mid n$.

P06
Prove by contradiction that there is no greatest integer.
Worked solution

Assume there is a greatest integer $N$. But $N+1$ is also an integer and $N+1>N$, contradicting the choice of $N$. Hence no greatest integer exists.

P07
Prove by contradiction that $\sqrt5$ is irrational.
Worked solution

Assume $\sqrt5=p/q$ in lowest terms, with integers $p,q$ and $q\ne0$. Then $p^2=5q^2$, so $5\mid p$; write $p=5r$. Substitution gives $q^2=5r^2$, so $5\mid q$. This contradicts $p/q$ being in lowest terms. Thus $\sqrt5$ is irrational.

P08
Prove that an integer $n$ is odd if and only if $n^2$ is odd.
Worked solution

Forward: if $n=2k+1$, then $n^2=2(2k^2+2k)+1$, so $n^2$ is odd.

Reverse: if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ cannot be even, because $n=2k$ would give $n^2=4k^2$, even. Hence $n$ is odd.

P09
Disprove: “$n^2+n+41$ is prime for every positive integer $n$.”
Worked solution

One counterexample is enough. At $n=41$:

$$41^2+41+41=41(41+2)=41\cdot43,$$

which is composite. Therefore the universal statement is false.

P10
A student writes: “$2,3,5,7$ are prime, so every integer from $2$ onward is prime.” Identify the logical error.
Worked solution

Checking examples can suggest a conjecture but cannot prove a universal statement. The claim is also false: $4$ is a counterexample.
