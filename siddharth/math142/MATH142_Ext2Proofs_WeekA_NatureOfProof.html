The goal is not to memorise ceremonial words. It is to make every claim traceable: what is assumed, what must be shown, why each step follows, and where the conclusion is reached.

01 Statements, implication and quantifiers

A statement is a sentence that is either true or false. A conditional $P\Rightarrow Q$ says that whenever $P$ is true, $Q$ must follow. It does not automatically give $Q\Rightarrow P$.

OriginalRelated statementEquivalent?
$P\Rightarrow Q$Converse: $Q\Rightarrow P$No
$P\Rightarrow Q$Inverse: $\neg P\Rightarrow\neg Q$No
$P\Rightarrow Q$Contrapositive: $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$Yes
Negating quantifiers

The negation of “for every $x$, $P(x)$” is “there exists an $x$ for which $P(x)$ is false”. The negation of “there exists $x$ such that $P(x)$” is “for every $x$, $P(x)$ is false”.

02 Direct proof and definitions

A direct proof starts with the hypothesis and uses definitions and known results until the conclusion appears. For integers, definitions are your algebraic handles:

  • even: $n=2k$;
  • odd: $n=2k+1$;
  • $a\mid b$: $b=ak$ for some integer $k$;
  • rational: $x=p/q$ for integers $p,q$, $q\ne0$.

Example — the square of an odd integer is odd

Let $n=2k+1$. Then $$n^2=(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1.$$ The bracket is an integer, so $n^2$ has the form $2m+1$ and is odd.

Contrapositive: to prove $P\Rightarrow Q$, prove the equivalent statement $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$. This is ideal when the negation of $Q$ has a useful algebraic form.

Contradiction: assume the claim is false, then derive an impossibility: a number is both odd and even, a reduced fraction has a common factor, or a quantity is simultaneously $<a$ and $\ge a$.

Do not confuse them

Contrapositive begins with $\neg Q$ and aims for $\neg P$. Contradiction assumes enough to negate the whole target and aims for any contradiction.

04 Equivalence, existence and disproof

To prove $P\Leftrightarrow Q$, prove both directions separately. To prove existence, construct one object or show why one must exist. To disprove a universal statement, give one valid counterexample and check it explicitly.

Example — disprove a tempting pattern

The claim “$n^2-n+11$ is prime for every positive integer $n$” is false. Set $n=11$: $11^2-11+11=121=11^2$.

05 30 exam-quality proof problems
How to use this set

Problems 1–10 establish rigorous habits, 11–20 combine methods, and 21–30 demand counterexample design, proof repair, modular reasoning, existence or uniqueness. Attempt each proof before revealing the detailed solution. Declare variables and domains, justify every implication, and finish with the exact claim.

P01
Negate: “For every real number $x$, $x^2+1>0$.”
Worked solution

A negation must reverse both the quantifier and the property. “For every” becomes “there exists at least one”, while the negation of $x^2+1>0$ is $x^2+1\le0$ (not merely $\lt0$).

Therefore the exact negation is

$$\boxed{\text{There exists }x\in\mathbb R\text{ such that }x^2+1\le0.}$$

The negated statement happens to be false, which is consistent with the original statement being true.

P02
Write the converse and contrapositive of: “If $n$ is divisible by $6$, then $n$ is even.”
Worked solution

Write the implication as $P\Rightarrow Q$, where $P$ is “$6\mid n$” and $Q$ is “$n$ is even”.

Converse, $Q\Rightarrow P$: If $n$ is even, then $n$ is divisible by $6$. This is false; $n=2$ satisfies the hypothesis but not the conclusion.

Contrapositive, $\neg Q\Rightarrow\neg P$: If $n$ is odd, then $n$ is not divisible by $6$. This is logically equivalent to the original. Indeed, every multiple of $6$ has the form $6k=2(3k)$ and is even.

P03
Prove that if $4\mid(a^2+b^2)$ for integers $a,b$, then both $a$ and $b$ are even.
Worked solution

Every integer is even or odd. An even square is congruent to $0\pmod4$, while an odd square is congruent to $1\pmod4$.

If either $a$ or $b$ were odd, then $a^2+b^2$ would be congruent to $1$ or $2\pmod4$, depending on whether one or both were odd. Neither remainder is divisible by $4$.

Therefore both squares must be congruent to $0\pmod4$, which forces both $a$ and $b$ to be even.

P04
Let $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Prove that if $a\mid n$ and $b\mid n$, then $ab\mid n$.
Worked solution

Since $a\mid n$, write $n=ar$ for some integer $r$. We also know $b\mid ar$.

Because $\gcd(a,b)=1$, Bézout’s identity gives integers $u,v$ satisfying $ua+vb=1$. Multiply by $r$:

$$r=uar+vbr.$$

The term $uar$ is divisible by $b$ because $b\mid ar$, and $vbr$ is visibly divisible by $b$. Hence their sum $r$ is divisible by $b$; write $r=bs$.

Then $n=ar=a(bs)=abs$, so $ab\mid n$. The coprimality condition is essential: $4\mid12$ and $6\mid12$, but $24\nmid12$.

P05
Prove by contraposition: if $n^2$ is divisible by $3$, then $n$ is divisible by $3$.
Worked solution

Prove the contrapositive. If $3\nmid n$, then $n=3q+1$ or $n=3q+2$. Squaring gives

$$(3q+1)^2=3(3q^2+2q)+1,\qquad(3q+2)^2=3(3q^2+4q+1)+1.$$

In either case $3\nmid n^2$. Therefore, by contraposition, $3\mid n^2\Rightarrow3\mid n$.

P06
Prove by contradiction that there is no greatest integer.
Worked solution

The target is a non-existence statement, so contradiction is natural. Assume its negation: suppose a greatest integer exists; call it $N$.

Integers are closed under addition, so $N+1$ is also an integer. But $N+1>N$, contradicting the defining property that no integer exceeds the greatest integer $N$.

Therefore the assumption is impossible, and there is no greatest integer.

P07
Prove by contradiction that $\sqrt5$ is irrational.
Worked solution

Assume the opposite: $\sqrt5$ is rational. Then $\sqrt5=p/q$ for integers $p,q$ with $q\ne0$, chosen so that $\gcd(p,q)=1$.

Squaring and clearing the denominator gives $p^2=5q^2$. Hence $5\mid p^2$. Since $5$ is prime, $5\mid p$; write $p=5r$.

Substitute back:

$$25r^2=5q^2\Rightarrow q^2=5r^2.$$

Thus $5\mid q^2$, and again primality gives $5\mid q$. So $p$ and $q$ share the factor $5$, contradicting $\gcd(p,q)=1$. The rationality assumption is false; therefore $\sqrt5$ is irrational.

P08
Prove that an integer $n$ is odd if and only if $n^2$ is odd.
Worked solution

An “if and only if” statement requires two separate implications.

Forward: suppose $n$ is odd, so $n=2k+1$ for some integer $k$. Then

$$n^2=(2k+1)^2=4k^2+4k+1=2(2k^2+2k)+1,$$

which is odd because $2k^2+2k$ is an integer.

Reverse: prove the contrapositive. If $n$ is even, write $n=2k$. Then $n^2=4k^2=2(2k^2)$ is even. Therefore, if $n^2$ is odd, $n$ cannot be even and must be odd.

P09
Disprove: “$n^2+n+41$ is prime for every positive integer $n$.”
Worked solution

The statement is universal, so one positive integer producing a composite value is sufficient. The constant $41$ suggests testing a value that makes every term divisible by $41$.

At $n=41$:

$$n^2+n+41=41^2+41+41=41(41+2)=41\cdot43.$$

Both factors exceed $1$, so the value is composite. Since $41$ lies in the claimed domain, this single counterexample disproves the statement.

P10
A student writes: “$2,3,5,7$ are prime, so every integer from $2$ onward is prime.” Identify the logical error.
Worked solution

The conclusion is universal—it claims something about infinitely many integers—but the evidence checks only four cases. Finite examples can motivate a conjecture; they cannot establish a statement beginning with “for every”.

In fact the reasoning overlooks $4$, which lies in the claimed domain and is composite. A single counterexample is enough to disprove a universal statement, so $n=4$ decisively refutes the claim.

P11
Negate precisely: “For every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a positive integer $N$ such that, for every $n\ge N$, $|a_n-L|<\varepsilon$.”
Worked solution

Reverse each quantifier in order and negate the final inequality:

$$\boxed{\text{There exists }\varepsilon>0\text{ such that for every }N\in\mathbb N,\text{ there exists }n\ge N\text{ with }|a_n-L|\ge\varepsilon.}$$

This says that some fixed tolerance is violated infinitely far along the sequence.

P12
Show that $P\Rightarrow Q$ is logically equivalent to $\neg(P\land\neg Q)$, without using a truth table.
Worked solution

If $P\Rightarrow Q$ holds, then $P$ and $\neg Q$ cannot both hold, so $\neg(P\land\neg Q)$ follows.

Conversely, assume $\neg(P\land\neg Q)$. If $P$ is true, $\neg Q$ cannot be true; hence $Q$ is true. Therefore $P\Rightarrow Q$.

P13
Prove that $n^3-n$ is divisible by $6$ for every integer $n$.
Worked solution

Factor the expression:

$$n^3-n=n(n-1)(n+1).$$

The factors $n-1,n,n+1$ are three consecutive integers. Among any three consecutive integers, exactly one is divisible by $3$. Also, among any two consecutive integers at least one is even, so this product is divisible by $2$.

Because $\gcd(2,3)=1$, divisibility by both $2$ and $3$ implies divisibility by $6$. Therefore $6\mid(n^3-n)$ for every integer $n$.

P14
Prove that $a^2+b^2$ is odd if and only if the integers $a$ and $b$ have opposite parity.
Worked solution

A square has the same parity as its base: even squares are even and odd squares are odd.

Forward: if $a^2+b^2$ is odd, the two squares cannot have the same parity, so $a,b$ have opposite parity.

Reverse: if $a,b$ have opposite parity, one square is even and the other odd, so their sum is odd.

P15
Let $r$ be rational and $x$ irrational. Prove that $r+x$ is irrational. Explain why the corresponding claim for $rx$ needs an extra condition.
Worked solution

If $r+x$ were rational, then $x=(r+x)-r$ would be a difference of rational numbers and hence rational, a contradiction.

For products, $r\ne0$ is required. If $r\ne0$ and $rx$ were rational, then $x=(rx)/r$ would be rational. When $r=0$, however, $rx=0$ is rational for every irrational $x$.

P16
Prove: if $x+y$ is irrational, then at least one of $x,y$ is irrational. Is the converse true?
Worked solution

Use contraposition. If both $x$ and $y$ are rational, closure of the rationals under addition makes $x+y$ rational. Therefore, if $x+y$ is irrational, at least one summand is irrational.

The converse is false: $x=\sqrt2$ and $y=-\sqrt2$ are irrational but $x+y=0$ is rational.

P17
Prove that $\sqrt2+\sqrt3$ is irrational.
Worked solution

Assume $\sqrt2+\sqrt3=r\in\mathbb Q$. Then $\sqrt3=r-\sqrt2$. Squaring gives

$$3=r^2+2-2r\sqrt2,\qquad 2r\sqrt2=r^2-1.$$

Here $r\ne0$, so $\sqrt2=(r^2-1)/(2r)$ would be rational, contradicting the irrationality of $\sqrt2$. Hence the sum is irrational.

P18
Euclid challenge: prove that there are infinitely many prime numbers.
Worked solution

Assume there are only finitely many primes $p_1,\ldots,p_k$. Form

$$N=p_1p_2\cdots p_k+1.$$

Every integer greater than $1$ has a prime divisor, say $q\mid N$. But division of $N$ by any listed $p_i$ leaves remainder $1$, so $q$ is not on the complete list. This contradiction proves there are infinitely many primes.

P19
Show that there exist irrational numbers $a,b$ for which $a^b$ is rational, without needing to decide whether $\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$ is rational.
Worked solution

Let $c=\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}$. If $c$ is rational, choose $a=b=\sqrt2$.

If $c$ is irrational, choose $a=c$ and $b=\sqrt2$. Then

$$a^b=\left(\sqrt2^{\sqrt2}\right)^{\sqrt2}=\sqrt2^{\,2}=2,$$

which is rational. One of the two cases must hold, so the required irrational pair exists.

P20
Disprove: “If $6\mid ab$, then $6\mid a$ or $6\mid b$.” Then state a nearby true theorem.
Worked solution

Take $a=2$, $b=3$. Then $6\mid ab$, but $6$ divides neither factor.

A nearby true theorem is Euclid’s lemma: if a prime $p$ divides $ab$, then $p\mid a$ or $p\mid b$. The failed claim treats the composite number $6$ as though it were prime.

P21
Prove that $4\mid n^2$ if and only if $n$ is even.
Worked solution

Reverse: if $n=2k$, then $n^2=4k^2$, so $4\mid n^2$.

Forward: prove the contrapositive. If $n=2k+1$ is odd, then $n^2=4(k^2+k)+1$, which is not divisible by $4$. Thus $4\mid n^2$ implies $n$ is even.

P22
Prove that the equation $x^2-y^2=2026$ has no integer solutions.
Worked solution

Every square is congruent to $0$ or $1\pmod4$. Therefore a difference of two squares is congruent to $0,1$, or $-1\equiv3\pmod4$, never $2\pmod4$.

But $2026\equiv2\pmod4$. Hence no integer pair $(x,y)$ can satisfy the equation.

P23
Let $p$ be prime. Prove that if $p\mid a^2$, then $p\mid a$.
Worked solution

By the fundamental theorem of arithmetic, write the prime factorisation of $a$. Squaring doubles every prime exponent. If $p\mid a^2$, the exponent of $p$ in $a^2$ is positive, so the exponent of $p$ in $a$ was already positive. Hence $p\mid a$.

This is the prime-divisor fact used in standard irrational-square-root proofs.

P24
Find the flaw in the classic argument below: $$a=b\Rightarrow a^2=ab\Rightarrow a^2-b^2=ab-b^2\Rightarrow(a-b)(a+b)=b(a-b)\Rightarrow a+b=b\Rightarrow2=1.$$
Worked solution

The cancellation of $a-b$ is illegal. The opening assumption $a=b$ makes $a-b=0$, so that step divides both sides by zero. Every earlier algebraic line is valid; the contradiction is manufactured exactly at the cancellation.

P26
Prove that the equation $x^3+x=1$ has exactly one real solution.
Worked solution

Let $f(x)=x^3+x-1$. Since $f(0)=-1$ and $f(1)=1$, continuity and the intermediate value theorem give at least one root in $(0,1)$.

For $y>x$:

$$f(y)-f(x)=(y-x)(y^2+xy+x^2+1)>0,$$

because $y-x>0$ and $y^2+xy+x^2=\left(y+\frac x2\right)^2+\frac34x^2\ge0$. Thus $f$ is strictly increasing and cannot have two roots. The root is therefore unique.

P27
Generalise the $\sqrt5$ argument: prove that $\sqrt p$ is irrational for every prime $p$.
Worked solution

Assume $\sqrt p=a/b$ in lowest terms. Then $a^2=pb^2$, so $p\mid a^2$ and therefore $p\mid a$. Write $a=pc$. Substitution gives $p^2c^2=pb^2$, hence $b^2=pc^2$, so $p\mid b$. This contradicts $\gcd(a,b)=1$. Therefore $\sqrt p$ is irrational.

P28
Construct irrational numbers $x,y$ such that both $x+y$ and $xy$ are rational.
Worked solution

Choose conjugate surds so their irrational parts cancel under addition and pair under multiplication. Let

$$x=\sqrt2,\qquad y=-\sqrt2.$$

Both numbers are irrational: changing the sign of an irrational number cannot make it rational. Yet

$$x+y=0,\qquad xy=-2,$$

and both results are rational. More generally, $\sqrt m$ and $-\sqrt m$ work for any positive nonsquare integer $m$.

P29
Prove that $\log_2 3$ is irrational.
Worked solution

Assume $\log_2 3=a/b\in\mathbb Q$ in lowest terms, with $b>0$. Then

$$2^{a/b}=3\quad\Rightarrow\quad2^a=3^b.$$

The left side has only the prime factor $2$, while the right side has only the prime factor $3$. Unique prime factorisation makes equality impossible. Hence $\log_2 3$ is irrational.

P30
A student tries to prove “if $n^2$ is odd, then $n$ is odd” by writing $n^2=2k+1$ and $n=\sqrt{2k+1}$, then declaring this odd. Diagnose the failure and give a valid proof.
Worked solution

The form $\sqrt{2k+1}$ is not the definition of an odd integer; it does not exhibit $n$ as $2m+1$ for an integer $m$.

Use contraposition. If $n$ is even, $n=2m$, then $n^2=4m^2=2(2m^2)$ is even. Therefore, if $n^2$ is odd, $n$ must be odd.
