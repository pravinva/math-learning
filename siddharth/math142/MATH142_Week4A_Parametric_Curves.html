Start here The big picture — what this week is about

Not every curve is the graph of a function $y=f(x)$. Circles, loops, projectile paths, and cam profiles often need two equations driven by a parameter $t$: $x=x(t)$, $y=y(t)$. That is a parametrized curve. Once you have it, calculus becomes derivatives and integrals with respect to $t$ — slope, arc length, and (with polar or Cartesian conversion) area.

This sub-week sits beside Week 4 (polar). Polar is one special parametrization ($x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$). Here we treat general parametric form, which the MATH142 outline lists together with polar under “calculus with parametrized and polar curves.”

Why this matters

Engineering paths are almost always parametric in time: a robot arm tip, a CNC toolpath, a satellite orbit. Arc length of a parametric path is the distance travelled; $dy/dx=(dy/dt)/(dx/dt)$ is the slope of the track.

The roadmap

Section 0: slope and tangents for $x(t),y(t)$. Section 1: parametric arc length and area under a parametric curve. Then practice.

Lay of the land — this week's toolkit
  • Slope: $dy/dx=y'(t)/x'(t)$ when $x'\ne0$.
  • Arc length: $L=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}\,dt$.
  • Area: $A=\int_{t_1}^{t_2} y(t)x'(t)\,dt$ (with orientation care).
  • Link to polar: $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ is a special parametric case.

If $x=x(t)$ and $y=y(t)$ are differentiable and $x'(t)\ne0$, the chain rule gives $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}=\frac{y'(t)}{x'(t)}.$$ Horizontal tangents where $y'=0$ and $x'\ne0$; vertical where $x'=0$ and $y'\ne0$. A cusp can occur when both vanish.

Key idea

$$\dfrac{dy}{dx}=\dfrac{y'(t)}{x'(t)},\qquad \dfrac{d^2y}{dx^2}=\dfrac{\dfrac{d}{dt}\big(y'/x'\big)}{x'(t)}$$

Worked example

For $x=t^2$, $y=t^3-3t$, find $dy/dx$ and the points with horizontal tangents.

$y'/x'=(3t^2-3)/(2t)$. Horizontal when $3t^2-3=0$ and $t\ne0$: $t=\pm1$, giving points $(1,-2)$ and $(1,2)$.

Practice 1

For $x=\cos t$, $y=\sin t$, find $dy/dx$ and simplify. What curve is this?

Solution

$dy/dx=(\cos t)/(-\sin t)=-\cot t$. Eliminating $t$: $x^2+y^2=1$, the unit circle.

Practice 2

For $x=t^3-3t$, $y=t^2$, find where the tangent is vertical.

Solution

$x'=3t^2-3=0\Rightarrow t=\pm1$. At those $t$, $y'=2t\ne0$, so vertical tangents at $(-2,1)$ and $(2,1)$.

Practice 3

Find the equation of the tangent line to $x=e^t$, $y=te^{2t}$ at $t=0$.

Solution

At $t=0$: $(x,y)=(1,0)$. $dy/dx=(e^{2t}+2te^{2t})/e^t=e^t(1+2t)$. At $0$: slope $1$. Tangent: $y-0=1(x-1)\Rightarrow y=x-1$.

Practice 4

Consider $x=t^2+1$, $y=t^3-t$.

(a) Eliminate $t$ to find a Cartesian relation between $x$ and $y$.

(b) Find $dy/dx$ in terms of $t$, and the equations of the tangent and normal at $t=1$.

Solution

(a) $x-1=t^2$, so $y=t(t^2-1)=t\bigl((x-1)-1\bigr)=t(x-2)$. Squaring: $y^2=t^2(x-2)^2=(x-1)(x-2)^2$.

(b) $x'=2t$, $y'=3t^2-1$, so $dy/dx=(3t^2-1)/(2t)$ for $t\ne0$. At $t=1$: point $(2,0)$, slope $(3-1)/2=1$. Tangent: $y=1\cdot(x-2)$, i.e. $y=x-2$. Normal (slope $-1$): $y=-1\cdot(x-2)$, i.e. $y=2-x$.

Practice 5

For the folium-type path $x=\dfrac{3t}{1+t^3}$, $y=\dfrac{3t^2}{1+t^3}$ (with $t\ne-1$):

(a) Compute $dy/dx$ and simplify.

(b) Find $d^2y/dx^2$ at $t=1$, and state whether the curve is concave up or down there.

Solution

(a) Quotient rule: $x'=\dfrac{3(1+t^3)-3t\cdot3t^2}{(1+t^3)^2}=\dfrac{3(1-2t^3)}{(1+t^3)^2}$, $y'=\dfrac{3\bigl(2t(1+t^3)-t^2\cdot3t^2\bigr)}{(1+t^3)^2}=\dfrac{3t(2-t^3)}{(1+t^3)^2}$. Hence $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{y'}{x'}=\frac{t(2-t^3)}{1-2t^3}\quad(t^3\ne\tfrac12).$$

(b) Let $m(t)=y'/x'$. Then $d^2y/dx^2=m'(t)/x'(t)$. At $t=1$: $m(1)=(1\cdot1)/(1-2)=-1$, and differentiating $m=(2t-t^4)/(1-2t^3)$ gives $$m'=\frac{(2-4t^3)(1-2t^3)-(2t-t^4)(-6t^2)}{(1-2t^3)^2}.$$ At $t=1$: numerator $(2-4)(1-2)-(2-1)(-6)=(-2)(-1)-(-6)=2+6=8$, denominator $1$, so $m'(1)=8$. Also $x'(1)=3(1-2)/(2)^2=-3/4$. Thus $d^2y/dx^2=8/(-3/4)=-32/3<0$: concave down at $(3/2,3/2)$.

Practice 6

A cam profile is traced by $x=t^3$, $y=t^2$ for $t\in\mathbb{R}$ (semicubical parabola).

(a) Find all $t$ where $x'=y'=0$ (singular parameters). Classify the point by examining the limiting slope $dy/dx$ from each side.

(b) Separately, for the related profile $x=t^3-3t$, $y=t^2-2$, find all horizontal and vertical tangents (where exactly one of $x'$, $y'$ vanishes).

Solution

(a) $x'=3t^2$, $y'=2t$. Both vanish only at $t=0$, the point $(0,0)$. For $t\ne0$, $dy/dx=(2t)/(3t^2)=2/(3t)$. As $t\to0^+$ the slope $\to+\infty$; as $t\to0^-$ the slope $\to-\infty$. The curve meets at a sharp cusp with a vertical limiting tangent; the parametrization has zero speed at the tip.

(b) Now $x'=3(t^2-1)$, $y'=2t$. Horizontal: $y'=0$ and $x'\ne0$ $\Rightarrow$ $t=0$, point $(0,-2)$, slope $0$. Vertical: $x'=0$ and $y'\ne0$ $\Rightarrow$ $t=\pm1$, points $(-2,-1)$ and $(2,-1)$. (Here speed never vanishes: at $t=0$, $x'=-3\ne0$.)

Practice 7

A CNC tool follows $x=2\cos t+\cos 2t$, $y=2\sin t-\sin 2t$ (a deltoid / hypocycloid), $t\in[0,2\pi]$.

(a) Show that $dy/dx=-\dfrac{\cos t-\cos 2t}{\sin t+\sin 2t}$ (simplify the derivatives).

(b) At a cusp the speed is zero. Find the cusp parameters and the corresponding points. Interpret what the cutter is doing at those instants.

Solution

(a) $x'=-2\sin t-2\sin 2t$, $y'=2\cos t-2\cos 2t$. Thus $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{2(\cos t-\cos 2t)}{-2(\sin t+\sin 2t)}=-\frac{\cos t-\cos 2t}{\sin t+\sin 2t}.$$

(b) Speed zero $\Leftrightarrow$ $x'=y'=0$. From $y'=2(\cos t-\cos 2t)=0$: $\cos t=\cos 2t=2\cos^2 t-1$, so $2\cos^2 t-\cos t-1=0\Rightarrow(\cos t-1)(2\cos t+1)=0$. Cases: $\cos t=1$ ($t=0,2\pi$) or $\cos t=-1/2$ ($t=2\pi/3,4\pi/3$). Check $x'$: at $t=0$, $x'=-2\sin0-2\sin0=0$. At $t=2\pi/3$: $\sin(2\pi/3)=\sqrt3/2$, $\sin(4\pi/3)=-\sqrt3/2$, so $x'=-2(\sqrt3/2)-2(-\sqrt3/2)=0$. Similarly $t=4\pi/3$. Points: $(3,0)$, $(-3/2,3\sqrt3/2)$, $(-3/2,-3\sqrt3/2)$. At each cusp the tool briefly stops and reverses direction of travel along the path — a sharp corner of the deltoid.

Practice 8

Let $x=\sinh t$, $y=\cosh t$ for $t\in\mathbb{R}$.

(a) Eliminate $t$ and identify the curve.

(b) Find $dy/dx$ and $d^2y/dx^2$. Where is the curve concave up?

(c) Write the tangent line at $t=\ln 2$.

Solution

(a) $y^2-x^2=\cosh^2 t-\sinh^2 t=1$, the right branch of the hyperbola $y=\sqrt{1+x^2}$ (since $\cosh t>0$).

(b) $dy/dx=\sinh t/\cosh t=\tanh t$. Then $$\frac{d^2y}{dx^2}=\frac{\mathrm{sech}^2 t}{\cosh t}=\mathrm{sech}^3 t>0$$ for all $t$, so the branch is always concave up.

(c) At $t=\ln2$: $x=\sinh(\ln2)=(e^{\ln2}-e^{-\ln2})/2=(2-1/2)/2=3/4$, $y=\cosh(\ln2)=(2+1/2)/2=5/4$, slope $\tanh(\ln2)=(3/4)/(5/4)=3/5$. Tangent: $y-5/4=(3/5)(x-3/4)$.

Practice 9

A projectile path (ideal, no drag) is $x=v_0\cos\alpha\cdot t$, $y=v_0\sin\alpha\cdot t-\tfrac12 gt^2$, with $v_0>0$, $0<\alpha<\pi/2$, $g>0$.

(a) Find $dy/dx$ as a function of $t$, and the slope at launch ($t=0$) and at the apex.

(b) Find the normal line to the trajectory at the apex, and interpret its geometric meaning for the velocity.

Solution

(a) $x'=v_0\cos\alpha$ (constant), $y'=v_0\sin\alpha-gt$, so $$\frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{v_0\sin\alpha-gt}{v_0\cos\alpha}=\tan\alpha-\frac{gt}{v_0\cos\alpha}.$$ At launch $t=0$: slope $\tan\alpha$. Apex when $y'=0$: $t_*=(v_0\sin\alpha)/g$, slope $0$ (horizontal tangent).

(b) At the apex the tangent is horizontal, so the normal is vertical: $x=x(t_*)=(v_0^2\sin\alpha\cos\alpha)/g=(v_0^2\sin 2\alpha)/(2g)$. Velocity is $(x',y')=(v_0\cos\alpha,0)$ — purely horizontal — so the normal is orthogonal to the velocity (and to the path). Geometrically the normal is the vertical line through the highest point; gravity acts along this normal.

Practice 10

A particle traces $x=t-t^3$, $y=t^2$.

(a) Find $dy/dx$ in terms of $t$.

(b) Find all horizontal and vertical tangent points.

(c) At which parameters (if any) does the particle stop ($x'=y'=0$)? Classify that event.

Solution

(a) $x'=1-3t^2$, $y'=2t$, so $dy/dx=2t/(1-3t^2)$ for $t^2\ne1/3$.

(b) Horizontal: $y'=0$, $x'\ne0$ $\Rightarrow$ $t=0$, point $(0,0)$. Vertical: $x'=0$, $y'\ne0$ $\Rightarrow$ $t=\pm1/\sqrt3$, points $\bigl(\pm\tfrac{2}{3\sqrt3},\tfrac13\bigr)$.

(c) $x'=y'=0$ would need $t=0$ and $1-3t^2=0$ simultaneously — impossible. Speed never vanishes; vertical tangents are not cusps.

Answer: $dy/dx=2t/(1-3t^2)$; H at $(0,0)$; V at $t=\pm1/\sqrt3$; no cusp.

Practice 11

For $x=\ln t$, $y=t+1/t$ with $t>0$:

(a) Eliminate $t$ to obtain a Cartesian equation.

(b) Find $dy/dx$ and locate the minimum of $y$ on $t>0$.

(c) Write the tangent at $t=1$.

Solution

(a) $t=e^x$, so $y=e^x+e^{-x}=2\cosh x$.

(b) $x'=1/t$, $y'=1-1/t^2$, so $dy/dx=t(1-1/t^2)=t-1/t$. Horizontal when $y'=0$: $t=1$ (since $t>0$), point $(0,2)$. This is the global minimum of $y=t+1/t$ on $(0,\infty)$ by AM–GM.

(c) Slope $0$ at $(0,2)$: tangent $y=2$.

Answer: $y=2\cosh x$; $dy/dx=t-1/t$; min at $(0,2)$; tangent $y=2$.

Practice 12

The curve $x=\cos^3 t$, $y=\sin^3 t$ (astroid) for $t\in[0,2\pi]$.

(a) Find $dy/dx$ and simplify using trig identities.

(b) Find the four cusp parameters and points.

(c) Explain why $dy/dx$ is undefined (or infinite in the limit) at those cusps even though the Cartesian relation $x^{2/3}+y^{2/3}=1$ has vertical/horizontal tangent segments nearby.

Solution

(a) $x'=-3\cos^2 t\sin t$, $y'=3\sin^2 t\cos t$, so for $\cos t\sin t\ne0$: $dy/dx=(\sin^2 t\cos t)/(-\cos^2 t\sin t)=-\tan t$.

(b) Speed zero when $x'=y'=0$: $\sin t=0$ or $\cos t=0$ forces both factors to vanish appropriately — cusps at $t=0,\pi/2,\pi,3\pi/2$ giving $(1,0)$, $(0,1)$, $(-1,0)$, $(0,-1)$.

(c) At a cusp the parametrization has $x'=y'=0$, so the formula $y'/x'$ is $0/0$. One-sided limits of $-\tan t$ blow up or jump; the geometric tangent still exists as a coordinate axis, but the speed-zero singularity makes the parametric slope formula fail.

Answer: $dy/dx=-\tan t$; cusps at the axis intercepts $(\pm1,0)$, $(0,\pm1)$.

Practice 13

Two particles: $A$ follows $x=t$, $y=t^2$; $B$ follows $x=2s$, $y=4s-s^2$.

(a) Do their paths intersect? Find candidate $(x,y)$ by eliminating parameters.

(b) At any intersection point that occurs at some $t$ and $s$, compare the slopes $dy/dx$ of the two paths. Do they collide with equal direction?

Solution

(a) Path $A$: $y=x^2$. Path $B$: $x=2s\Rightarrow s=x/2$, $y=4(x/2)-(x/2)^2=2x-x^2/4$. Set $x^2=2x-x^2/4\Rightarrow (5/4)x^2-2x=0\Rightarrow x\bigl(\tfrac54 x-2\bigr)=0$. Points $(0,0)$ and $(8/5,64/25)$.

(b) Slope $A$: $2t=2x$. Slope $B$: $y'_s/x'_s=(4-2s)/2=2-s=2-x/2$. At $(0,0)$: both slopes $0$ — same tangent. At $(8/5,64/25)$: $A$ has slope $16/5$; $B$ has $2-4/5=6/5$ — different directions (crossing, not tangent collision).

Answer: intersections $(0,0)$ (same slope $0$) and $(8/5,64/25)$ (slopes $16/5$ vs $6/5$).

Practice 14

For $x=e^{-t}\cos t$, $y=e^{-t}\sin t$ (logarithmic spiral / damped circle):

(a) Compute $dy/dx$ and simplify.

(b) Show that the angle $\psi$ between the tangent and the radius vector satisfies $\tan\psi=1$ (constant). Hint: radius slope is $y/x$; use the formula for the angle between two slopes.

Solution

(a) $x'=e^{-t}(-\cos t-\sin t)$, $y'=e^{-t}(-\sin t+\cos t)$, so $dy/dx=(\cos t-\sin t)/(-\cos t-\sin t)$.

(b) Radius slope $m_r=y/x=\tan t$. Tangent slope $m=dy/dx$. Then $\tan\psi=\bigl|(m-m_r)/(1+mm_r)\bigr|$. Substituting and simplifying with the expressions above yields $|\tan\psi|=1$ for all $t$ with defined slopes — the classic equiangular property.

Answer: $dy/dx=(\cos t-\sin t)/(-(\cos t+\sin t))$; $\tan\psi=1$.

Practice 15

A beam centreline is modelled by $x=t$, $y=\dfrac{t^3}{6}-\dfrac{t}{2}$ on $[-\sqrt3,\sqrt3]$.

(a) Find inflection parameters using $d^2y/dx^2=0$ (here $x=t$, so ordinary derivatives work — verify with the parametric formula too).

(b) Find the tangent line at an inflection point.

Solution

(a) Since $x=t$, $dy/dx=y'=t^2/2-1/2$, $d^2y/dx^2=t$. Inflection at $t=0$, point $(0,0)$. Parametric check: $m(t)=y'/x'=t^2/2-1/2$, $m'/x'=t$, same.

(b) Slope at $t=0$ is $-1/2$. Tangent: $y=-\tfrac12 x$.

Answer: inflection at $(0,0)$; tangent $y=-x/2$.

Practice 16

Consider $x=t^2$, $y=t^3-3t$ (same family as the worked example).

(a) Find $d^2y/dx^2$ in terms of $t$.

(b) Determine the concavity at the horizontal-tangent points $t=\pm1$.

(c) A student claims "both horizontal points look the same so concavity matches." Refute or confirm with (b).

Solution

(a) $m(t)=(3t^2-3)/(2t)=\tfrac32 t-\tfrac{3}{2t}$ for $t\ne0$. Then $m'(t)=\tfrac32+\tfrac{3}{2t^2}$, $x'=2t$, so $d^2y/dx^2=m'/x'=\bigl(\tfrac32+\tfrac{3}{2t^2}\bigr)/(2t)=\dfrac{3(t^2+1)}{4t^3}$.

(b) At $t=1$: $d^2y/dx^2=3(2)/4=3/2>0$ (concave up) at $(1,-2)$. At $t=-1$: $3(2)/(-4)=-3/2<0$ (concave down) at $(1,2)$.

(c) Same $x$-coordinate and horizontal tangents, but opposite concavity — the claim is false.

Answer: $d^2y/dx^2=3(t^2+1)/(4t^3)$; up at $t=1$, down at $t=-1$.

Practice 17

An ellipse $x=a\cos t$, $y=b\sin t$ with $a>b>0$.

(a) Find the points where the tangent is parallel to $y=x$.

(b) Show that at those points $t$ satisfies $\tan t=-a/b$ or an equivalent relation you derive, and give the corresponding $(x,y)$.

Solution

(a) $dy/dx=(b\cos t)/(-a\sin t)=-(b/a)\cot t$. Set equal to $1$: $-(b/a)\cot t=1\Rightarrow\cot t=-a/b\Rightarrow\tan t=-b/a$.

(b) Let $\tan t=-b/a$. Then $\sin t$ and $\cos t$ share signs in QII/QIV. From $\sin^2+\cos^2=1$ and $\sin/\cos=-b/a$: points $\bigl(\pm a^2/\sqrt{a^2+b^2},\ \mp ab/\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\bigr)$ (two solutions).

Answer: $\tan t=-b/a$; points $\bigl(\pm a^2/\sqrt{a^2+b^2},\mp ab/\sqrt{a^2+b^2}\bigr)$.

Practice 18

Challenge (self-intersection). For $x=t^2-1$, $y=t^3-t$:

(a) Find distinct $t_1\ne t_2$ giving the same point (a crossing).

(b) Compute the two tangent slopes at that point and the angle between the branches.

Solution

(a) Set $t_1^2-1=t_2^2-1$ and $t_1^3-t_1=t_2^3-t_2$ with $t_1\ne t_2$. First $\Rightarrow t_2=\pm t_1$; need $t_2=-t_1\ne t_1$ so $t_1\ne0$. Then $y$: $t_1^3-t_1=(-t_1)^3-(-t_1)=-t_1^3+t_1\Rightarrow 2t_1^3-2t_1=0\Rightarrow t_1(t_1^2-1)=0$. Thus $t_1=\pm1$, $t_2=\mp1$, common point $(0,0)$.

(b) $dy/dx=(3t^2-1)/(2t)$. At $t=1$: slope $1$; at $t=-1$: slope $-1$. Angle between branches: $90^\circ$ (product of slopes $=-1$).

Answer: crosses at $(0,0)$ with perpendicular tangents $y=x$ and $y=-x$.

Arc length follows Pythagoras in $t$: $$L=\int_{t_1}^{t_2}\sqrt{\big(x'(t)\big)^2+\big(y'(t)\big)^2}\,dt.$$ Area under a parametric curve (assuming $y\ge0$ and $x$ increasing) is $$A=\int_{t_1}^{t_2} y(t)\,x'(t)\,dt.$$

Key idea

$$L=\int\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}\,dt,\qquad A=\int y\,x'\,dt$$

Worked example

Arc length of $x=\cos^3 t$, $y=\sin^3 t$ from $t=0$ to $\pi/2$ (astroid quarter).

$x'=-3\cos^2 t\sin t$, $y'=3\sin^2 t\cos t$. Then $(x')^2+(y')^2=9\sin^2 t\cos^2 t$, so $L=\int_0^{\pi/2}3|\sin t\cos t|\,dt=\tfrac32$.

Practice 19

Find the arc length of $x=3t^2$, $y=2t^3$ from $t=0$ to $t=1$.

Solution

$L=\int_0^1\sqrt{(6t)^2+(6t^2)^2}\,dt=\int_0^1 6t\sqrt{1+t^2}\,dt$. Let $u=1+t^2$: $3\int_1^2 u^{1/2}du=2(2\sqrt2-1)$.

Practice 20

Find the area under one arch of the cycloid $x=a(t-\sin t)$, $y=a(1-\cos t)$, $0\le t\le 2\pi$.

Solution

$A=\int_0^{2\pi} a(1-\cos t)\cdot a(1-\cos t)\,dt=a^2\int_0^{2\pi}(1-\cos t)^2\,dt=a^2\int_0^{2\pi}(1-2\cos t+\tfrac{1+\cos2t}{2})\,dt=3\pi a^2$.

Practice 21

For $x=t-\sin t$, $y=1-\cos t$ ($a=1$), set up $L$ on $[0,2\pi]$ and evaluate.

Solution

$L=\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{(1-\cos t)^2+\sin^2 t}\,dt=\int_0^{2\pi}\sqrt{2-2\cos t}\,dt=\int_0^{2\pi}2\big|\sin(t/2)\big|\,dt=8$.

Practice 22

Explain why polar $r=f(\theta)$ is parametric with $x=f(\theta)\cos\theta$, $y=f(\theta)\sin\theta$, and write the polar arc-length formula from the parametric one.

Solution

Parameter is $\theta$. Differentiating and simplifying gives $L=\int\sqrt{r^2+(dr/d\theta)^2}\,d\theta$, the standard polar arc-length formula.

Practice 23

Find $dy/dx$ for the parametric ellipse $x=2\cos t$, $y=3\sin t$, and the slope at $t=\pi/4$.

Solution

$dy/dx=(3\cos t)/(-2\sin t)=-\tfrac32\cot t$. At $\pi/4$: $-\tfrac32$.

Practice 24

For the ellipse $x=a\cos t$, $y=b\sin t$, $0\le t\le 2\pi$ (counterclockwise):

(a) Set up and evaluate the oriented area $A=\oint y\,dx$ enclosed by the ellipse.

(b) What happens to the sign of $\int y\,x'\,dt$ if you reverse the orientation ($t$ from $2\pi$ down to $0$)?

Solution

(a) $x'=-a\sin t$, so $$A=\int_0^{2\pi}(b\sin t)(-a\sin t)\,dt=-ab\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^2 t\,dt=-ab\int_0^{2\pi}\tfrac{1-\cos2t}{2}\,dt=-\pi ab.$$ For counterclockwise orientation, $-\oint y\,dx$ equals the enclosed area, so the geometric area is $\pi ab=|A|$.

(b) Reversing limits flips the sign: $\int_{2\pi}^{0}=-\int_0^{2\pi}$, so the oriented integral becomes $+\pi ab$. Orientation matters for signed area.

Practice 25

Astroid $x=a\cos^3 t$, $y=a\sin^3 t$, $0\le t\le 2\pi$.

(a) Find the total arc length of the full astroid.

(b) Using symmetry, find the enclosed area via $4\int_{t=0}^{\pi/2} y\,x'\,dt$ (take absolute value if needed).

Solution

(a) As in the worked example, $\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}=3a|\sin t\cos t|$. On $[0,2\pi]$, $$L=3a\int_0^{2\pi}|\sin t\cos t|\,dt=3a\cdot4\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin t\cos t\,dt=12a\cdot\tfrac12=6a.$$

(b) On first quadrant $x'=-3a\cos^2 t\sin t\le0$ (so $\int y x'\,dt$ is negative). Enclosed area: $$4\left|\int_0^{\pi/2} a\sin^3 t\cdot(-3a\cos^2 t\sin t)\,dt\right|=12a^2\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 t\cos^2 t\,dt.$$ Using the beta-integral value $$\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 t\cos^2 t\,dt =\frac12B\!\left(\frac52,\frac32\right)=\frac{\pi}{32},$$ the enclosed area is $\boxed{\dfrac{3\pi a^2}{8}}$.

Practice 26

Cycloid $x=a(t-\sin t)$, $y=a(1-\cos t)$.

(a) Show that one arch has length $8a$ (general $a>0$), recovering Practice 21 when $a=1$.

(b) A wheel of radius $a$ rolls without slipping on the $x$-axis. Interpret $L=8a$ in terms of the wheel geometry.

Solution

(a) $x'=a(1-\cos t)$, $y'=a\sin t$, so $$(x')^2+(y')^2=a^2\bigl((1-\cos t)^2+\sin^2 t\bigr)=a^2(2-2\cos t)=4a^2\sin^2(t/2).$$ Thus $L=\int_0^{2\pi} 2a\bigl|\sin(t/2)\bigr|\,dt=4a\int_0^{\pi}\sin u\,du=8a$ (with $u=t/2$).

(b) The marked rim point travels a path of length $8a$ while the wheel rolls through one full turn (centre advances $2\pi a$). The path length $8a$ is larger than the circumference $2\pi a$ because the point moves both with the centre and around the hub — a classic rolling-wheel interpretation of the cycloid.

Practice 27

Consider $x=t^3$, $y=t^2$ from $t=-1$ to $t=1$.

(a) Set up the arc-length integral and explain why you must split at $t=0$ (or use absolute values carefully).

(b) Evaluate $L$. (You may leave a factor of the form $\int_0^1\sqrt{9t^2+4}\,dt$ if you identify a hyperbolic or trig substitution; give the exact antiderivative.)

Solution

(a) $x'=3t^2$, $y'=2t$, so $\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}=\sqrt{9t^4+4t^2}=|t|\sqrt{9t^2+4}$. The factor $|t|$ forces a split at $t=0$ (cusp).

(b) By evenness, $L=2\int_0^1 t\sqrt{9t^2+4}\,dt$. Let $u=9t^2+4$, $du=18t\,dt$: $$L=\frac{1}{9}\int_4^{13}\sqrt{u}\,du=\frac{2}{27}\bigl(13^{3/2}-8\bigr)=\frac{2}{27}\bigl(13\sqrt{13}-8\bigr).$$

Practice 28

Parametric curve $x=\cos^3 t$, $y=\sin^3 t$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ (first-quadrant astroid arc with $a=1$).

(a) Compute the oriented integral $A=\int y\,x'\,dt$ on this interval.

(b) Explain why $A$ is not the area under the graph $y=y(x)$ between the endpoints, and correct the setup for that area.

Solution

(a) $x'=-3\cos^2 t\sin t$, so $$A=\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^3 t(-3\cos^2 t\sin t)\,dt=-3\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 t\cos^2 t\,dt.$$ Since $\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4t\cos^2t\,dt=\pi/32$, the oriented value is $\boxed{A=-3\pi/32}$.

(b) On this interval $x$ decreases from $1$ to $0$, so $\int y\,dx$ runs right-to-left and is negative. Area under the graph (above the $x$-axis, left-to-right) is $$\int_{x=0}^{1}y\,dx=-\int_{t=0}^{\pi/2}y\,x'\,dt=\boxed{\frac{3\pi}{32}}.$$

Practice 29

Link parametric and polar area. For a polar curve $r=f(\theta)$, use $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$ to show that the standard polar area $\tfrac12\int r^2\,d\theta$ equals $-\tfrac12\int\bigl(y\,x'-x\,y'\bigr)\,d\theta$ (Green/sector form), and verify on the circle $r=a$.

Solution

With parameter $\theta$: $x'=r'\cos\theta-r\sin\theta$, $y'=r'\sin\theta+r\cos\theta$. Then $$yx'-xy'=r\sin\theta(r'\cos\theta-r\sin\theta)-r\cos\theta(r'\sin\theta+r\cos\theta)=-r^2.$$ Hence $-\tfrac12\int(yx'-xy')\,d\theta=\tfrac12\int r^2\,d\theta$. For $r=a$ on $[0,2\pi]$: $\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}a^2\,d\theta=\pi a^2$.

Practice 30

Numerical / setup. A robot arm tip follows $x=e^{-t}\cos(4t)$, $y=e^{-t}\sin(4t)$ for $0\le t\le 2$ (a decaying spiral).

(a) Write the exact arc-length integrand $\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}$ in simplified closed form.

(b) Set up $L$ as a definite integral (do not numerically approximate unless you wish). Explain briefly how you would estimate it in practice.

Solution

(a) $x'=e^{-t}(-\cos4t-4\sin4t)$, $y'=e^{-t}(-\sin4t+4\cos4t)$. Then $$(x')^2+(y')^2=e^{-2t}\bigl[(\cos4t+4\sin4t)^2+(\sin4t-4\cos4t)^2\bigr]=e^{-2t}(1+16)=17e^{-2t}.$$ So $\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}=\sqrt{17}\,e^{-t}$.

(b) $L=\sqrt{17}\int_0^2 e^{-t}\,dt=\sqrt{17}\,(1-e^{-2})$ (exact). In practice, if the integrand did not simplify, use a numerical quadrature (Simpson, Gauss, or adaptive) on the sampled speed $\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}$.

Practice 31

Enclosed area of a loop. The curve $x=t^2$, $y=t^3-3t$ has a loop for $t\in[-\sqrt3,\sqrt3]$.

(a) Confirm the loop closes by checking the endpoints.

(b) Compute the enclosed area using $A=\bigl|\int y\,x'\,dt\bigr|$ over that interval.

Solution

(a) At $t=\pm\sqrt3$: $x=3$, $y=\pm3\sqrt3-3(\pm\sqrt3)=0$. Both endpoints are $(3,0)$; the curve returns to the same point, forming a closed loop.

(b) $x'=2t$, so $$\int_{-\sqrt3}^{\sqrt3}(t^3-3t)(2t)\,dt=2\int_{-\sqrt3}^{\sqrt3}(t^4-3t^2)\,dt=4\int_0^{\sqrt3}(t^4-3t^2)\,dt=4\Bigl[\tfrac15 t^5-t^3\Bigr]_0^{\sqrt3}=4\bigl(\tfrac{9\sqrt3}{5}-3\sqrt3\bigr)=4\cdot\bigl(-\tfrac{6\sqrt3}{5}\bigr)=-\tfrac{24\sqrt3}{5}.$$ Enclosed area $=\tfrac{24\sqrt3}{5}$.

Practice 32

Ellipse arc length (setup / incomplete elliptic integral). For $x=a\cos t$, $y=b\sin t$ with $a>b>0$, show that the perimeter is $$L=4a\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2 t}\,dt,\qquad e=\sqrt{1-(b/a)^2},$$ the complete elliptic integral of the second kind. Do not evaluate in elementary functions — explain why.

Solution

$x'=-a\sin t$, $y'=b\cos t$, so $$\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}=\sqrt{a^2\sin^2 t+b^2\cos^2 t}=a\sqrt{\sin^2 t+(b/a)^2\cos^2 t}=a\sqrt{1-e^2\cos^2 t}$$ after writing $b^2=a^2(1-e^2)$. Equivalently (shift $t\mapsto\pi/2-t$) $a\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2 t}$. Full perimeter: four congruent quadrants give $L=4a\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{1-e^2\sin^2 t}\,dt=4a\,E(e)$. This is not elementary for $0<e<1$; it defines the special function $E$.

Practice 33

Polar–parametric hybrid. The cardioid $r=a(1+\cos\theta)$ is parametric via $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$.

(a) Using the polar arc-length formula derived in Practice 22, find the total length of the cardioid.

(b) Using $\tfrac12\int r^2\,d\theta$, find the enclosed area, and relate one line of the calculation to $\int y\,x'\,d\theta$.

Solution

(a) $dr/d\theta=-a\sin\theta$, so $r^2+(r')^2=a^2(1+\cos\theta)^2+a^2\sin^2\theta=a^2(2+2\cos\theta)=4a^2\cos^2(\theta/2)$. Thus $$L=\int_0^{2\pi} 2a\bigl|\cos(\theta/2)\bigr|\,d\theta=8a.$$

(b) Area $=\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}a^2(1+\cos\theta)^2\,d\theta=\tfrac{a^2}{2}\int_0^{2\pi}(1+2\cos\theta+\tfrac{1+\cos2\theta}{2})\,d\theta=\tfrac{3}{2}\pi a^2$. By Practice 29, this equals $-\tfrac12\oint(yx'-xy')\,d\theta$; for a simple closed polar graph both formulas agree on the enclosed region.

Practice 34

Find the arc length of $x=e^t\cos t$, $y=e^t\sin t$ from $t=0$ to $t=\pi$. Interpret the result geometrically.

Solution

$x'=e^t(\cos t-\sin t)$, $y'=e^t(\sin t+\cos t)$, so $(x')^2+(y')^2=2e^{2t}$ and $L=\int_0^\pi \sqrt2\, e^t\,dt=\sqrt2(e^\pi-1)$.

This is the length of one half-turn of the expanding logarithmic spiral (radius grows by $e^\pi$).

Answer: $L=\sqrt2(e^\pi-1)$.

Practice 35

For the ellipse $x=a\cos t$, $y=b\sin t$, set up the perimeter integral. Explain why it is an elliptic integral (cannot be elementary for $a\ne b$), and evaluate the special case $a=b=R$.

Solution

$L=4\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{a^2\sin^2 t+b^2\cos^2 t}\,dt=4\int_0^{\pi/2}\sqrt{a^2-(a^2-b^2)\cos^2 t}\,dt$ (standard elliptic form of the second kind). No elementary antiderivative when $a\ne b$.

If $a=b=R$: $L=4\int_0^{\pi/2}R\,dt=2\pi R$.

Answer: elliptic integral in general; $2\pi R$ when circular.

Practice 36

A wire is bent along $x=t$, $y=\cosh t$ from $t=-1$ to $t=1$ (catenary).

(a) Find its length.

(b) Find the area between the wire and the $x$-axis on that interval.

Solution

(a) $x'=1$, $y'=\sinh t$, $L=\int_{-1}^1\sqrt{1+\sinh^2 t}\,dt=\int_{-1}^1\cosh t\,dt=2\sinh1$.

(b) $A=\int_{-1}^1 y\,x'\,dt=\int_{-1}^1\cosh t\,dt=2\sinh1$ (same value here by coincidence of the integrands).

Answer: $L=A=2\sinh1$.

Practice 37

Orientation trap. The curve $x=\sin t$, $y=\sin^2 t$ for $t\in[0,\pi]$ traces the parabola $y=x^2$ from $(0,0)$ to $(0,0)$ via $(1,1)$ then back.

(a) Compute $\int_0^\pi y(t)x'(t)\,dt$ blindly.

(b) Explain why this is not the geometric area under $y=x^2$ on $[0,1]$, and give the correct area.

Solution

(a) $x'=\cos t$, $y=\sin^2 t$, so $\int_0^\pi \sin^2 t\cos t\,dt=\bigl[\tfrac13\sin^3 t\bigr]_0^\pi=0$.

(b) On $[0,\pi/2]$ the path goes right ($x'>0$); on $[\pi/2,\pi]$ it returns left ($x'<0$), cancelling the integral. Geometric area under $y=x^2$ on $[0,1]$ is $\int_0^1 x^2\,dx=1/3$ (use $t\in[0,\pi/2]$ only, or Cartesian).

Answer: parametric integral $0$; true area $1/3$.

Practice 38

Find the surface area obtained by rotating $x=t^3$, $y=t^2$ for $0\le t\le1$ about the $x$-axis. (Formula: $S=2\pi\int y\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}\,dt$.)

Solution

$x'=3t^2$, $y'=2t$, so $\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}=t\sqrt{9t^2+4}$ for $t\ge0$.

$S=2\pi\int_0^1 t^2\cdot t\sqrt{9t^2+4}\,dt=2\pi\int_0^1 t^3\sqrt{9t^2+4}\,dt$.

Substitute $u=9t^2+4$, so $t^2=(u-4)/9$ and $du=18t\,dt$. Then $t^3\sqrt{9t^2+4}\,dt=t^2\cdot(t\sqrt{\cdots}\,dt)=\dfrac{u-4}{9}\cdot\dfrac{1}{18}u^{1/2}\,du=\dfrac{1}{162}(u^{3/2}-4u^{1/2})\,du$.

Limits $t=0\to1$ give $u=4\to13$: $\int_0^1 t^3\sqrt{9t^2+4}\,dt=\dfrac{1}{162}\Bigl[\dfrac{2}{5}u^{5/2}-\dfrac{8}{3}u^{3/2}\Bigr]_4^{13}$.

Hence $S=\dfrac{2\pi}{162}\Bigl[\dfrac{2}{5}13^{5/2}-\dfrac{8}{3}13^{3/2}-\bigl(\dfrac{2}{5}\cdot32-\dfrac{8}{3}\cdot8\bigr)\Bigr]=\dfrac{\pi}{81}\Bigl[\dfrac{2}{5}13^{5/2}-\dfrac{8}{3}13^{3/2}+\dfrac{128}{15}\Bigr]$, using $-\bigl(\tfrac{64}{5}-\tfrac{64}{3}\bigr)=\tfrac{128}{15}$.

Answer: $S=\dfrac{\pi}{81}\left(\dfrac{2}{5}13^{5/2}-\dfrac{8}{3}13^{3/2}+\dfrac{128}{15}\right)$.

Practice 39

One arch of the cycloid $x=a(t-\sin t)$, $y=a(1-\cos t)$, $0\le t\le 2\pi$, is rotated about the $x$-axis. Find the surface area.

Solution

Known: $\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}=a\sqrt{2-2\cos t}=2a\bigl|\sin(t/2)\bigr|$. On $[0,2\pi]$, $S=2\pi\int_0^{2\pi} y\cdot 2a\sin(t/2)\,dt$ with $y=a(1-\cos t)=2a\sin^2(t/2)$.

$S=2\pi\int_0^{2\pi} 2a\sin^2(t/2)\cdot 2a\sin(t/2)\,dt=8\pi a^2\int_0^{2\pi}\sin^3(t/2)\,dt$.

Let $u=t/2$: $16\pi a^2\int_0^\pi \sin^3 u\,du=16\pi a^2\int_0^\pi(1-\cos^2 u)\sin u\,du=16\pi a^2\bigl[-\cos u+\tfrac13\cos^3 u\bigr]_0^\pi=16\pi a^2\bigl(2-\tfrac23\bigr)=\dfrac{64\pi a^2}{3}$.

Answer: $\dfrac{64}{3}\pi a^2$.

Practice 40

Speed vs arc length. A robot arm tip follows $x=3\cos 2t$, $y=3\sin 2t$, $0\le t\le\pi$.

(a) Find the speed $|\mathbf r'(t)|$ and the arc length.

(b) How many times does the tip traverse the circle? Reconcile with the arc-length value.

Solution

(a) $x'=-6\sin 2t$, $y'=6\cos 2t$, speed $=6$. $L=\int_0^\pi 6\,dt=6\pi$.

(b) Radius $3$, circumference $6\pi$. In time $\pi$, angle $2t$ advances $2\pi$ — exactly one full lap. Arc length equals one circumference, consistent.

Answer: speed $6$; $L=6\pi$ (one lap).

Practice 41

Find the area enclosed by the parametric curve $x=\cos^3 t$, $y=\sin^3 t$, $0\le t\le 2\pi$ (astroid), using $A=-\oint y\,x'\,dt$ (or an equivalent closed-curve formula). Compare with $4$ times the first-quadrant area under the curve.

Solution

On the first quadrant ($t:0\to\pi/2$), $x$ decreases from $1$ to $0$, so the geometric area under the curve is $A_1=\int_0^{\pi/2} y(t)\bigl(-x'(t)\bigr)\,dt$. With $y=\sin^3 t$ and $-x'=3\cos^2 t\sin t$, $$A_1=3\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 t\cos^2 t\,dt.$$ By Wallis, $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\sin^4 t\cos^2 t\,dt=\dfrac{\pi}{32}$, so $A_1=\dfrac{3\pi}{32}$. Four symmetric quadrants give the enclosed area $A=4A_1=\dfrac{3\pi}{8}$.

(Equivalently, a full closed-curve integral $-\oint y\,x'\,dt$ over $[0,2\pi]$ yields the same total.)

Answer: enclosed area $\dfrac{3\pi}{8}$.

Practice 42

Set up — but do not numerically approximate — the arc length of $x=t$, $y=e^{-t^2}$ from $t=-1$ to $1$. Explain why a closed elementary antiderivative is unlikely, and name a reasonable numerical method from Week 3 to evaluate it.

Solution

$L=\int_{-1}^1\sqrt{1+4t^2 e^{-2t^2}}\,dt$ (even integrand: $2\int_0^1\cdots$).

The integrand mixes polynomial and Gaussian factors under a square root; it is not a standard elementary form. Use Simpson's Rule (even $n$) or trapezoidal with an error bound on a derivative estimate.

Practice 43

Green-type check. For the unit circle $x=\cos t$, $y=\sin t$, $0\le t\le 2\pi$, compute $\tfrac12\oint -y\,dx+x\,dy$ and confirm it equals the enclosed area.

Solution

$dx=-\sin t\,dt$, $dy=\cos t\,dt$. Then $-y\,dx+x\,dy=(-\sin t)(-\sin t\,dt)+\cos t(\cos t\,dt)=(\sin^2 t+\cos^2 t)\,dt=dt$.

$\tfrac12\int_0^{2\pi}1\,dt=\pi$, the unit disk area.

Answer: $\pi$.

Practice 44

A ramp is $x=t$, $y=1-t$ for $0\le t\le1$. Find the area under the ramp by (i) Cartesian and (ii) parametric $A=\int y x'\,dt$. Then find the length of the ramp.

Solution

(i) $y=1-x$ on $[0,1]$: $A=\int_0^1(1-x)\,dx=1/2$.

(ii) $x'=1$, $A=\int_0^1(1-t)\,dt=1/2$.

Practice 45

Reparametrisation invariance. Show that the arc length of $x=t^2$, $y=t^3$ from $t=1$ to $t=2$ equals the arc length of $x=u$, $y=u^{3/2}$ from $u=1$ to $u=4$ (the same geometric curve with $u=t^2$). Compute one of them.

Practice 46

Engineering load path. A cable follows $x=30t$, $y=40t-5t^2$ for $0\le t\le4$ (metres, $t$ dimensionless parameter).

(a) Find the cable length.

(b) Find the horizontal span and the maximum sag relative to the higher support (compare $y(0)$ and $y(4)$ and the minimum of $y$).

Solution

(a) $x'=30$, $y'=40-10t$, so $L=\int_0^4\sqrt{900+(40-10t)^2}\,dt$. Let $u=40-10t$, $du=-10\,dt$; as $t:0\to4$, $u:40\to0$, hence $L=\dfrac{1}{10}\int_0^{40}\sqrt{900+u^2}\,du$. Using $\int\sqrt{a^2+u^2}=\dfrac{u}{2}\sqrt{a^2+u^2}+\dfrac{a^2}{2}\ln\bigl(u+\sqrt{a^2+u^2}\bigr)$ with $a=30$: $$\Bigl[\dfrac{u}{2}\sqrt{900+u^2}+450\ln\bigl(u+\sqrt{900+u^2}\bigr)\Bigr]_0^{40}=1000+450\ln90-450\ln30=1000+450\ln3.$$ Thus $L=100+45\ln3$.

Practice 47

Challenge. The curve $x=\dfrac{1-t^2}{1+t^2}$, $y=\dfrac{2t}{1+t^2}$ is a circle. Identify which circle, note which point is missing for $t\in\mathbb{R}$, compute the arc length as $t$ runs over all reals, and reconcile that length with the geometry of the missing point.

Solution

$x^2+y^2=1$ (rational parametrisation of the unit circle). As $t:\mathbb{R}\to$ circle, the point $(-1,0)$ is never reached (limit $t\to\pm\infty$).

$x'=\dfrac{-4t}{(1+t^2)^2}$, $y'=\dfrac{2(1-t^2)}{(1+t^2)^2}$, $(x')^2+(y')^2=\dfrac{4}{(1+t^2)^2}$, so $L=\int_{-\infty}^\infty \dfrac{2}{1+t^2}\,dt=2\arctan t\big|_{-\infty}^\infty=2\pi$.

Geometrically you traverse the whole circle once in the limit (approaching the missing point from both sides), so length $2\pi$ is correct; the missing point has measure zero.

Answer: unit circle; $L=2\pi$ over $t\in\mathbb{R}$.
