Arc Length & Surfaces of Revolution

The straight-line distance formula measures the distance between two points, but a curved path between them is longer than that straight line. Arc length adapts Pythagoras to an infinite sum of infinitesimally short straight segments hugging the curve — the last topic before Exam 1. Exam-level worked examples and practice throughout. Start here The big picture — what this week is about

You can measure a straight distance with a ruler, but how long is a curved path — a suspension cable’s sag, a winding road over a hill, the trajectory of a projectile, the profile of a turbine blade? You cannot lay a ruler along a curve, but you can approximate it by many tiny straight segments and add their lengths. Taking that sum to the limit gives the arc-length integral.

The formula $L=\int\sqrt{1+\left(\tfrac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx$ is not something to memorise blindly: it is just Pythagoras applied to an infinitesimal step, where a tiny bit of curve is the hypotenuse of a right triangle with legs $dx$ and $dy$. Spin that curve about an axis and the same length element sweeps out a band, giving the surface-area-of-revolution integral. Both often produce exactly the tough integrals you practised in Weeks 1–3.

Why this matters

Arc length is how you cost a run of cable or pipe, compute the length of a conveyor belt or road, find the fabric needed for a curved sail, or measure the wire in a coil. Surface area of revolution sizes the material and the paint / heat-transfer area of tanks, domes, nozzles, and turbine components. This is also where numerical integration earns its keep — most real arc-length integrals have no closed form.

The roadmap — how the pieces connect

We derive the arc-length element from Pythagoras, apply it to curves given as $y=f(x)$ and in parametric form, then extend the same length element to surfaces of revolution. Expect the resulting integrals to call on every technique so far — this week is a proving ground for Weeks 1–3.

Lay of the land — this week's toolkit
  • Arc length formula: $L=\displaystyle\int_a^b \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx$.
  • Derivation: approximate the curve between $x$ and $x+dx$ by a straight segment; by Pythagoras its length is $\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}=\sqrt{1+(dy/dx)^2}\,dx$; sum (integrate) over the whole curve.
  • Connection to trig substitution: $1+[f'(x)]^2$ frequently has the form $a^2+u^2$ once simplified — exactly the pattern trig substitution (Week 1) was built to handle.
  • Exam pattern: problems are often deliberately constructed so $1+[f'(x)]^2$ is a perfect square (via a specific combination like $x^n\pm\frac{1}{cx^n}$) — recognising this shortcut avoids a much harder integral.
  • Surface of revolution (about $x$-axis): $S=\displaystyle\int_a^b 2\pi y\sqrt{1+(y')^2}\,dx$ — arc-length element times circumference $2\pi y$.
  • About $y$-axis: $S=\displaystyle\int_c^d 2\pi x\sqrt{1+(x')^2}\,dy$.

Arclength Formula — Derivation + circumference of a circle

Dr. Trefor Bazett · 10:13

Derives the formula from Pythagoras, then sanity-checks it on a circle.

Open on YouTube ↗

Approximate the curve $y=f(x)$ between $x$ and $x+dx$ by the straight segment connecting $(x,f(x))$ to $(x+dx,f(x+dx))$. By Pythagoras, that tiny segment's length is $\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2}$. Factoring out $dx$:

$$\sqrt{dx^2+dy^2} = \sqrt{1+\left(\frac{dy}{dx}\right)^2}\,dx$$

Summing (integrating) these tiny segments over the whole curve gives the total arc length. These integrals frequently contain a square root of a sum of squares — precisely the pattern trig substitution was built to handle, so arc length is a natural place that technique gets used in practice.

Exam shortcut. Many textbook/exam problems are deliberately constructed so that $1+[f'(x)]^2$ simplifies to a perfect square — typically arising from a function of the form $y=\dfrac{x^{n+1}}{2(n+1)}+\dfrac{x^{1-n}}{2(1-n)}$ or similar combinations, where $f'(x)$ takes the form $x^n-\dfrac{1}{4x^n}$ (or similar), so that $1+[f'(x)]^2=\left(x^n+\dfrac{1}{4x^n}\right)^2$. Recognising this pattern saves substantial work.

Key idea

$$L = \int_a^b \sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx$$

Worked example

Example 1 — simple curve. Find the arc length of $y=\dfrac23x^{3/2}$ from $x=0$ to $x=3$.

$f'(x)=x^{1/2}$; $1+[f'(x)]^2=1+x$. $L=\displaystyle\int_0^3\sqrt{1+x}\,dx = \left[\dfrac23(1+x)^{3/2}\right]_0^3=\dfrac23(8-1)=\dfrac{14}{3}$.

Example 2 — setup needing trig substitution. Set up the arc length of $y=x^2$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$.

$f'(x)=2x$; $L=\displaystyle\int_0^1\sqrt{1+4x^2}\,dx$ — matches the $\sqrt{a^2+x^2}$ pattern, solvable via $2x=\tan\theta$.

Practice 1

Find the arc length of $y=\dfrac{x^3}{3}+\dfrac{1}{4x}$ from $x=1$ to $x=2$ (designed so $1+[f'(x)]^2$ is a perfect square).

Solution

$f'(x)=x^2-\dfrac{1}{4x^2}$; $1+[f'(x)]^2=1+x^4-\dfrac12+\dfrac{1}{16x^4}=x^4+\dfrac12+\dfrac{1}{16x^4}=\left(x^2+\dfrac{1}{4x^2}\right)^2$.

$L=\displaystyle\int_1^2\left(x^2+\dfrac{1}{4x^2}\right)\,dx=\left[\dfrac{x^3}{3}-\dfrac{1}{4x}\right]_1^2=\left(\dfrac83-\dfrac18\right)-\left(\dfrac13-\dfrac14\right)$.

$=\dfrac83-\dfrac18-\dfrac13+\dfrac14 = \dfrac{7}{3}+\dfrac18 = \dfrac{56}{24}+\dfrac{3}{24}=\dfrac{59}{24}$.

Answer: $\dfrac{59}{24}\approx2.458$.

Practice 2

Find the arc length of $y=\cosh x$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$ (using the identity $\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1$).

Solution

$f'(x)=\sinh x$; $1+[f'(x)]^2=1+\sinh^2x=\cosh^2x$ (using $\cosh^2x-\sinh^2x=1$).

$L=\displaystyle\int_0^1\sqrt{\cosh^2x}\,dx=\int_0^1\cosh x\,dx=\big[\sinh x\big]_0^1=\sinh1-0$.

Answer: $\sinh(1)\approx1.175$.

Practice 3

Find the arc length of $y=\ln(\sin x)$ from $x=\pi/4$ to $x=\pi/2$.

Solution

$f'(x)=\cot x$; $1+[f'(x)]^2=1+\cot^2x=\csc^2x$.

$L=\displaystyle\int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}\sqrt{\csc^2x}\,dx=\int_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}\csc x\,dx = \big[-\ln|\csc x+\cot x|\big]_{\pi/4}^{\pi/2}$.

At $\pi/2$: $\csc=1,\cot=0$: $-\ln1=0$. At $\pi/4$: $\csc=\sqrt2,\cot=1$: $-\ln(\sqrt2+1)$.

Subtract: $0-\big(-\ln(\sqrt2+1)\big)=\ln(\sqrt2+1)$.

Answer: $\ln(\sqrt2+1)\approx0.8814$.

Practice 4

Find the arc length of $y=\dfrac{x^2}{2}-\dfrac{\ln x}{4}$ from $x=1$ to $x=2$ (another perfect-square design, this time mixing a power and a logarithm).

Solution

$f'(x)=x-\dfrac{1}{4x}$; $1+[f'(x)]^2=1+x^2-\dfrac12+\dfrac{1}{16x^2}=x^2+\dfrac12+\dfrac{1}{16x^2}=\left(x+\dfrac{1}{4x}\right)^2$.

$L=\displaystyle\int_1^2\left(x+\dfrac{1}{4x}\right)\,dx=\left[\dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{\ln x}{4}\right]_1^2 = \left(2+\dfrac{\ln2}{4}\right)-\left(\dfrac12+0\right)=\dfrac32+\dfrac{\ln2}{4}$.

Answer: $\dfrac32+\dfrac{\ln2}{4}\approx1.673$.

Practice 5

Find the arc length of $y=\dfrac{x^4}{4}+\dfrac{1}{8x^2}$ from $x=1$ to $x=2$.

Solution

$f'(x)=x^3-\dfrac{1}{4x^3}$; $1+[f'(x)]^2=1+x^6-\dfrac12+\dfrac{1}{16x^6}=x^6+\dfrac12+\dfrac{1}{16x^6}=\left(x^3+\dfrac{1}{4x^3}\right)^2$.

$L=\displaystyle\int_1^2\left(x^3+\dfrac{1}{4x^3}\right)\,dx=\left[\dfrac{x^4}{4}-\dfrac{1}{8x^2}\right]_1^2=\left(4-\dfrac{1}{32}\right)-\left(\dfrac14-\dfrac18\right)$.

$=4-\dfrac{1}{32}-\dfrac18=4-\dfrac{1}{32}-\dfrac{4}{32}=4-\dfrac{5}{32}=\dfrac{123}{32}$.

Answer: $\dfrac{123}{32}=3.84375$.

Practice 6

Set up (do not evaluate) the arc length integral for $y=e^x$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$, and identify what technique would be needed to actually evaluate it.

Solution

$f'(x)=e^x$; $L=\displaystyle\int_0^1\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}\,dx$.

This doesn't match any of the three standard trig-substitution patterns directly (the "$x^2$" role is played by $e^x$, not $x$ itself) — a substitution like $u=e^x$ first (giving $du=e^x\,dx$, so $dx=du/u$) converts it to $\displaystyle\int\dfrac{\sqrt{1+u^2}}{u}\,du$, which then matches the $\sqrt{a^2+u^2}$ trig-substitution pattern with $u=\tan\theta$.

Answer: $\displaystyle\int_0^1\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}\,dx$; requires the substitution $u=e^x$ followed by trig substitution $u=\tan\theta$.

Practice 7

Find the arc length of the line segment $y=3x-2$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$ using the arc length formula, and verify it matches the plain distance formula between the endpoints.

Solution

$f'(x)=3$ (constant). $L=\displaystyle\int_0^4\sqrt{1+9}\,dx=\sqrt{10}\int_0^4\,dx=4\sqrt{10}$.

Check: endpoints are $(0,-2)$ and $(4,10)$. Distance $=\sqrt{(4-0)^2+(10-(-2))^2}=\sqrt{16+144}=\sqrt{160}=4\sqrt{10}$. ✓ Matches.

Answer: $4\sqrt{10}\approx12.65$.

Practice 8

Set up the arc length integral for one full arch of the sine curve, $y=\sin x$ from $x=0$ to $x=\pi$, and explain why this integral cannot be evaluated using any elementary antiderivative (i.e. it's a case demanding numerical methods, connecting back to Weeks 3's Simpson's Rule).

Solution

$f'(x)=\cos x$; $L=\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi}\sqrt{1+\cos^2x}\,dx$.

This is an example of an elliptic integral — a well-known class of integrals proven not to have a closed-form elementary antiderivative. Numerical integration (Simpson's Rule, Week 3) is the practical way to evaluate it, since no combination of algebraic, trigonometric, exponential, or logarithmic functions can express its antiderivative exactly.

Practice 9

Find the arc length of $y=\dfrac{(2x+1)^{3/2}}{3}$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$.

Solution

$f'(x)=(2x+1)^{1/2}$; $1+[f'(x)]^2=1+(2x+1)=2x+2$.

$L=\displaystyle\int_0^4\sqrt{2x+2}\,dx = \left[\dfrac{1}{3}(2x+2)^{3/2}\right]_0^4=\dfrac13\big(10^{3/2}-2^{3/2}\big)$.

$10^{3/2}=31.623$, $2^{3/2}=2.828$: $\dfrac13(31.623-2.828)=\dfrac13(28.795)\approx9.598$.

Answer: $\dfrac{10^{3/2}-2^{3/2}}{3}\approx9.60$.

Practice 10

The upper semicircle $y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}$ has arc length integral $L=\displaystyle\int_{-r}^{r}\dfrac{r}{\sqrt{r^2-x^2}}\,dx$, which is improper (undefined at both endpoints). Evaluate it using the limit techniques from Week 2, and confirm it matches half the circle's circumference.

Solution

Handle both endpoints via limits (or use the symmetric antiderivative directly, since $\arcsin$ is continuous and bounded on the open interval, making this a removable-type improper integral): $$L = r\Big[\arcsin\Big(\frac{x}{r}\Big)\Big]_{-r}^{r} = r\left(\frac{\pi}{2}-\left(-\frac{\pi}{2}\right)\right)=\pi r$$

Answer: $L=\pi r$, exactly half the full circle's circumference $2\pi r$ — confirming the formula against basic geometry.

Practice 11

Find arc length of $y=\ln x$ from $x=1$ to $x=e$. Set up carefully and evaluate with a trig/hyperbolic substitution.

Solution

$f'=1/x$, so $L=\displaystyle\int_1^e\sqrt{1+\dfrac1{x^2}}\,dx=\int_1^e\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x}\,dx$.

Write $\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x}=\sqrt{1+\dfrac1{x^2}}$. Integration by parts / standard form yields $$\int\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x}\,dx=\sqrt{x^2+1}-\ln\left|\dfrac{1+\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x}\right|+C$$ (equivalently $\sqrt{x^2+1}-\operatorname{arsech}x$ up to constants on $(0,1]$, but the log form is fine on $[1,e]$).

Answer: $L=\Big[\sqrt{x^2+1}-\ln\!\big(1+\sqrt{x^2+1}\big)+\ln x\Big]_1^e=\sqrt{e^2+1}-\sqrt2-\ln\!\big(1+\sqrt{e^2+1}\big)+\ln\!\big(1+\sqrt2\big)+1$.

Practice 12

For $y=\dfrac{x^{3/2}}{3}-x^{1/2}$, find $L$ on $[1,4]$ by showing $1+(y')^2$ is a perfect square.

Solution

$y'=\tfrac12 x^{1/2}-\tfrac12 x^{-1/2}$. Then $1+(y')^2=\big(\tfrac12\sqrt x+\tfrac{1}{2\sqrt x}\big)^2$.

$L=\int_1^4\tfrac12\big(\sqrt x+x^{-1/2}\big)\,dx=\big[\tfrac13 x^{3/2}+x^{1/2}\big]_1^4=\tfrac{8}{3}+2-(\tfrac13+1)=\tfrac{10}{3}$.

Practice 13

Parametric arc length: $x=t^3$, $y=t^2$ from $t=0$ to $t=1$. Evaluate.

Solution

$L=\int_0^1\sqrt{(3t^2)^2+(2t)^2}\,dt=\int_0^1 t\sqrt{9t^2+4}\,dt$. Let $u=9t^2+4$, $du=18t\,dt$: $\tfrac{1}{18}\int_4^{13}\sqrt u\,du=\dfrac{1}{27}(13^{3/2}-8)$.

Practice 14

Set up the arc length of $y=\sinh^{-1}x$ on $[0,1]$. Show the derivative identity, then leave the integral in simplified radical form (no elementary closed form required).

Solution

$y=\sinh^{-1}x=\ln\big(x+\sqrt{x^2+1}\big)$, so $y'=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{1+x^2}}$.

Then $1+(y')^2=1+\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}=\dfrac{x^2+2}{1+x^2}$, hence $$L=\int_0^1\sqrt{\dfrac{x^2+2}{x^2+1}}\,dx.$$

This is a standard elliptic-type integral: the exam skill is getting the integrand right, not forcing a closed elementary antiderivative.

Practice 15

Arc length of the catenary $y=a\cosh(x/a)$ from $x=0$ to $x=a$.

Solution

$y'=\sinh(x/a)$; $1+(y')^2=\cosh^2(x/a)$. $L=\int_0^a\cosh(x/a)\,dx=a\sinh1$.

Practice 16

Set up arc length for the ellipse $\dfrac{x^2}{a^2}+\dfrac{y^2}{b^2}=1$ in the first quadrant, and explain why it is elliptic (no elementary antiderivative).

Solution

$y=b\sqrt{1-x^2/a^2}$, $y'=-\dfrac{bx}{a\sqrt{a^2-x^2}}$. Then $L=\int_0^a\sqrt{1+\dfrac{b^2x^2}{a^2(a^2-x^2)}}\,dx$, which rearranges to an elliptic integral of the second kind.

Practice 17

Find $L$ for $x=\dfrac{y^3}{3}+\dfrac{1}{4y}$ from $y=1$ to $y=2$ (arc length formula in $y$).

Solution

$\dfrac{dx}{dy}=y^2-\dfrac{1}{4y^2}$; $1+(x')^2=\big(y^2+\dfrac{1}{4y^2}\big)^2$.

$L=\int_1^2\big(y^2+\dfrac{1}{4y^2}\big)\,dy=\big[\dfrac{y^3}{3}-\dfrac{1}{4y}\big]_1^2=\dfrac{59}{24}$ (same numeric pattern as Practice 1 swapped axes).

Practice 18

Approximate the sine-arch elliptic integral $\int_0^\pi\sqrt{1+\cos^2 x}\,dx$ by comparing bounds: show $L$ satisfies $\pi\sqrt{1}\le L\le\pi\sqrt{2}$.

Solution

$1\le1+\cos^2 x\le2$, so $\pi\le L\le\pi\sqrt2$. This bounds the numerically intractable elliptic integral without computing it.

Practice 19

For $y=\dfrac{2}{3}(x^2+1)^{3/2}$, compute $L$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$.

Solution

$y'=2x\sqrt{x^2+1}$; $1+(y')^2=1+4x^2(x^2+1)=(2x^2+1)^2$.

$L=\int_0^1(2x^2+1)\,dx=\dfrac{5}{3}$.

Practice 20

Capstone: a wire shaped as $y=x^{3/2}$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ costs density $\rho$ per length. Find total mass, and compare to the straight-line distance cost.

Solution

$f'=\tfrac32 x^{1/2}$; $L=\int_0^1\sqrt{1+\tfrac94 x}\,dx=\dfrac{8}{27}\big[(1+\tfrac94)^{3/2}-1\big]=\dfrac{8}{27}\big((\tfrac{13}{4})^{3/2}-1\big)$.

Straight distance $\sqrt2$. Mass $=\rho L>\rho\sqrt2$.

Surface Area Part II — Revolving around the y-axis

Dr. Trefor Bazett · 6:39

The variant that trips people up in exams.

Open on YouTube ↗

Spinning a curve $y=f(x)$ about the $x$-axis sweeps out a surface. A tiny arc of length $ds=\sqrt{1+(y')^2}\,dx$ travels in a circle of radius $|y|$, so its band area is $2\pi |y|\,ds$. Integrating gives the surface area. This is on the MATH142 outline alongside arc length.

Key idea

About the \(x\)-axis, for \(y\ge 0\):

$$S=\int_a^b 2\pi y\sqrt{1+[f'(x)]^2}\,dx$$

About the \(y\)-axis, for \(x\ge 0\):

$$S=\int_c^d 2\pi x\sqrt{1+[g'(y)]^2}\,dy$$

Worked example

Surface from $y=\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,4]$ about the $x$-axis.

$y'=1/(2\sqrt{x})$; $1+(y')^2=1+1/(4x)=(4x+1)/(4x)$. $S=\int_0^4 2\pi\sqrt{x}\cdot\sqrt{(4x+1)/(4x)}\,dx=\pi\int_0^4\sqrt{4x+1}\,dx=\tfrac{\pi}{6}\big[(4x+1)^{3/2}\big]_0^4=\tfrac{\pi}{6}(17^{3/2}-1)$.

Practice 21

Revolve $y=x^3$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$ about the $x$-axis. Set up and evaluate $S$.

Solution

$y'=3x^2$; $S=\int_0^1 2\pi x^3\sqrt{1+9x^4}\,dx$. Let $u=1+9x^4$, $du=36x^3\,dx$: $S=\dfrac{\pi}{18}\int_1^{10}\sqrt{u}\,du=\dfrac{\pi}{27}(10^{3/2}-1)$.

Practice 22

Revolve $y=\sqrt{4-x^2}$ from $x=0$ to $x=2$ about the $x$-axis. Identify the surface geometrically and find $S$.

Solution

Upper semicircle radius 2; full revolution of the quarter in the first quadrant gives a hemisphere of radius 2. $S=2\pi r^2=8\pi$. (Or compute the integral; it matches.)

Practice 23

Find the surface area generated by revolving $y=\cosh x$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$ about the $x$-axis.

Solution

$y'=\sinh x$; $1+(y')^2=\cosh^2 x$. $S=\int_0^1 2\pi\cosh x\cdot\cosh x\,dx=2\pi\int_0^1\cosh^2 x\,dx=\pi\int_0^1(1+\cosh2x)\,dx=\pi\big(1+\tfrac12\sinh2\big)$.

Practice 24

Set up (do not evaluate) the surface area of $y=\ln x$, $1\le x\le e$, about the $y$-axis.

Solution

About $y$-axis: use $x=e^y$ for $0\le y\le1$, $S=\int_0^1 2\pi e^y\sqrt{1+e^{2y}}\,dy$.

Practice 25

Revolve $x=\dfrac{1}{3}y^3+\dfrac{1}{4y}$ from $y=1$ to $y=2$ about the $y$-axis. Evaluate $S$ using a perfect-square shortcut.

Solution

$dx/dy=y^2-1/(4y^2)$, so $1+(dx/dy)^2=(y^2+1/(4y^2))^2$.

$$S=2\pi\int_1^2\left(\frac{y^3}{3}+\frac{1}{4y}\right) \left(y^2+\frac{1}{4y^2}\right)\,dy =2\pi\int_1^2\left(\frac{y^5}{3}+\frac{y}{3}+\frac{1}{16y^3}\right)\,dy.$$

Answer: $S=\dfrac{515\pi}{64}$.

Practice 26

Compare: for $y=x$ on $[0,1]$, find (a) arc length and (b) surface area about the $x$-axis. Why is $S$ not equal to $2\pi$ times the midpoint radius times $L$?

Solution

(a) $L=\sqrt2$. (b) $S=\int_0^1 2\pi x\sqrt2\,dx=\pi\sqrt2$. The frustum formula uses average radius; for a cone, exact $S=\pi r\ell=\pi\cdot1\cdot\sqrt2=\pi\sqrt2$, matching. The naive midpoint $2\pi(1/2)L=\pi\sqrt2$ happens to work for a cone; it fails for curved profiles because radius varies nonlinearly along the arc.

Practice 27

Revolve $y=\sqrt{x}$ on $[1,4]$ about the $y$-axis using shells-style setup in $x$: write $S=\int 2\pi x\,ds$ with $ds$ in $x$.

Solution

$ds=\sqrt{1+1/(4x)}\,dx$, so $S=\int_1^4 2\pi x\sqrt{1+1/(4x)}\,dx=\int_1^4 2\pi x\sqrt{(4x+1)/(4x)}\,dx=\pi\int_1^4\sqrt{x(4x+1)}\,dx$.

Practice 28

Outline reason the sphere area $4\pi r^2$ equals revolving $y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}$ on $[-r,r]$ about the $x$-axis.

Solution

$y'=-x/y$; $1+(y')^2=r^2/y^2$; $S=\int_{-r}^{r}2\pi y\cdot(r/y)\,dx=\int_{-r}^{r}2\pi r\,dx=4\pi r^2$.

Practice 29

Revolve $y=x^2$ from $(0,0)$ to $(1,1)$ about the $y$-axis. Set up $S$ both in $x$ (radius $=x$) and in $y$ (using $x=\sqrt{y}$). Evaluate the cleaner form and confirm they agree.

Solution

In $x$: $y'=2x$, so $$S=\int_0^1 2\pi x\sqrt{1+4x^2}\,dx.$$ Let $u=1+4x^2$, $du=8x\,dx$: $S=\dfrac{\pi}{4}\int_1^5\sqrt{u}\,du=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big(5^{3/2}-1\big)$.

In $y$: $x=\sqrt{y}$, $\dfrac{dx}{dy}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{y}}$. Then $$1+\Big(\dfrac{dx}{dy}\Big)^2=\dfrac{4y+1}{4y},\qquad S=\int_0^1 2\pi\sqrt{y}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{4y+1}}{2\sqrt{y}}\,dy=\pi\int_0^1\sqrt{4y+1}\,dy=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big(5^{3/2}-1\big).$$ Same value. (Near $y=0$ the $y$-form looks improper, but $\sqrt{y}\cdot\sqrt{1/(4y)}$ cancels to a constant, so the integral converges.)

Answer: $S=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big(5^{3/2}-1\big)$.

Practice 30

Revolve the line $y=\dfrac{3}{4}x$ from $x=0$ to $x=4$ about the $x$-axis. Identify the solid geometrically, predict $S$ from the cone formula, then confirm by integrating.

Solution

A right circular cone of base radius $R=3$ and height $H=4$. Slant length $\ell=\sqrt{3^2+4^2}=5$, so lateral area $S=\pi R\ell=15\pi$.

Integral check: $y'=\tfrac{3}{4}$, $\sqrt{1+(y')^2}=\tfrac{5}{4}$, $$S=\int_0^4 2\pi\cdot\dfrac{3}{4}x\cdot\dfrac{5}{4}\,dx=\dfrac{15\pi}{8}\int_0^4 x\,dx=\dfrac{15\pi}{8}\cdot 8=15\pi.$$

Answer: $S=15\pi$ (cone lateral surface).

Practice 31

Revolve $y=x+1$ from $x=1$ to $x=3$ about the $x$-axis. Recognise a frustum of a cone, compute $S$ from $\pi(r_1+r_2)\ell$, and verify the surface-of-revolution integral.

Solution

End radii $r_1=y(1)=2$, $r_2=y(3)=4$. Rise over run is $2$ over $2$, so slant $\ell=\sqrt{2^2+2^2}=2\sqrt{2}$. Frustum formula: $$S=\pi(r_1+r_2)\ell=\pi(6)(2\sqrt{2})=12\pi\sqrt{2}.$$

Integral: $y'=1$, $$S=\int_1^3 2\pi(x+1)\sqrt{2}\,dx=2\pi\sqrt{2}\Big[\tfrac{x^2}{2}+x\Big]_1^3=2\pi\sqrt{2}\big(7.5-1.5\big)=12\pi\sqrt{2}.$$

Answer: $S=12\pi\sqrt{2}$.

Practice 32

Parametric curve $x=t^2$, $y=2t$ for $0\le t\le 1$ is revolved about the $x$-axis. Set up $S=\displaystyle\int 2\pi y\,\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}\,dt$ and evaluate.

Solution

$x'=2t$, $y'=2$, so $\sqrt{(x')^2+(y')^2}=2\sqrt{t^2+1}$. Then $$S=\int_0^1 2\pi(2t)\cdot 2\sqrt{t^2+1}\,dt=8\pi\int_0^1 t\sqrt{t^2+1}\,dt.$$ Let $u=t^2+1$, $du=2t\,dt$: $S=4\pi\int_1^2\sqrt{u}\,du=\dfrac{8\pi}{3}\big(2^{3/2}-1\big)=\dfrac{8\pi}{3}\big(2\sqrt{2}-1\big)$.

Answer: $S=\dfrac{8\pi}{3}\big(2\sqrt{2}-1\big)$.

Practice 33

Polar cardioid $r=a(1+\cos\theta)$, $0\le\theta\le\pi$, is revolved about the polar axis. Set up the surface integral in $\theta$ and evaluate. (Use $y=r\sin\theta$ and $ds=\sqrt{r^2+(dr/d\theta)^2}\,d\theta$.)

Solution

$dr/d\theta=-a\sin\theta$, so $$r^2+(r')^2=a^2(1+\cos\theta)^2+a^2\sin^2\theta=2a^2(1+\cos\theta),\qquad \sqrt{r^2+(r')^2}=a\sqrt{2}\,\sqrt{1+\cos\theta}.$$ Then $$S=\int_0^\pi 2\pi\,a(1+\cos\theta)\sin\theta\cdot a\sqrt{2}\,\sqrt{1+\cos\theta}\,d\theta =2\pi a^2\sqrt{2}\int_0^\pi(1+\cos\theta)^{3/2}\sin\theta\,d\theta.$$ Let $u=1+\cos\theta$, $du=-\sin\theta\,d\theta$ ($\theta=0\to u=2$, $\theta=\pi\to u=0$): $$S=2\pi a^2\sqrt{2}\int_0^2 u^{3/2}\,du=2\pi a^2\sqrt{2}\cdot\dfrac{2}{5}\cdot 2^{5/2} =\dfrac{4\pi a^2\sqrt{2}}{5}\cdot 4\sqrt{2}=\dfrac{32\pi a^2}{5}.$$

Answer: $S=\dfrac{32\pi a^2}{5}$.

Practice 34

Revolve $y=\sqrt{x}$ on $[0,1]$ about the $x$-axis. Note that $y'$ is improper at $x=0$. Show the surface integral still converges and evaluate $S$.

Solution

$y'=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}$ blows up at $0$, but $$1+(y')^2=\dfrac{4x+1}{4x},\qquad y\sqrt{1+(y')^2}=\sqrt{x}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt{x}}=\dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{4x+1},$$ which is continuous on $[0,1]$. Hence $$S=\int_0^1 2\pi\cdot\dfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{4x+1}\,dx=\pi\int_0^1\sqrt{4x+1}\,dx=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big[(4x+1)^{3/2}\big]_0^1=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big(5^{3/2}-1\big).$$

Answer: $S=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big(5^{3/2}-1\big)$ (improper but convergent).

Practice 35

Engineering coating: a parabolic bowl is generated by revolving $y=\dfrac{x^2}{4}$, $0\le x\le 2$, about the $y$-axis. Find the interior surface area that must be coated (ignore the rim).

Solution

Prefer $x=2\sqrt{y}$ for $0\le y\le 1$: $\dfrac{dx}{dy}=\dfrac{1}{\sqrt{y}}$, so $$\sqrt{1+(dx/dy)^2}=\dfrac{\sqrt{y+1}}{\sqrt{y}}.$$ Radius of revolution is $x=2\sqrt{y}$, hence $$S=\int_0^1 2\pi(2\sqrt{y})\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{y+1}}{\sqrt{y}}\,dy=4\pi\int_0^1\sqrt{y+1}\,dy =\dfrac{8\pi}{3}\big[(y+1)^{3/2}\big]_0^1=\dfrac{8\pi}{3}\big(2\sqrt{2}-1\big).$$ (Again $dx/dy$ is improper at $y=0$, but the product with the radius cancels the singularity.)

Answer: coating area $S=\dfrac{8\pi}{3}\big(2\sqrt{2}-1\big)$.

Practice 36

Inverse design: a cone of fixed height $H=4$ is formed by revolving $y=\dfrac{R}{4}x$ on $[0,4]$ about the $x$-axis. What base radius $R>0$ makes the lateral surface area exactly $20\pi$?

Solution

Slant $\ell=\sqrt{R^2+16}$, so $S=\pi R\ell=\pi R\sqrt{R^2+16}$. Require $R\sqrt{R^2+16}=20$: $$R^2(R^2+16)=400\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad u^2+16u-400=0\quad(u=R^2).$$ $$u=-8+\sqrt{64+400}=-8+\sqrt{464}=-8+2\sqrt{116}=-8+4\sqrt{29}=4\big(\sqrt{29}-2\big).$$ Hence $R=2\sqrt{\sqrt{29}-2}$ (positive root). Check: $R\sqrt{R^2+16}=20$ by construction.

Answer: $R=2\sqrt{\sqrt{29}-2}$.

Practice 37

Without evaluating the integral, bound the surface area of $y=e^{-x}$ on $[0,1]$ revolved about the $x$-axis. Produce sharp, elementary bounds for $S$.

Solution

$y'=-e^{-x}$, so $S=\displaystyle\int_0^1 2\pi e^{-x}\sqrt{1+e^{-2x}}\,dx$. On $[0,1]$, $$\sqrt{1+e^{-2}}\le\sqrt{1+e^{-2x}}\le\sqrt{2},$$ and both constants are attained at endpoints. Hence $$2\pi\sqrt{1+e^{-2}}\int_0^1 e^{-x}\,dx \le S\le 2\pi\sqrt{2}\int_0^1 e^{-x}\,dx.$$ Evaluating $\int_0^1 e^{-x}\,dx=1-e^{-1}$ gives $$2\pi\sqrt{1+e^{-2}}\big(1-e^{-1}\big) \le S\le 2\pi\sqrt{2}\big(1-e^{-1}\big).$$

Answer: These are the sharp bounds obtained from the minimum and maximum of the radical factor on $[0,1]$.

Practice 38

Numerical setup: revolve $y=\ln x$ from $x=1$ to $x=e$ about the $x$-axis. Write a simplified integral for $S$ and explain why a calculator / numerical quadrature is the appropriate next step (no elementary closed form required).

Solution

$y'=1/x$, so $$S=\int_1^e 2\pi\ln x\sqrt{1+\dfrac{1}{x^2}}\,dx=2\pi\int_1^e\ln x\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x}\,dx.$$ The factor $(\ln x)\sqrt{x^2+1}/x$ has no elementary antiderivative (a mix of logarithmic and hyperbolic-type radicals). Exam skill: reduce to this clean form, then use Simpson / Gauss quadrature if a decimal is needed. Rough check: integrand is between $0$ and about $(\sqrt{e^2+1}/e)$ on the interval, so $S$ is on the order of a few units of $\pi$.

Answer: $S=2\pi\displaystyle\int_1^e\dfrac{\ln x\,\sqrt{x^2+1}}{x}\,dx$ (evaluate numerically).

Practice 39

Revolve $y=\dfrac{1}{2}x^2$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$ about the $x$-axis. Evaluate $S$ using the standard antiderivative for $\int x^2\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx$ (trig or hyperbolic substitution from earlier techniques).

Solution

$y'=x$, so $S=\displaystyle\int_0^1 2\pi\cdot\dfrac{x^2}{2}\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx=\pi\int_0^1 x^2\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx$.

Standard formula (e.g.\ $x=\sinh t$ or $x=\tan\theta$ then parts): $$\int x^2\sqrt{1+x^2}\,dx=\dfrac{x}{8}(2x^2+1)\sqrt{1+x^2}-\dfrac{1}{8}\ln\big|x+\sqrt{1+x^2}\big|+C.$$ Hence $$S=\pi\Big[\dfrac{3\sqrt{2}}{8}-\dfrac{1}{8}\ln\big(1+\sqrt{2}\big)\Big]=\dfrac{\pi}{8}\Big(3\sqrt{2}-\ln\big(1+\sqrt{2}\big)\Big).$$

Answer: $S=\dfrac{\pi}{8}\big(3\sqrt{2}-\ln(1+\sqrt{2})\big)$.

Practice 40

Revolve $y=e^x$ from $x=0$ to $x=1$ about the $x$-axis. Use a substitution to reach $\int\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du$ and evaluate in closed form.

Solution

$y'=e^x$, so $S=\displaystyle\int_0^1 2\pi e^x\sqrt{1+e^{2x}}\,dx$. Let $u=e^x$, $du=e^x\,dx$: $$S=2\pi\int_1^e\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du.$$ Using $\int\sqrt{1+u^2}\,du=\dfrac{1}{2}\big(u\sqrt{1+u^2}+\ln\big(u+\sqrt{1+u^2}\big)\big)+C$, $$S=\pi\Big[e\sqrt{1+e^2}+\ln\big(e+\sqrt{1+e^2}\big)-\sqrt{2}-\ln\big(1+\sqrt{2}\big)\Big].$$

Answer: $S=\pi\big(e\sqrt{1+e^2}+\ln(e+\sqrt{1+e^2})-\sqrt{2}-\ln(1+\sqrt{2})\big)$.

Practice 41

Revolve $x=\sqrt{y+1}$ from $y=0$ to $y=3$ about the $y$-axis. Choose the $y$-form deliberately and evaluate $S$.

Solution

$\dfrac{dx}{dy}=\dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{y+1}}$, so $$1+(dx/dy)^2=\dfrac{4y+5}{4(y+1)},\qquad \sqrt{1+(dx/dy)^2}=\dfrac{\sqrt{4y+5}}{2\sqrt{y+1}}.$$ $$S=\int_0^3 2\pi\sqrt{y+1}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{4y+5}}{2\sqrt{y+1}}\,dy=\pi\int_0^3\sqrt{4y+5}\,dy =\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big[(4y+5)^{3/2}\big]_0^3=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big(17^{3/2}-5^{3/2}\big).$$ (Writing $y=x^2-1$ and integrating in $x$ about the $y$-axis is messier: this is why the axis / variable choice matters.)

Answer: $S=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big(17^{3/2}-5^{3/2}\big)$.

Practice 42

Geometric zone theorem: revolve $y=\sqrt{r^2-x^2}$ from $x=a$ to $x=b$ (with $-r\le a<b\le r$) about the $x$-axis. Show $S=2\pi r(b-a)$, independent of where the band sits on the sphere.

Solution

$y'=-x/y$, so $1+(y')^2=r^2/y^2$ and $\sqrt{1+(y')^2}=r/y$. Therefore $$S=\int_a^b 2\pi y\cdot\dfrac{r}{y}\,dx=\int_a^b 2\pi r\,dx=2\pi r(b-a).$$ The height of the zone is $h=b-a$, so $S=2\pi rh$ — Archimedes' surprising result that only height matters, not the latitude of the band.

Answer: $S=2\pi r(b-a)=2\pi rh$.

Practice 43

Material cost: a reflector panel is the surface from revolving $y=\dfrac{x^3}{6}+\dfrac{1}{2x}$ on $[1,2]$ about the $x$-axis. Use the perfect-square identity for $1+(y')^2$ (earlier arc-length technique) to find $S$. If material costs $c$ per unit area, state the total cost.

Solution

$y'=\dfrac{x^2}{2}-\dfrac{1}{2x^2}$, so $1+(y')^2=\Big(\dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{1}{2x^2}\Big)^2$ and $\sqrt{1+(y')^2}=\dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{1}{2x^2}$ (positive on $[1,2]$).

$$S=2\pi\int_1^2\Big(\dfrac{x^3}{6}+\dfrac{1}{2x}\Big)\Big(\dfrac{x^2}{2}+\dfrac{1}{2x^2}\Big)\,dx =2\pi\int_1^2\Big(\dfrac{x^5}{12}+\dfrac{x}{12}+\dfrac{x}{4}+\dfrac{1}{4x^3}\Big)\,dx =2\pi\int_1^2\Big(\dfrac{x^5}{12}+\dfrac{x}{3}+\dfrac{1}{4x^3}\Big)\,dx.$$ Antiderivative: $\dfrac{x^6}{72}+\dfrac{x^2}{6}-\dfrac{1}{8x^2}$. Evaluating: $$\Big[\dfrac{x^6}{72}+\dfrac{x^2}{6}-\dfrac{1}{8x^2}\Big]_1^2=\dfrac{47}{32},\qquad S=2\pi\cdot\dfrac{47}{32}=\dfrac{47\pi}{16}.$$ Cost $=cS=\dfrac{47\pi c}{16}$.

Answer: $S=\dfrac{47\pi}{16}$; cost $=\dfrac{47\pi c}{16}$.

Practice 44

Capstone: for $y=\sqrt{x}$ on $[1,4]$, compare surface areas about the $x$-axis versus about the $y$-axis (set up the $y$-axis integral in $x$ as $S=\int 2\pi x\,ds$). Evaluate the $x$-axis case; leave the $y$-axis case as a definite integral ready for numerical work if needed, and state which should be larger and why.

Solution

$ds=\sqrt{1+\dfrac{1}{4x}}\,dx=\dfrac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt{x}}\,dx$.

About $x$-axis (radius $=\sqrt{x}$): $$S_x=\int_1^4 2\pi\sqrt{x}\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt{x}}\,dx=\pi\int_1^4\sqrt{4x+1}\,dx =\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big[(4x+1)^{3/2}\big]_1^4=\dfrac{\pi}{6}\big(17^{3/2}-5^{3/2}\big).$$

About $y$-axis (radius $=x$): $$S_y=\int_1^4 2\pi x\cdot\dfrac{\sqrt{4x+1}}{2\sqrt{x}}\,dx=\pi\int_1^4\sqrt{x(4x+1)}\,dx=\pi\int_1^4\sqrt{4x^2+x}\,dx.$$ Complete the square / trig sub yields an elementary but bulky closed form; leaving the integral is fine. Since on $[1,4]$ the horizontal distance $x$ from the $y$-axis exceeds the vertical distance $\sqrt{x}$ from the $x$-axis at each $x$, and $ds$ is the same, expect $S_y>S_x$. Indeed $\sqrt{x(4x+1)}>\sqrt{4x+1}$ for $x>1$, so the integrands confirm $S_y>S_x$.

Answer: $S_x=\dfrac{\pi}{6}(17^{3/2}-5^{3/2})$; $S_y=\pi\displaystyle\int_1^4\sqrt{4x^2+x}\,dx>S_x$.
