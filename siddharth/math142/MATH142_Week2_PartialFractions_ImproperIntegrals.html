Start here The big picture — what this week is about

Week 1’s partial fractions only handled distinct linear factors. Real denominators are messier: they contain repeated factors like $(x-2)^3$ and irreducible quadratics like $x^2+1$ that never factor over the reals. The first half of this week completes the partial-fraction toolkit so you can decompose any proper rational function. The second half confronts a different frontier entirely: integrals whose interval runs to infinity, or whose integrand blows up inside the interval.

An improper integral asks a subtle question: can you add up infinitely much and still get a finite answer? Sometimes yes — $\int_1^\infty x^{-2}\,dx = 1$ — and sometimes no — $\int_1^\infty x^{-1}\,dx=\infty$. The trick is to replace the troublesome endpoint with a limit, integrate normally, and check whether the limit is finite (converges) or not (diverges). This idea of “a finite total from an infinite process” is exactly what powers infinite series later in the course.

Why this matters

Improper integrals are unavoidable in science and engineering. A probability density must integrate to $1$ over all outcomes — an integral to infinity. The Laplace transform, the workhorse for solving circuits and control systems, is an improper integral $\int_0^\infty e^{-st}f(t)\,dt$. Deciding whether the total energy of a signal or the escape work against gravity is finite is a convergence question. Partial fractions, meanwhile, are the standard pre-processing step before inverting those transforms.

The roadmap — how the pieces connect

First we extend partial fractions to repeated linear factors and irreducible quadratics, so every proper rational function can be decomposed. Then we define improper integrals via limits, handle both infinite intervals and interior singularities, and use comparison tests to decide convergence without always evaluating. The convergence mindset built here is the on-ramp to Weeks 10–11.

Lay of the land — this week's toolkit
  • Repeated linear factor $(x-a)^n$: include a term for every power from 1 to $n$: $\dfrac{A_1}{x-a}+\dfrac{A_2}{(x-a)^2}+\cdots+\dfrac{A_n}{(x-a)^n}$.
  • Irreducible quadratic factor (negative discriminant): use a linear numerator, not a constant: $\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}$.
  • Infinite limits: $\displaystyle\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx$; converges iff the limit is finite.
  • Discontinuous integrand at $c\in[a,b]$: split at $c$, wrap each piece in a one-sided limit; both pieces must converge for the whole integral to converge.
  • $p$-integral rule: $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \dfrac{1}{x^p}\,dx$ converges iff $p>1$ — a result you'll reuse directly for series convergence in Week 11.

Partial Fractions — Repeated and Irreducible Quadratic Terms

Dr. Trefor Bazett · 10:10

Exactly this section: what to write down when a factor repeats or won't factor.

Open on YouTube ↗

Distinct linear factors were the simplest case. When a factor repeats, or the denominator contains a quadratic that can't be factored over the reals, the decomposition needs extra terms — built by the same coefficient-matching idea, just with a richer set of unknowns.

Repeated linear factors. For $(x-a)^n$, a single term $\dfrac{A}{x-a}$ can only reproduce a degree-0 numerator once you clear $(x-a)^n$ — matching a general proper numerator needs the extra degrees of freedom that $\dfrac{A_2}{(x-a)^2},\dots,\dfrac{A_n}{(x-a)^n}$ provide. The cover-up trick only pins down the last coefficient $A_n$ directly (substituting $x=a$ after clearing denominators); the rest require expanding and matching coefficients.

Irreducible quadratic factors. A quadratic $x^2+bx+c$ with negative discriminant doesn't factor over the reals, so it gets a linear numerator $\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}$ — a constant numerator alone can't supply the $x$-term needed when you multiply through and match coefficients.

Integrating the quadratic term. $\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}$ typically splits into two pieces once integrated: one matching $\int\dfrac{2x+b}{x^2+bx+c}\,dx$ (a logarithm, via substitution), the other needing the arctan form (often after completing the square if $b\ne0$).

Key idea

$$(x-a)^n \to \sum_{k=1}^n \frac{A_k}{(x-a)^k}, \qquad \text{irreducible } x^2+bx+c \to \frac{Bx+C}{x^2+bx+c}$$

Worked example

Example 1 — repeated linear factor. Decompose $\dfrac{3x-1}{(x-1)^2}$.

$3x-1=A(x-1)+B$. At $x=1$: $B=2$. Match $x$-coefficients: $A=3$. Result: $\dfrac{3}{x-1}+\dfrac{2}{(x-1)^2}$.

Example 2 — irreducible quadratic factor. Decompose $\dfrac{x+1}{x(x^2+1)}$.

$x+1=A(x^2+1)+(Bx+C)x$. At $x=0$: $A=1$. Match $x^2$: $B=-1$. Match $x$: $C=1$. Result: $\dfrac{1}{x}+\dfrac{-x+1}{x^2+1}$.

Practice 1

Decompose $\dfrac{x^2}{(x-1)^3}$ (three repeated-factor terms needed), then find $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^2}{(x-1)^3}\,dx$.

Solution

Set $x^2=A(x-1)^2+B(x-1)+C$. At $x=1$: $C=1$. Expanding and matching the $x^2$ coefficient: $A=1$. Matching the $x$ coefficient (or comparing constants) gives $B=2$.

Decomposition: $\dfrac{1}{x-1}+\dfrac{2}{(x-1)^2}+\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^3}$.

Integrate: $\ln|x-1|-\dfrac{2}{x-1}-\dfrac{1}{2(x-1)^2}+C$.

Answer: $\ln|x-1|-\dfrac{2}{x-1}-\dfrac{1}{2(x-1)^2}+C$.

Practice 2

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{3x^2+4}{x(x^2+4)}\,dx$ (mixing a distinct linear factor with an irreducible quadratic), including the arctan term fully evaluated.

Solution

$3x^2+4=A(x^2+4)+(Bx+C)x$. At $x=0$: $4=4A\implies A=1$. Match $x^2$: $3=A+B\implies B=2$. Match $x$: $0=C$.

Decomposition: $\dfrac{1}{x}+\dfrac{2x}{x^2+4}$.

Integrate: $\int\dfrac{1}{x}\,dx+\int\dfrac{2x}{x^2+4}\,dx = \ln|x|+\ln(x^2+4)+C$ (the second term is a clean logarithm since the numerator $2x$ is exactly the derivative of $x^2+4$ — no arctan needed here since $C=0$).

Answer: $\ln|x|+\ln(x^2+4)+C = \ln\big|x(x^2+4)\big|+C$.

Practice 3

Decompose $\dfrac{2x^2-x+4}{(x-2)(x^2+4)}$, then find $\displaystyle\int \frac{2x^2-x+4}{(x-2)(x^2+4)}\,dx$ (this one genuinely needs the arctan form).

Solution

$2x^2-x+4=A(x^2+4)+(Bx+C)(x-2)$. At $x=2$: $8-2+4=10=8A\implies A=\tfrac54$.

Match $x^2$: $2=A+B\implies B=\tfrac34$. Match constant: $4=4A-2C\implies 4=5-2C\implies C=\tfrac12$.

Decomposition: $\dfrac{5/4}{x-2}+\dfrac{\tfrac34x+\tfrac12}{x^2+4}$.

Split the quadratic term: $\dfrac{\tfrac34x}{x^2+4}$ integrates to $\tfrac38\ln(x^2+4)$ (numerator matches $\tfrac34\cdot\tfrac12(2x)$); $\dfrac{\tfrac12}{x^2+4}$ integrates to $\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac12\arctan(x/2)=\tfrac14\arctan(x/2)$.

Answer: $\dfrac54\ln|x-2|+\dfrac38\ln(x^2+4)+\dfrac14\arctan\!\left(\dfrac{x}{2}\right)+C$.

Practice 4

Explain why the repeated-factor term $(x-a)^n$ requires $n$ separate partial-fraction terms rather than just one, using a degree-counting argument, and confirm with $\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}$ that a single term IS enough there.

Solution

A single term $\dfrac{A}{(x-a)^k}$ (for one fixed $k$) can only reproduce, after clearing the common denominator $(x-a)^n$, a numerator that is a specific power of $(x-a)$ — it can't match an arbitrary proper numerator of the combined fraction unless every power from 1 to $n$ is represented.

For $\dfrac{1}{(x-1)^2}$: the numerator is already just the constant $1$ over $(x-1)^2$ exactly — it needs no decomposition at all, since it's already in the simplest single-term form for a repeated factor of order 2 with a matching (degree-0) numerator. This is the trivial case where $n=1$ term already suffices, precisely because the given numerator has no lower-order structure to capture.

Comparison Test for Improper Integrals

Dr. Trefor Bazett · 7:55

How to decide convergence without evaluating the integral.

Open on YouTube ↗

Ordinary definite integrals assume a finite interval and a well-behaved (bounded, continuous) integrand. When either assumption fails — an infinite limit of integration, or a vertical asymptote inside the interval — the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus can't be applied directly. Improper integrals patch this by wrapping the troublesome point in a limit.

Infinite limits of integration. Replace the infinite bound with a finite variable, integrate normally, then take the limit as that variable heads to infinity. If the limit exists (finite), the integral converges; otherwise it diverges.

Discontinuous integrands. If $f$ has an infinite discontinuity at some point $c$ within $[a,b]$, split the integral at $c$ and wrap each piece in a one-sided limit. Both pieces must converge individually for the whole integral to converge.

Key idea

$$\int_a^\infty f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to\infty}\int_a^t f(x)\,dx, \qquad \int_a^b f(x)\,dx = \lim_{t\to c^-}\int_a^t f\,dx + \lim_{t\to c^+}\int_t^b f\,dx$$

Worked example

Example 1 — infinite limit, converges. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \dfrac{1}{x^2}\,dx$.

$\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[-\dfrac1x\right]_1^t = \lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-\dfrac1t+1\right)=1$. Converges to $1$.

Example 2 — discontinuous integrand. Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \dfrac{1}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx$.

$\lim_{t\to0^+}\left[2\sqrt{x}\right]_t^1 = \lim_{t\to0^+}(2-2\sqrt t)=2$. Converges to $2$.

Practice 5

Determine whether $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{1}{x^p}\,dx$ converges for general $p$, proving both the $p>1$ and $p\le1$ cases explicitly.

Solution

Case $p\ne1$: $\displaystyle\int_1^t x^{-p}\,dx = \left[\frac{x^{1-p}}{1-p}\right]_1^t = \frac{t^{1-p}-1}{1-p}$.

If $p>1$: exponent $1-p<0$, so $t^{1-p}\to0$ as $t\to\infty$, giving $\dfrac{-1}{1-p}=\dfrac{1}{p-1}$ — finite, converges.

If $p<1$: exponent $1-p>0$, so $t^{1-p}\to\infty$ — diverges.

Case $p=1$: $\displaystyle\int_1^t x^{-1}\,dx=\ln t\to\infty$ — diverges.

Answer: converges iff $p>1$, with value $\dfrac{1}{p-1}$; diverges for $p\le1$.

Practice 6

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx$ carefully — show that treating it as a single symmetric limit gives a misleading answer, and find the correct (properly split) result.

Solution

The trap: if you (incorrectly) took a single symmetric limit $\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{-R}^R \dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx$, the odd-function symmetry makes every finite piece exactly $0$, tempting you to conclude the integral "is" $0$.

The correct approach: the definition requires the two infinite limits to be evaluated independently: $\int_{-\infty}^0+\int_0^\infty$. Consider just $\int_0^\infty\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx=\lim_{t\to\infty}\left[\tfrac12\ln(1+x^2)\right]_0^t=\lim_{t\to\infty}\tfrac12\ln(1+t^2)=\infty$.

Since this one-sided piece already diverges, the whole improper integral diverges — the symmetric-limit shortcut (giving $0$) is not valid here and would be marked incorrect in an exam.

Answer: diverges (the naive symmetric answer of $0$ is a common but incorrect shortcut).

Practice 7

Determine whether $\displaystyle\int_0^2 \frac{1}{(x-1)^2}\,dx$ converges, being careful about the discontinuity's location inside the interval.

Solution

The integrand is discontinuous at $x=1$, which lies strictly inside $[0,2]$ — split there: $\int_0^1+\int_1^2$.

$\displaystyle\int_0^1\frac{dx}{(x-1)^2} = \lim_{t\to1^-}\left[-\frac{1}{x-1}\right]_0^t = \lim_{t\to1^-}\left(-\frac{1}{t-1}-1\right)$. As $t\to1^-$, $t-1\to0^-$, so $-\dfrac{1}{t-1}\to+\infty$ — this piece diverges.

Since one piece already diverges, the whole integral diverges — regardless of what the second piece does.

Answer: diverges.

Practice 8

Determine whether $\displaystyle\int_2^\infty \frac{1}{x(\ln x)^2}\,dx$ converges, using the substitution $u=\ln x$.

Solution

Let $u=\ln x$, $du=dx/x$. When $x=2,u=\ln2$; as $x\to\infty,u\to\infty$.

$\displaystyle\int_{\ln2}^\infty \frac{1}{u^2}\,du = \lim_{t\to\infty}\left[-\frac1u\right]_{\ln2}^t = \lim_{t\to\infty}\left(-\frac1t+\frac{1}{\ln2}\right)=\frac{1}{\ln2}$.

Answer: converges, to $\dfrac{1}{\ln2}\approx1.4427$.

Practice 9

Decompose $\dfrac{5x+7}{(x+1)^2(x-2)}$ and evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{5x+7}{(x+1)^2(x-2)}\,dx$.

Solution

Use $\dfrac{A}{x-2}+\dfrac{B}{x+1}+\dfrac{C}{(x+1)^2}$. Solving gives $A=\frac{17}{9},\,B=-\frac{17}{9},\,C=-\frac{4}{3}$.

So $\int = \frac{17}{9}\ln|x-2|-\frac{17}{9}\ln|x+1|+\frac{4}{3(x+1)}+C$.

Practice 10

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int \frac{x^2+3x+5}{(x+1)(x^2+4x+13)}\,dx$ (quadratic completion required).

Solution

Set $\dfrac{A}{x+1}+\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+4x+13}$ and solve: $A=\frac{3}{10},\,B=\frac{7}{10},\,C=\frac{9}{5}$.

Write $x^2+4x+13=(x+2)^2+9$ and split $\dfrac{7x+18}{10(x^2+4x+13)}=\dfrac{7}{20}\dfrac{2x+4}{x^2+4x+13}+\dfrac{2}{5}\dfrac{1}{(x+2)^2+9}$.

Answer: $$\frac{3}{10}\ln|x+1|+\frac{7}{20}\ln(x^2+4x+13)+\frac{2}{15}\arctan\!\left(\frac{x+2}{3}\right)+C.$$

Practice 11

Find all real $k$ such that $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^k}$ converges to a value less than $2$.

Solution

Convergence requires $k>1$, and then value is $\dfrac{1}{k-1}$.

Need $\dfrac{1}{k-1}<2 \Rightarrow 1<2(k-1)\Rightarrow k>\frac{3}{2}$.

Answer: $k>\frac{3}{2}$.

Practice 12

Determine convergence of $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{\ln x}{\sqrt{x}}\,dx$ and evaluate it if convergent.

Solution

Let $x=u^2$ so $dx=2u\,du$, $\sqrt{x}=u$, $\ln x=2\ln u$.

Integral becomes $4\int_0^1 \ln u\,du = 4[u\ln u-u]_0^1 =4(0-1)=-4$.

Answer: converges, value $-4$.

Practice 13

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{(x+1)^2}\,dx$ and classify convergence.

Solution

$\int_0^t (x+1)^{-2}\,dx = \left[-\frac{1}{x+1}\right]_0^t = 1-\frac{1}{t+1}$.

$t\to\infty$ gives $1$.

Answer: convergent, value $1$.

Practice 14

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{x}{x^2+9}\,dx$ and explain briefly why comparison with $\int_1^\infty \frac1x\,dx$ is relevant.

Solution

$\int_0^t \frac{x}{x^2+9}\,dx=\frac12\ln(x^2+9)\big|_0^t=\frac12\ln\!\frac{t^2+9}{9}\to\infty$.

Asymptotically $\frac{x}{x^2+9}\sim\frac1x$, so divergence matches the comparison intuition.

Answer: diverges.

Practice 15

Compute $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty \frac{dx}{x^2+4x+5}$.

Solution

$x^2+4x+5=(x+2)^2+1$, so $\int_1^t \frac{dx}{(x+2)^2+1}=\arctan(x+2)\big|_1^t$.

Limit gives $\frac{\pi}{2}-\arctan 3$.

Answer: converges to $\dfrac{\pi}{2}-\arctan 3$.

Practice 16

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1 \frac{dx}{x^{3/2}}$ and explain the singularity test near $0$.

Solution

$\int x^{-3/2}\,dx=-2x^{-1/2}$. From $t$ to $1$: $-2+2/\sqrt{t}\to\infty$ as $t\to0^+$.

Power test near $0$: $\int_0^1 x^{-p}\,dx$ converges iff $p<1$; here $p=\frac32>1$.

Answer: diverges.

Practice 17

Decompose and integrate $\displaystyle\int \frac{2x^2+7x+6}{(x+2)^2(x+1)}\,dx$.

Solution

Use $\dfrac{A}{x+1}+\dfrac{B}{x+2}+\dfrac{C}{(x+2)^2}$; solve to get $A=1,\;B=1,\;C=0$.

Integral is $\int\left(\frac1{x+1}+\frac1{x+2}\right)\,dx=\ln|x+1|+\ln|x+2|+C$.

Practice 18

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty xe^{-2x}\,dx$ (improper at infinity, but absolutely convergent).

Solution

By parts: $\int xe^{-2x}\,dx=-\frac{x}{2}e^{-2x}-\frac14e^{-2x}+C$.

Evaluate $0$ to $t$ then $t\to\infty$: upper terms vanish, lower gives $-\left(-\frac14\right)=\frac14$.

Answer: converges to $\frac14$.

Practice 19

Show that $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{\sqrt{x}(1+x)}$ converges and evaluate it using $x=u^2$.

Solution

Let $x=u^2$, $dx=2u\,du$, $\sqrt{x}=u$. Integrand becomes $\dfrac{2}{1+u^2}$.

Limits: $x:0\to\infty$ gives $u:0\to\infty$. So integral is $2\int_0^\infty\frac{du}{1+u^2}=2[\arctan u]_0^\infty=\pi$.

Answer: converges to $\pi$.

Practice 20

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_3^\infty \frac{dx}{x^2-4}$ via partial fractions and improper limits.

Solution

$\frac{1}{x^2-4}=\frac14\left(\frac1{x-2}-\frac1{x+2}\right)$.

$\int_3^t = \frac14\ln\left|\frac{x-2}{x+2}\right|\Big|_3^t$. As $t\to\infty$, log term $\to\ln1=0$.

Value $= -\frac14\ln\frac{1}{5}=\frac14\ln5$.

Practice 21

Determine convergence of $\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x^2}$ with full split-limit setup.

Solution

Split at $0$: $\int_{-1}^0 x^{-2}\,dx+\int_0^1 x^{-2}\,dx$.

Each piece has antiderivative $-1/x$ and blows to $+\infty$ near $0$.

Answer: diverges (both sides diverge).

Practice 22

Find constants $A,B,C$ so that $$\frac{4x+1}{(x-1)(x^2+1)}=\frac{A}{x-1}+\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+1},$$ then integrate.

Solution

Matching coefficients gives $A=\frac52,\;B=-\frac52,\;C=\frac32$.

Integral: $$\frac52\ln|x-1|-\frac54\ln(x^2+1)+\frac32\arctan x+C.$$

Practice 23

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{(x+1)^p}$ in terms of $p$, and state convergence conditions.

Solution

For $p\neq1$: $\int_0^t (x+1)^{-p}\,dx=\frac{(t+1)^{1-p}-1}{1-p}$.

Converges iff $p>1$, then limit is $\frac{1}{p-1}$. For $p=1$, gives $\ln(t+1)\to\infty$ (diverges).

Practice 24

Challenge: determine convergence and value of $$\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{(x+1)(x+2)}.$$ Use partial fractions and one improper limit.

Solution

$\frac{1}{(x+1)(x+2)}=\frac{1}{x+1}-\frac{1}{x+2}$.

$\int_0^t = \ln\frac{x+1}{x+2}\Big|_0^t=\ln\frac{t+1}{t+2}-\ln\frac12$.

As $t\to\infty$, first log $\to\ln1=0$. So value is $\ln2$.

Answer: converges to $\ln2$.

Practice 25

Decide whether $\dfrac{x^3+2x+1}{(x-1)(x^2+1)}$ is already proper. If not, divide first, then decompose and integrate.

Solution

Degree of numerator equals degree of denominator (both 3), so divide first: $\dfrac{x^3+2x+1}{(x-1)(x^2+1)}=1+\dfrac{x^2+x+2}{(x-1)(x^2+1)}$.

Then $\dfrac{x^2+x+2}{(x-1)(x^2+1)}=\dfrac{A}{x-1}+\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+1}$. Solving: $A=2,\,B=-1,\,C=1$.

Answer: $x+2\ln|x-1|-\tfrac12\ln(x^2+1)+\arctan x+C$.

Practice 26

Decompose $\dfrac{x^2+1}{(x+1)^2(x-1)}$ and integrate. Identify which coefficient cover-up gives immediately.

Solution

Form: $\dfrac{A}{x-1}+\dfrac{B}{x+1}+\dfrac{C}{(x+1)^2}$. Cover-up at $x=1$ gives $A=1$; at $x=-1$ gives $C=-1$. Matching then $B=-1$.

Answer: $\ln|x-1|-\ln|x+1|+\dfrac{1}{x+1}+C$.

Practice 27

Show that $\displaystyle\int\dfrac{dx}{x^2(x^2+1)}$ needs both a repeated factor and an irreducible quadratic, then evaluate.

Solution

$\dfrac{1}{x^2(x^2+1)}=\dfrac{A}{x}+\dfrac{B}{x^2}+\dfrac{Cx+D}{x^2+1}$. Matching yields $A=0,\,B=1,\,C=0,\,D=-1$.

Answer: $-\dfrac1x-\arctan x+C$.

Practice 28

For $\dfrac{3x+5}{(x^2+2x+2)(x+1)}$, complete the square on the quadratic factor, decompose, and integrate fully.

Solution

First complete the square: $x^2+2x+2=(x+1)^2+1$. Write

$$\frac{3x+5}{(x^2+2x+2)(x+1)} =\frac{A}{x+1}+\frac{Bx+C}{x^2+2x+2}.$$

Clearing denominators and matching coefficients gives $$3x+5=A(x^2+2x+2)+(Bx+C)(x+1),$$ so $A+B=0$, $2A+B+C=3$, and $2A+C=5$. Hence $A=2$, $B=-2$, and $C=1$.

For the quadratic numerator, $$-2x+1=-(2x+2)+3.$$

Therefore the derivative-over-function term produces a logarithm and the remaining constant produces an arctangent: $$\int\frac{3x+5}{(x^2+2x+2)(x+1)}\,dx =2\ln|x+1|-\ln(x^2+2x+2)+3\arctan(x+1)+C.$$

Answer: $2\ln|x+1|-\ln(x^2+2x+2)+3\arctan(x+1)+C$.

Practice 29

Challenge: explain why $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}\,dx$ converges while $\displaystyle\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}\,dx$ diverges, even though both involve similar-looking integrands.

Solution

$\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\dfrac{1}{1+x^2}$ is even and both one-sided pieces converge: each equals $\pi/2$, total $\pi$.

$\dfrac{x}{1+x^2}$ is odd, but each one-sided piece $\int_0^\infty$ diverges logarithmically, so the two-sided improper integral diverges. Symmetric principal-value $=0$ is not the exam definition of convergence.

Practice 30

Determine convergence of $\displaystyle\int_0^1\dfrac{\arcsin x}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}\,dx$ and evaluate if convergent.

Solution

Let $u=\arcsin x$, $du=\dfrac{dx}{\sqrt{1-x^2}}$. Limits $0\to\pi/2$. Integral becomes $\int_0^{\pi/2} u\,du=\dfrac{\pi^2}{8}$.

Near $x=1$ the integrand behaves like $\dfrac{\pi/2}{\sqrt{1-x}}$, which is integrable ($p=1/2<1$).

Answer: converges to $\dfrac{\pi^2}{8}$.

Practice 31

Use comparison to decide $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\dfrac{\sqrt{x}}{x^2+\sin^2x}\,dx$ without evaluating.

Solution

For $x\ge1$, denominator $\ge x^2$, so $0<\dfrac{\sqrt x}{x^2+\sin^2x}\le\dfrac{\sqrt x}{x^2}=x^{-3/2}$.

$\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}\,dx$ converges ($p=3/2>1$), so by comparison the original converges.

Practice 32

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_2^\infty\dfrac{dx}{x\sqrt{x^2-4}}$ with a trig or hyperbolic substitution, stating convergence.

Solution

Let $x=2\sec\theta$ ($\theta\in(0,\pi/2)$), $dx=2\sec\theta\tan\theta\,d\theta$. Then $\sqrt{x^2-4}=2\tan\theta$.

Integral becomes $\int_0^{\pi/2}\dfrac{2\sec\theta\tan\theta}{2\sec\theta\cdot 2\tan\theta}\,d\theta=\tfrac12\int_0^{\pi/2}\,d\theta=\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

Answer: converges to $\dfrac{\pi}{4}$.

Practice 33

Decompose $\dfrac{x^3}{(x^2+1)^2}$ (repeated irreducible quadratic) and integrate.

Solution

Write $\dfrac{x^3}{(x^2+1)^2}=\dfrac{Ax+B}{x^2+1}+\dfrac{Cx+D}{(x^2+1)^2}$. Matching: $A=1,\,B=0,\,C=-1,\,D=0$.

So $\dfrac{x}{x^2+1}-\dfrac{x}{(x^2+1)^2}$. Integrate: $\tfrac12\ln(x^2+1)+\tfrac{1}{2(x^2+1)}+C$.

Practice 34

Find all $\alpha$ so that $\displaystyle\int_0^1 x^\alpha\ln x\,dx$ converges, and evaluate when it does.

Solution

Set $\beta=\alpha+1$. When $\alpha>-1$, $\beta>0$. Integrating from $\varepsilon$ to $1$ by parts gives

$$\int_\varepsilon^1x^\alpha\ln x\,dx =\left[\frac{x^\beta\ln x}{\beta} -\frac{x^\beta}{\beta^2}\right]_\varepsilon^1.$$

As $\varepsilon\to0^+$, both $\varepsilon^\beta$ and $\varepsilon^\beta\ln\varepsilon$ tend to zero, so the integral converges to $-1/\beta^2=-1/(\alpha+1)^2$.

If $\alpha=-1$, the antiderivative is $\tfrac12(\ln x)^2$, whose lower-end limit is unbounded. If $\alpha<-1$, the power singularity is even stronger. Thus the integral diverges for every $\alpha\le-1$.

Answer: converges iff $\alpha>-1$, value $-\dfrac{1}{(\alpha+1)^2}$.

Practice 35

The Cauchy principal value uses equal cutoffs on the two sides of a singularity: $$\mathrm{P.V.}\int_{-1}^{1}\frac{dx}{x} =\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\left( \int_{-1}^{-\varepsilon}\frac{dx}{x} +\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{dx}{x}\right).$$ Evaluate this symmetric limit and explain why the integral still diverges under the ordinary improper-integral definition.

Solution

$\mathrm{P.V.}=\lim_{\varepsilon\to0^+}\left(\int_{-1}^{-\varepsilon}+\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\right)=\lim(\ln\varepsilon-\ln\varepsilon)=0$.

Ordinary definition requires each one-sided limit separately; each diverges, so the improper integral diverges even though the principal value is $0$.

Practice 36

Integrate $\displaystyle\int\dfrac{4x^2-3x+6}{(x-1)(x^2+4x+5)}\,dx$ after completing the square.

Solution

Decomposition $\dfrac{A}{x-1}+\dfrac{Bx+C}{x^2+4x+5}$ with $A=1,\,B=3,\,C=-1$.

$x^2+4x+5=(x+2)^2+1$. Split $3x-1=\tfrac32(2x+4)-7$.

Answer: $\ln|x-1|+\tfrac32\ln(x^2+4x+5)-7\arctan(x+2)+C$.

Practice 37

Does $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty e^{-x}\sin(e^{x})\,dx$ converge? Justify by a substitution that turns it into a comparison-friendly integral.

Solution

Let $u=e^{x}$, so $x=\ln u$, $dx=\dfrac{du}{u}$. Limits: $x:0\to\infty$ gives $u:1\to\infty$.

The integrand becomes $e^{-\ln u}\sin u\cdot\dfrac{du}{u}=\dfrac{\sin u}{u^2}\,du$.

Since $\left|\dfrac{\sin u}{u^2}\right|\le\dfrac1{u^2}$ and $\int_1^\infty u^{-2}\,du$ converges, the integral converges absolutely (hence converges).

Answer: converges (absolutely). A closed antiderivative is not required.

Practice 38

Set up and evaluate $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\dfrac{dx}{x(x+1)(x+2)}$ using partial fractions.

Solution

$\dfrac{1}{x(x+1)(x+2)}=\dfrac{1/2}{x}-\dfrac{1}{x+1}+\dfrac{1/2}{x+2}$.

$\int_1^t=\tfrac12\ln x-\ln(x+1)+\tfrac12\ln(x+2)\Big|_1^t\to\ln2-\tfrac12\ln3$ as $t\to\infty$ after simplification.

Answer: $\ln\!\left(\dfrac{2}{\sqrt3}\right)$.

Practice 39

Explain the difference between a vertical asymptote singularity and an infinite-limit singularity using $\int_0^1 x^{-1/2}\,dx$ vs $\int_1^\infty x^{-3/2}\,dx$.

Solution

Both converge, but for opposite $p$-tests: near $0$, $\int_0^1 x^{-p}$ converges for $p<1$; at $\infty$, $\int_1^\infty x^{-p}$ converges for $p>1$.

Here $p=1/2<1$ (endpoint singularity OK) and $p=3/2>1$ (tail OK). Mixing the two rules is a common exam error.

Practice 40

Decompose $\dfrac{2x^3+x}{(x^2+1)^2}$ and integrate, identifying the $f'/f$ piece vs the arctan-type leftover.

Solution

$\dfrac{2x^3+x}{(x^2+1)^2}=\dfrac{Ax+B}{x^2+1}+\dfrac{Cx+D}{(x^2+1)^2}$. Solve: $A=2,\,B=0,\,C=-1,\,D=0$.

Integral: $\ln(x^2+1)+\dfrac{1}{2(x^2+1)}+C$.

Practice 41

Determine whether $\displaystyle\int_0^{\pi/2}\tan\theta\,d\theta$ converges.

Solution

$\tan\theta=\sin\theta/\cos\theta$ has asymptote at $\pi/2$. $\int_0^{\pi/2-\varepsilon}\tan\theta\,d\theta=-\ln|\cos\theta|_0^{\pi/2-\varepsilon}=-\ln(\sin\varepsilon)\to\infty$.

Answer: diverges.

Practice 42

Evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^\infty\dfrac{dx}{(x+2)^2(x+5)}$ by partial fractions and an improper limit.

Solution

$\dfrac{1}{(x+2)^2(x+5)}=\dfrac{A}{x+2}+\dfrac{B}{(x+2)^2}+\dfrac{C}{x+5}$. Clearing: $1=A(x+2)(x+5)+B(x+5)+C(x+2)^2$.

At $x=-2$: $1=3B\Rightarrow B=\tfrac13$. At $x=-5$: $1=9C\Rightarrow C=\tfrac19$. Matching $x^2$: $0=A+C\Rightarrow A=-\tfrac19$.

$\int_0^t=\left[-\dfrac19\ln|x+2|-\dfrac{1}{3(x+2)}+\dfrac19\ln|x+5|\right]_0^t$.

As $t\to\infty$: $-\tfrac19\ln\!\dfrac{t+2}{t+5}\to0$, and $-\dfrac{1}{3(t+2)}\to0$. Lower limit contributes $+\dfrac19\ln2+\dfrac16-\dfrac19\ln5$.

Answer: $\dfrac16+\dfrac19\ln\dfrac25=\dfrac16-\dfrac19\ln\dfrac52$.

Practice 43

Challenge: use the geometric series for $\dfrac{1}{1-x}$ to evaluate $\displaystyle\int_0^1\dfrac{\ln x}{1-x}\,dx$, justifying term-by-term integration.

Solution

For $0<x<1$, $\dfrac{1}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n$. Then $\dfrac{\ln x}{1-x}=\sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n\ln x$.

Integration by parts gives $$\int_0^1x^n\ln x\,dx=-\frac{1}{(n+1)^2}.$$

To justify exchanging sum and integral, apply the monotone convergence theorem to the non-negative functions $-x^n\ln x$: their partial sums increase pointwise to $-\ln x/(1-x)$, and the theorem states that the integral of such an increasing non-negative limit equals the limit of the integrals.

Therefore $$\int_0^1\frac{\ln x}{1-x}\,dx =-\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac1{n^2} =-\frac{\pi^2}{6}.$$

Answer: $-\dfrac{\pi^2}{6}$.

Practice 44

Find $A,B,C,D$ for $\dfrac{x^3+x+1}{(x-1)^2(x^2+x+1)}$ and set up (do not fully expand) the integral's log and arctan pieces after completing the square.

Solution

Form $\dfrac{A}{x-1}+\dfrac{B}{(x-1)^2}+\dfrac{Cx+D}{x^2+x+1}$. Matching gives $A=\tfrac13,\,B=1,\,C=\tfrac23,\,D=\tfrac13$ (verify by clearing).

$x^2+x+1=(x+\tfrac12)^2+\tfrac34$. The quadratic piece splits into a $\ln(x^2+x+1)$ term and an $\arctan\!\big((2x+1)/\sqrt3\big)$ term.

Practice 45

Does $\displaystyle\int_e^\infty\dfrac{dx}{x(\ln x)(\ln\ln x)}$ converge? Use $u=\ln\ln x$.

Solution

The integrand is undefined at the lower endpoint because $\ln\ln e=0$, so this is improper at both $x=e$ and $x=\infty$. Let $u=\ln\ln x$, giving $du=\dfrac{dx}{x\ln x}$. As $x\to e^+$, $u\to0^+$; as $x\to\infty$, $u\to\infty$.

Therefore the integral becomes $$\int_{0}^{\infty}\frac{du}{u}.$$

Already at the lower endpoint, $$\int_{\varepsilon}^{1}\frac{du}{u}=-\ln\varepsilon\longrightarrow\infty \qquad(\varepsilon\to0^+).$$

Hence the original improper integral diverges. It also diverges at infinity, but failure at either endpoint is sufficient.

Practice 46

Integrate $\displaystyle\int\dfrac{dx}{(x^2+1)(x^2+4)}$ and then decide if $\int_0^\infty$ of that integrand converges.

Solution

$\dfrac{1}{(x^2+1)(x^2+4)}=\dfrac13\left(\dfrac{1}{x^2+1}-\dfrac{1}{x^2+4}\right)$.

Indefinite: $\tfrac13\arctan x-\tfrac16\arctan(x/2)+C$. From $0$ to $\infty$: $\tfrac13(\pi/2)-\tfrac16(\pi/2)=\pi/12$.

Answer: converges to $\dfrac{\pi}{12}$.

Practice 47

A student claims $\displaystyle\int_0^2\dfrac{dx}{(x-1)^{2/3}}$ diverges because of the asymptote at $x=1$. Prove or refute.

Solution

Split at $1$. Near $1$, let $u=x-1$, integrand $|u|^{-2/3}$. Since $p=2/3<1$, $\int|u|^{-2/3}\,du$ converges on both sides.

Explicitly: $\int(x-1)^{-2/3}\,dx=3(x-1)^{1/3}$, which approaches $0$ as $x\to1$. Whole integral equals $3\cdot1^{1/3}-3(-1)^{1/3}$ interpreted carefully on each side = $6$.

Answer: student is wrong; converges (to $6$).

Practice 48

Capstone: for $\displaystyle\int_1^\infty\dfrac{x+2}{x^2+4x+5}\,dx$, complete the square, identify a divergent log piece and a convergent arctan piece, and conclude.

Solution

$x^2+4x+5=(x+2)^2+1$, numerator $x+2$ is exactly half of derivative of denominator's square-complete form: $\dfrac{x+2}{(x+2)^2+1}$.

$\int_1^t=\tfrac12\ln((x+2)^2+1)\big|_1^t\to\infty$. The integral diverges (pure log growth).

If numerator had been constant, arctan would converge; here the $f'/f$ piece wins and forces divergence.
