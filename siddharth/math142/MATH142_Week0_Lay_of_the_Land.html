01 What MATH142 adds to calculus

MATH141 introduced derivatives and integrals. A derivative measures a local rate of change, and an integral accumulates small contributions into a total. MATH142 develops those two ideas into working engineering tools.

The course starts by expanding the range of functions you can integrate. It then uses integration to measure curved shapes. Differential equations reverse the direction of the problem: you are given a rate law and must recover the function that follows it. The final weeks study infinite processes and replace complicated functions with polynomial approximations.

The central thread

Differentiation moves from a quantity to its rate. Integration moves from a rate or density to a total. Differential equations begin with the rate and ask for the quantity. Taylor series use repeated derivatives at one point to rebuild the function nearby. The whole subject explores the movement between a function, its rates of change, and its accumulated effect.

03 Weeks 1–3: integration as an engineering language

An integral represents a total built from continuously varying contributions:

$$\text{total}=\int \text{density or rate}\times\text{small interval}$$

The integrand may be a force spread over distance, flow spread over time, density spread through a section, or power spread over an operating period. Weeks 1 and 2 expand the library of integrals you can evaluate exactly.

  • Integration by parts reverses the product rule and handles products such as $x e^x$ or $x\sin x$.
  • Trigonometric integrals use identities to handle powers and products of sine and cosine, which occur in oscillation and periodic motion.
  • Trigonometric substitution handles square roots associated with circles and quadratic geometry.
  • Partial fractions break rational functions into pieces that integrate directly. This technique returns in differential equations and becomes central to inverse Laplace transforms in MATH283.
  • Improper integrals define totals over infinite intervals and functions with singularities through limits.

Week 3 covers the case where an antiderivative is unavailable or the available information comes as measurements. The trapezoidal and Simpson rules estimate the area under a curve from sampled values, and their error bounds state how much accuracy the sampling interval can support.

Mining applications
  • Integrate a variable conveyor mass-flow rate over a shift to obtain total tonnes moved.
  • Use numerical integration on pump-flow readings recorded every five minutes to estimate total water removed.
  • Integrate a stress distribution across a beam or support section to obtain a resultant force.
  • Use an improper integral in models where an effect decays over a long time or distance.
04 Weeks 4–6: choosing coordinates and measuring shapes

Cartesian coordinates describe a point by horizontal and vertical displacement. Polar coordinates describe it by distance and direction, $(r,\theta)$. Circles, spirals, lobes, rotating machinery and radial influence zones often have short polar equations and long Cartesian equations. The coordinate system is part of the model.

Parametric equations add a third description. Both coordinates are functions of a parameter, often time:

$$x=x(t),\qquad y=y(t),\qquad \frac{dy}{dx}=\frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$$

This form records motion along a path and allows the tangent, enclosed area and travelled length to be calculated directly from the motion law.

Weeks 5 and 6 apply integration to geometry. Area comes from summing thin strips, volume from summing disks, washers or shells, arc length from summing short straight segments, and surface area from summing narrow bands around a rotated curve. The formulas differ because the small piece being accumulated differs.

Mining applications
  • Calculate the cross-sectional area and volume of a stockpile from a fitted profile.
  • Use polar coordinates for the influence zone around a blasthole or dewatering bore.
  • Describe the trajectory of a haul truck or projectile with parametric equations.
  • Calculate the length of a decline, conveyor or pipeline along a curved alignment.
  • Estimate the liner or coating area of a hopper, chute or vessel from a surface of revolution.
05 Weeks 7–9: differential equations and system behaviour

Engineering laws are often written as statements about rates. A tank level changes according to inflow and outflow. A body cools according to its temperature difference from the surroundings. Current in an RL circuit changes according to the applied voltage and the stored magnetic energy. A differential equation converts the rate statement into mathematics.

A first-order differential equation contains one derivative. A second-order equation contains a second derivative and can describe acceleration and vibration. The solution is a whole function that predicts the system through time.

The engineering reading of a solution

Many solutions contain a transient term and a long-term term. The transient records the effect of the initial condition and fades with time in a stable system. The long-term term records the continuing input or equilibrium. Reading those two parts often gives more information than the final formula alone.

Mining applications
  • Model concentration in a process tank with changing inflow and outflow.
  • Predict the decay of airborne dust concentration after a source stops.
  • Model groundwater recovery after pumping with a first-order approximation.
  • Describe vibration in a screen, support or machine component with a second-order equation.
  • Use an initial condition to match the model to a measured starting temperature, level or displacement.
06 Weeks 10–12: limits, infinite sums, and local models

Limits provide the language for long-term behaviour and approximation error. L'Hôpital's rule resolves indeterminate forms by comparing the derivatives of two competing quantities. It is also a systematic way to compare growth rates as the input becomes large.

A sequence is an ordered list of numbers. A series is the sum of that list. Convergence tests decide whether infinitely many contributions settle to a finite total. This question appears whenever a model contains repeated corrections, repeated reflections, decaying payments, or modes of vibration.

Taylor's theorem uses the value and derivatives of a function at one point to build a polynomial:

$$f(x)\approx f(a)+f'(a)(x-a)+\frac{f''(a)}{2!}(x-a)^2+\cdots$$

Polynomials are easy to calculate, differentiate and integrate. A Taylor polynomial gives a computer, calculator or engineer a practical local model for functions such as $e^x$, $\sin x$ and $\ln(1+x)$. The remainder term measures the approximation error.

Mining applications
  • Use a limit to find the steady-state concentration or temperature predicted by a model.
  • Use a geometric series to total a repeating discounted cost or a chain of diminishing effects.
  • Use a Taylor polynomial to simplify a nonlinear sensor-calibration equation near its operating point.
  • Use an error bound to choose how many terms give sufficient accuracy for an engineering estimate.

08 One mining project across the course

Consider a mine dewatering system. The same project can use every phase of MATH142.

  1. Integration: pump flow varies through the day. Integrating the flow-rate curve gives the total volume removed. Numerical integration works directly from logged flow readings.
  2. Geometry: the drawdown zone around a bore is naturally radial, so polar coordinates describe it. Area and volume integrals estimate the affected water-bearing region.
  3. Differential equations: the water level changes according to recharge and pumping. A first-order equation predicts the level through time and identifies its equilibrium.
  4. Limits: the limit as $t\to\infty$ gives the long-term level under continuous pumping.
  5. Series: a nonlinear relationship between head and flow can be approximated near the operating point with a Taylor polynomial, making control calculations quicker.

The techniques answer different questions about the same physical system. This is how they will appear in engineering work: selected according to the quantity being sought.

Preparation priority

Fluency with integration by parts, partial fractions, trigonometric integrals and first-order differential equations gives the strongest preparation for the calculus strand of MATH283. These techniques appear inside the new methods, so keeping them active reduces the amount of work competing for attention.

10 What you will be able to solve

By the end of MATH142 you should be able to:

  • select an integration technique from the structure of the integrand and carry it through accurately;
  • estimate an integral from measured data and report an error bound;
  • choose Cartesian, polar or parametric coordinates to suit the geometry;
  • calculate areas, volumes, arc lengths and surfaces generated by curves;
  • translate a physical rate statement into a differential equation and solve the main first- and second-order families in the syllabus;
  • interpret initial conditions, transient response, equilibrium and oscillation;
  • decide whether an infinite sequence or series converges;
  • build a Taylor polynomial and control its approximation error.
Carry this into Week 1
  • Integration is the common language. It supports geometry, differential equations, numerical work and the later transform subjects.
  • A coordinate system is a modelling choice. Cartesian, polar and parametric forms make different features easy to calculate.
  • A differential equation is a rate law. Its solution predicts the system response through time.
  • A series is a controlled approximation. Convergence and error bounds determine how much trust to place in it.
  • The phases reinforce one another. Techniques learned early continue to appear in later calculations.
