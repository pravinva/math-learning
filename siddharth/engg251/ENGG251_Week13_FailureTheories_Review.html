0 Maximum Shear Stress Theory (Tresca)

A failure theory predicts when a general (multi-axial) stress state will cause a material to yield or fracture, by comparing it to the material's simple uniaxial test data (yield stress $\sigma_Y$ from a standard tension test). This is essential because most real components experience combined/multi-axial stress (Weeks 10–12), not the simple uniaxial stress of a tensile test coupon.

The maximum shear stress theory (Tresca criterion, for ductile materials) states that yielding begins when the maximum shear stress at a point reaches the maximum shear stress at yield in a simple tension test. Since a uniaxial tension test has $\tau_{\max}=\sigma_Y/2$ at yield (Week 10, Section 0), the criterion becomes: $\tau_{\max}=\dfrac{|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|}2\ge\dfrac{\sigma_Y}2$, i.e. yielding occurs when $|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|\ge\sigma_Y$ (using the two extreme principal stresses, in the general 3D case, of the three principal stresses).

Key idea

$$\tau_{\max}=\frac{|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|}2\ge\frac{\sigma_Y}2 \quad\iff\quad |\sigma_1-\sigma_2|\ge\sigma_Y \quad \text{(Tresca yield criterion, ductile materials)}$$

Worked example

Example. A steel component ($\sigma_Y=300$ MPa) has principal stresses $\sigma_1=180$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-60$ MPa. Check for yielding by the Tresca criterion.

$|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|=|180-(-60)|=240$ MPa $<300$ MPa $=\sigma_Y$. No yielding predicted; factor of safety against yield $=300/240=1.25$.

Practice 1

A component ($\sigma_Y=250$ MPa) has principal stresses $\sigma_1=150$ MPa, $\sigma_2=50$ MPa. Check for yielding by Tresca.

Solution

$|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|=100$ MPa $<250$ MPa. No yielding; $FS=250/100=2.5$.

Practice 2

A component ($\sigma_Y=300$ MPa) has $\sigma_1=200$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-150$ MPa. Check for yielding by Tresca.

Solution

$|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|=350$ MPa $>300$ MPa. Yielding IS predicted by Tresca.

Practice 3

A shaft in pure torsion has principal stresses $\sigma_1=\tau,\sigma_2=-\tau$ (from Week 10's pure-shear result). Using Tresca, find the shear stress $\tau_Y$ at which yielding begins, in terms of $\sigma_Y$.

Solution

$|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|=|\tau-(-\tau)|=2\tau=\sigma_Y\Rightarrow\tau_Y=\sigma_Y/2$ — the Tresca-predicted shear yield stress is exactly half the tensile yield stress, a widely used design rule of thumb.

Practice 4

A steel component ($\sigma_Y=350$ MPa) has $\sigma_1=100$ MPa, $\sigma_2=80$ MPa (both positive/tensile, close together). Check Tresca, and comment on why this state is far from yielding despite both stresses being individually significant fractions of $\sigma_Y$.

Solution

$|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|=20$ MPa, very small compared to $\sigma_Y=350$ MPa. This illustrates that Tresca (and shear-stress-based yielding generally) depends on the difference between principal stresses, not their individual magnitudes — a state close to equal biaxial stress (small difference) is far safer against yielding than the individual stress values might suggest, since shear stress (the actual yielding mechanism in ductile materials) is comparatively small when the principal stresses are close together.

Practice 5

Find the factor of safety against yielding (Tresca) for a component with $\sigma_Y=400$ MPa, $\sigma_1=120$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-90$ MPa.

Solution

$|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|=210$ MPa. $FS=400/210=1.90$.

Practice 6

Explain why the Tresca criterion uses $|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|$ (the difference between the two extreme principal stresses) rather than, say, $\sigma_1$ alone or the sum $\sigma_1+\sigma_2$, referencing the physical mechanism of ductile yielding.

Solution

Ductile materials yield via shear-driven plastic slip along crystallographic planes, not through direct normal-stress-driven separation. Since the maximum shear stress at a point is $\tau_{\max}=|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|/2$ (Week 10), it is specifically this difference between the extreme principal stresses that drives the shear-slip yielding mechanism — a state with large individual principal stresses but a small difference between them produces comparatively little shear stress and is therefore much safer against yielding than the raw magnitudes might suggest, which is exactly why the criterion is built around the difference rather than either individual value or their sum.

1 Maximum Distortion Energy Theory (von Mises)

The maximum distortion energy theory (von Mises criterion, also for ductile materials) is based on the idea that yielding is driven specifically by the energy associated with shape distortion (shear-type deformation), not the energy associated with pure volume change (hydrostatic stress). For plane stress ($\sigma_3=0$), the criterion is: yielding occurs when $\sigma_1^2-\sigma_1\sigma_2+\sigma_2^2\ge\sigma_Y^2$.

Von Mises is generally regarded as slightly more accurate than Tresca for real ductile metals (better agreement with experimental yield data), though Tresca is more conservative (always predicts yielding at an equal or lower combined-stress level than von Mises) and mathematically simpler — which is why Tresca remains popular for quick, conservative hand-calculations, while von Mises is the standard choice in most modern finite-element software and design codes.

Key idea

$$\sigma_1^2-\sigma_1\sigma_2+\sigma_2^2\ge\sigma_Y^2 \quad \text{(von Mises yield criterion, plane stress, ductile materials)}$$

Worked example

Example. Using the same stress state as Section 0's worked example ($\sigma_Y=300$ MPa, $\sigma_1=180$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-60$ MPa), check for yielding by von Mises.

$\sigma_1^2-\sigma_1\sigma_2+\sigma_2^2=180^2-180(-60)+(-60)^2=32400+10800+3600=46800$.

$\sigma_Y^2=300^2=90000$. Since $46800<90000$, no yielding predicted — consistent with Tresca's conclusion, though von Mises gives an effective stress of $\sqrt{46800}=216.3$ MPa (vs Tresca's 240 MPa), showing von Mises is the less conservative of the two.

Practice 7

A component ($\sigma_Y=250$ MPa) has $\sigma_1=150$ MPa, $\sigma_2=50$ MPa. Check for yielding by von Mises.

Solution

$150^2-150(50)+50^2=22500-7500+2500=17500$. $\sigma_Y^2=62500$. $17500<62500$: no yielding.

Practice 8

Find the effective (von Mises) stress for the previous question, and compare to $\sigma_Y=250$ MPa.

Solution

$\sqrt{17500}=132.3$ MPa $<250$ MPa — well within the safe range.

Practice 9

For a shaft in pure torsion ($\sigma_1=\tau,\sigma_2=-\tau$), find the shear yield stress $\tau_Y$ predicted by von Mises, in terms of $\sigma_Y$, and compare to the Tresca result ($\tau_Y=\sigma_Y/2$) found earlier.

Solution

$\tau^2-\tau(-\tau)+\tau^2=\sigma_Y^2\Rightarrow3\tau^2=\sigma_Y^2\Rightarrow\tau_Y=\sigma_Y/\sqrt3\approx0.577\sigma_Y$ — slightly higher than Tresca's $0.5\sigma_Y$, confirming von Mises is less conservative (predicts a higher, less restrictive, allowable shear stress) than Tresca for the same material.

Practice 10

A component ($\sigma_Y=300$ MPa) has $\sigma_1=200$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-150$ MPa (same stress state that Tresca predicted WOULD yield in Section 0). Check von Mises for the same state.

Solution

$200^2-200(-150)+(-150)^2=40000+30000+22500=92500$. $\sigma_Y^2=90000$. $92500>90000$: von Mises ALSO predicts yielding for this state (both theories agree here, though close to the boundary).

Practice 11

Explain, conceptually, why von Mises is described as being based on "distortion energy" specifically, rather than total strain energy, referencing the role of hydrostatic (equal tri-axial) stress.

Solution

Total strain energy in a stressed body includes both a volumetric (dilatational) component, associated with pure hydrostatic stress causing uniform volume change without any shape distortion, and a distortional component, associated with shape-changing (shear-type) deformation. Experimentally, ductile metals can withstand enormous hydrostatic pressure (equal stress in all three directions) without yielding at all, since there is no shape distortion to drive slip — this shows that only the distortional part of the strain energy actually correlates with yielding, which is precisely why von Mises deliberately excludes the hydrostatic/volumetric contribution and bases its criterion purely on the distortion energy.

Practice 12

Compare the Tresca and von Mises predicted "effective stress" for a state of equal biaxial tension, $\sigma_1=\sigma_2=\sigma_0$ (equal, both positive). Show that Tresca predicts NO yielding regardless of how large $\sigma_0$ becomes, while von Mises correctly predicts eventual yielding.

Solution

Tresca: $|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|=|\sigma_0-\sigma_0|=0$, always less than $\sigma_Y$ regardless of $\sigma_0$ — Tresca incorrectly predicts the material never yields under equal biaxial tension, a known limitation of the theory. Von Mises: $\sigma_0^2-\sigma_0^2+\sigma_0^2=\sigma_0^2$, which reaches $\sigma_Y^2$ (predicting yield) once $\sigma_0=\sigma_Y$ — a physically sensible result, since equal biaxial tension is genuinely known to cause yielding at $\sigma_0=\sigma_Y$ experimentally, illustrating one specific scenario where von Mises is measurably more accurate than Tresca.

2 Comparing Failure Theories for Ductile Materials

Plotted on a $\sigma_1$-$\sigma_2$ diagram, the Tresca criterion forms a hexagon and the von Mises criterion forms a smooth ellipse that circumscribes the Tresca hexagon (touching it exactly at the six points corresponding to uniaxial and pure-shear stress states) — visually confirming Tresca is always the more conservative (smaller safe region) of the two theories.

Practical guidance: both theories agree closely for most real stress states (typically within about 15% of each other), and either is acceptable for standard design; von Mises is preferred where slightly better accuracy matters (and is the default in most FEA software), while Tresca remains popular for hand calculations and code-based design due to its mathematical simplicity and inherent conservatism (a small safety margin "for free").

Key idea

$$\text{Tresca: hexagon (conservative)} \qquad \text{von Mises: circumscribing ellipse (slightly less conservative, generally more accurate)}$$

Worked example

Example. A component ($\sigma_Y=280$ MPa) has $\sigma_1=160$ MPa, $\sigma_2=100$ MPa (both positive, fairly close together). Compare the factor of safety predicted by Tresca and von Mises.

Tresca: $|\sigma_1-\sigma_2|=60$ MPa. $FS_{\text{Tresca}}=280/60=4.67$.

Von Mises: $\sqrt{160^2-160(100)+100^2}=\sqrt{25600-16000+10000}=\sqrt{19600}=140$ MPa. $FS_{\text{vonMises}}=280/140=2.0$ — a dramatically different result here, illustrating that for stress states with principal stresses close together (small difference, but not equal), Tresca can be extremely (sometimes unrealistically) conservative since it only depends on the difference, while von Mises captures the true combined severity of both stresses.

Practice 13

A component ($\sigma_Y=320$ MPa) has $\sigma_1=100$ MPa, $\sigma_2=0$ (simple uniaxial tension). Confirm both Tresca and von Mises give the same factor of safety for this simple case, and explain why.

Solution

Tresca: $|100-0|=100$ MPa, $FS=3.2$. Von Mises: $\sqrt{100^2-0+0}=100$ MPa, $FS=3.2$ — identical, because both theories are specifically calibrated to exactly match uniaxial tension test data (this is one of the six points where the Tresca hexagon and von Mises ellipse touch, by construction).

Practice 14

A component has $\sigma_1=120$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-40$ MPa, $\sigma_Y=250$ MPa. Find the factor of safety by both Tresca and von Mises, and note how close the two results are for this case.

Solution

Tresca: $|120-(-40)|=160$ MPa, $FS=250/160=1.56$. Von Mises: $\sqrt{120^2-120(-40)+(-40)^2}=\sqrt{14400+4800+1600}=\sqrt{20800}=144.2$ MPa, $FS=250/144.2=1.73$ — reasonably close (about 10% difference), typical of most general stress states away from the special equal-biaxial or near-equal-principal-stress cases.

Practice 15

Explain why the two theories always agree exactly at the six "corner" points of the Tresca hexagon (e.g. pure uniaxial tension, pure uniaxial compression, and pure shear), using the geometric fact that the von Mises ellipse is inscribed touching the hexagon at those specific points.

Solution

Both theories are deliberately calibrated using the same simple uniaxial tension test data ($\sigma_Y$) as their reference point, so by construction they must agree exactly at pure uniaxial stress states. The pure shear case is also a point of exact agreement because both theories independently derive a shear-yield-stress prediction directly and consistently from that same shared uniaxial calibration (even though the specific predicted values, $\sigma_Y/2$ for Tresca vs $\sigma_Y/\sqrt3$ for von Mises, differ numerically) — the geometric picture of the ellipse touching the hexagon at exactly six points visually captures these several points of forced agreement between the two theories.

Practice 16

A ductile component is analysed and found to have a Tresca factor of safety of 1.8. Without further calculation, state whether the true (von Mises) factor of safety is likely to be higher, lower, or the same, and explain why using the geometric relationship between the two criteria.

Solution

Since the von Mises ellipse circumscribes (is always outside or on) the Tresca hexagon, von Mises always predicts an equal or larger "safe" stress region for the same material — meaning the true von Mises factor of safety will be equal to or somewhat higher than the Tresca value of 1.8 (never lower), confirming that a Tresca-based design is always at least as safe (often more conservative) than one based on von Mises.

3 Brittle Material Theories & Subject Review

Brittle materials (cast iron, ceramics, concrete, glass) fail by fracture rather than yielding, typically at a much lower strain, and often have significantly different strength in tension versus compression (unlike most ductile metals). The simplest brittle criterion, the maximum normal stress theory, predicts fracture when the largest principal stress reaches the material's ultimate tensile strength $\sigma_{ut}$ (or the most negative principal stress reaches the ultimate compressive strength $\sigma_{uc}$, if different): $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_{ut}$ or $\sigma_2\le-\sigma_{uc}$.

A refined criterion, Mohr's failure theory for brittle materials, uses separate Mohr's circles constructed from uniaxial tension, uniaxial compression, and (if available) pure shear test data, then draws an envelope tangent to all of them — any stress state whose own Mohr's circle stays within this envelope is predicted safe. This subject has now covered the complete toolkit: statics review and stress/strain fundamentals (Weeks 1–2), beam internal forces and bending/deflection (Weeks 3–5), column buckling (Week 6), transverse shear and torsion (Weeks 8–9), stress/strain transformation via Mohr's circle (Weeks 10–11), and combined loading with pressure vessels and failure theories (Weeks 12–13) — each week's techniques building directly on the ones before it.

Key idea

$$\text{Max normal stress (brittle): } \sigma_1\ge\sigma_{ut} \text{ or } \sigma_2\le-\sigma_{uc} \qquad \text{Mohr’s theory: envelope tangent to tension/compression/shear circles}$$

Worked example

Example. A cast iron component ($\sigma_{ut}=200$ MPa, $\sigma_{uc}=800$ MPa) has principal stresses $\sigma_1=150$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-300$ MPa. Check for fracture by the maximum normal stress theory.

Tensile check: $\sigma_1=150$ MPa $<200$ MPa $=\sigma_{ut}$. Safe in tension. Compressive check: $\sigma_2=-300$ MPa, magnitude $300<800$ MPa $=\sigma_{uc}$. Safe in compression. No fracture predicted by either individual check.

Practice 17

A cast iron component ($\sigma_{ut}=180$ MPa) has $\sigma_1=200$ MPa. Check for fracture by the maximum normal stress theory.

Solution

$\sigma_1=200$ MPa $>180$ MPa $=\sigma_{ut}$. Fracture IS predicted (tensile failure).

Practice 18

A brittle component ($\sigma_{ut}=150$ MPa, $\sigma_{uc}=600$ MPa) has $\sigma_1=80$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-500$ MPa. Check both the tension and compression criteria.

Solution

Tension: $80<150$, safe. Compression: $|-500|=500<600$, safe. No fracture predicted by either check.

Practice 19

Explain why using a ductile failure theory (Tresca or von Mises) would be fundamentally inappropriate for a cast iron component, referencing the different failure mechanisms of brittle versus ductile materials.

Solution

Tresca and von Mises are both built around the idea that failure is driven by shear stress causing plastic slip (yielding) — a mechanism specific to ductile materials that can sustain significant plastic deformation before failing. Brittle materials fail instead by direct fracture under normal (tensile) stress, typically with negligible plastic deformation beforehand, and often have dramatically different strength in tension versus compression (a distinction that ductile-material shear-based theories don't even account for, since they generally assume roughly symmetric tensile/compressive behaviour). Applying a shear-based ductile theory to a brittle material would completely miss the actual governing failure mechanism and could give a dangerously unsafe (or overly conservative and impractical) prediction.

Practice 20

A component made from a ductile steel ($\sigma_Y=350$ MPa) has principal stresses $\sigma_1=220$ MPa, $\sigma_2=180$ MPa. Using the more accurate of the two ductile theories covered this week (von Mises), find the factor of safety.

Solution

$\sqrt{220^2-220(180)+180^2}=\sqrt{48400-39600+32400}=\sqrt{41200}=203.0$ MPa. $FS=350/203.0=1.72$.

Practice 21

Summarising the whole subject: a solid shaft under combined bending and torsion, made of ductile steel, has principal stresses $\sigma_1=90$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-30$ MPa at its critical point (found via the flexure formula, torsion formula, and Mohr's circle, as in Week 10 and Week 12). Using $\sigma_Y=280$ MPa, find the factor of safety against yielding by both Tresca and von Mises.

Solution

Tresca: $|90-(-30)|=120$ MPa, $FS=280/120=2.33$. Von Mises: $\sqrt{90^2-90(-30)+(-30)^2}=\sqrt{8100+2700+900}=\sqrt{11700}=108.2$ MPa, $FS=280/108.2=2.59$ — this single problem draws together bending stress (Week 4), torsion (Week 9), stress transformation (Week 10), and failure theory (Week 13), the complete arc of the subject.

Practice 22

Reflecting on the subject as a whole, explain the general pattern connecting the "deformation" formulas across different load types (axial $\delta=PL/AE$, torsional $\phi=TL/JG$, beam $EIv''=M$) and why this consistent mathematical structure made statically indeterminate problems solvable using the same equilibrium-plus-compatibility method in every case.

Solution

In every load type covered this session, deformation is directly proportional to the applied load/moment/torque and inversely proportional to a stiffness quantity (the product of a material property and a geometric property: $AE$, $JG$, or $EI$) — this consistent linear relationship between load and deformation (a direct consequence of Hooke's law applying at the material level in every case) is precisely what allows the same general method (write equilibrium with more unknowns than equations, write a compatibility equation matching deformations to the physical constraint, solve simultaneously) to work identically whether the indeterminacy involves axial members (Week 2), torsion shafts (Week 9), or, with appropriately more advanced techniques, indeterminate beams. Recognising this unifying structure is one of the most valuable takeaways from the entire subject.
