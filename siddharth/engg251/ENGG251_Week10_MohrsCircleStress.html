Mohr’s Circle of Stress

Lay of the land — this block's toolkit
  • Transformation equations convert stress components between any two orientations at the same point.
  • Principal stresses $\sigma_{1,2}=\sigma_{\text{avg}}\pm R$ occur where shear is zero; max shear $\tau_{\max}=R$ occurs $45\,{}^\circ$ away.
  • Mohr's circle: centre $=\left(\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2,0\right)$, radius $R=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}$; physical $\theta\to$ circle $2\theta$, same direction.
  • Combined loading: compute $\sigma_x$ (bending/axial) and $\tau_{xy}$ (shear/torsion) separately, then combine via Mohr's circle for the true peak stress.
  • Design implication: checking individual stress components alone can miss a higher combined principal stress — the essential setup for Week 13's failure theories.

Understanding Stress Transformation and Mohr’s Circle

A seven-minute visual preview of why the equations trace a circle and why physical angles become $2\theta$ on Mohr’s circle. Watch once, then use Hanson Lesson 50 and the handwritten problems for method.

Open on YouTube ↗

A point in a stressed body generally has normal stresses $\sigma_x,\sigma_y$ and shear stress $\tau_{xy}$ acting on an $x$-$y$ oriented element. Rotating the element by angle $\theta$ (measured counterclockwise from the $x$-axis) reveals different stress values on the rotated faces — the same physical stress state, viewed from a different orientation, given by the stress transformation equations.

$$\sigma_{x'}=\dfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2+\dfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\cos2\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta \qquad \tau_{x'y'}=-\dfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\sin2\theta+\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta$$

This combines the effects of axial stress (Week 1–2), bending stress (Week 4), shear stress (Week 8), and torsion (Week 9) into one general framework — essential wherever a real component experiences more than one type of loading simultaneously (the theme of the final weeks of this subject).

Key idea

$$\sigma_{x'}=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2+\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\cos2\theta+\tau_{xy}\sin2\theta \qquad \tau_{x'y'}=-\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\sin2\theta+\tau_{xy}\cos2\theta$$

Worked example

Example. At a point, $\sigma_x=80$ MPa, $\sigma_y=20$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=30$ MPa. Find $\sigma_{x'}$ and $\tau_{x'y'}$ at $\theta=30\,{}^\circ$.

$\sigma_{x'}=\dfrac{80+20}2+\dfrac{80-20}2\cos60\,{}^\circ+30\sin60\,{}^\circ=50+30(0.5)+30(0.866)=50+15+26.0=91.0$ MPa.

$\tau_{x'y'}=-\dfrac{80-20}2\sin60\,{}^\circ+30\cos60\,{}^\circ=-30(0.866)+30(0.5)=-26.0+15=-11.0$ MPa.

Practice 1

At a point, $\sigma_x=60$ MPa, $\sigma_y=0$, $\tau_{xy}=0$ (uniaxial stress). Find $\sigma_{x'}$ at $\theta=45\,{}^\circ$.

Solution

$\sigma_{x'}=\dfrac{60+0}2+\dfrac{60-0}2\cos90\,{}^\circ+0=30+0=30$ MPa.

Practice 2

For the same uniaxial stress state ($\sigma_x=60$ MPa, $\sigma_y=\tau_{xy}=0$), find $\tau_{x'y'}$ at $\theta=45\,{}^\circ$.

Solution

$\tau_{x'y'}=-\dfrac{60-0}2\sin90\,{}^\circ+0=-30(1)=-30$ MPa (the classic result: uniaxial tension produces maximum shear stress, equal to half the normal stress, on planes at 45°).

Practice 3

At a point, $\sigma_x=0,\sigma_y=0,\tau_{xy}=40$ MPa (pure shear). Find $\sigma_{x'}$ at $\theta=45\,{}^\circ$.

Solution

$\sigma_{x'}=0+0+40\sin90\,{}^\circ=40$ MPa (pure shear at 45° transforms into pure normal stress — the basis for why brittle materials in torsion often fail along 45° helical planes).

Practice 4

At a point, $\sigma_x=50$ MPa, $\sigma_y=-20$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=0$. Find $\sigma_{x'}$ and $\tau_{x'y'}$ at $\theta=0\,{}^\circ$, and verify this simply recovers the original values.

Solution

At $\theta=0$: $\cos0=1,\sin0=0$. $\sigma_{x'}=\dfrac{50-20}2+\dfrac{50+20}2(1)+0=15+35=50$ MPa $=\sigma_x$. ✓ $\tau_{x'y'}=-\dfrac{70}2(0)+0=0=\tau_{xy}$. ✓

Practice 5

At a point, $\sigma_x=40$ MPa, $\sigma_y=10$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=15$ MPa. Find $\sigma_{x'}$ at $\theta=90\,{}^\circ$, and verify it equals $\sigma_y$ (the original y-face stress, since rotating 90° swaps the roles of the x- and y-faces).

Solution

At $\theta=90\,{}^\circ$: $\cos180\,{}^\circ=-1,\sin180\,{}^\circ=0$. $\sigma_{x'}=\dfrac{40+10}2+\dfrac{40-10}2(-1)+15(0)=25-15=10$ MPa $=\sigma_y$. ✓

Practice 6

Explain why $\sigma_x+\sigma_y$ is invariant under rotation (i.e. $\sigma_{x'}+\sigma_{y'}=\sigma_x+\sigma_y$ for any $\theta$), using the transformation equations.

Solution

Since $\sigma_{y'}$ is found by substituting $\theta+90\,{}^\circ$ into the $\sigma_{x'}$ formula, and $\cos(2\theta+180\,{}^\circ)=-\cos2\theta$, the $\cos2\theta$ and $\sin2\theta$ terms exactly cancel when $\sigma_{x'}$ and $\sigma_{y'}$ are added, leaving only $\sigma_{x'}+\sigma_{y'}=\dfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2+\dfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2=\sigma_x+\sigma_y$ — a genuine physical invariant (this sum equals twice the average, or "hydrostatic", stress at the point, which doesn't depend on the arbitrarily chosen orientation of the coordinate axes).

1 Principal Stresses & Maximum Shear Stress

The principal stresses $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ are the maximum and minimum normal stresses at a point, occurring on the principal planes where shear stress is exactly zero: $\sigma_{1,2}=\dfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2\pm\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}$, at orientation angle $\theta_p$ found from $\tan2\theta_p=\dfrac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}$.

The maximum in-plane shear stress occurs on planes rotated exactly $45\,{}^\circ$ from the principal planes: $\tau_{\max}=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}$ (note this is exactly the square-root term from the principal stress formula), with an associated average normal stress $\sigma_{\text{avg}}=\dfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2$ acting on those same planes.

Key idea

$$\sigma_{1,2}=\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2} \qquad \tau_{\max}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2} \qquad \tan2\theta_p=\frac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}$$

Worked example

Example. At a point, $\sigma_x=80$ MPa, $\sigma_y=20$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=30$ MPa (same as Section 0). Find the principal stresses and the maximum in-plane shear stress.

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=\dfrac{80+20}2=50$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{80-20}2\right)^2+30^2}=\sqrt{30^2+30^2}=\sqrt{1800}=42.43$ MPa.

$\sigma_1=50+42.43=92.43$ MPa. $\sigma_2=50-42.43=7.57$ MPa. $\tau_{\max}=42.43$ MPa.

Practice 7

At a point, $\sigma_x=100$ MPa, $\sigma_y=40$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=0$ (no shear, already a principal state). Confirm $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ using the formula.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=70$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{30^2+0}=30$ MPa. $\sigma_1=100$ MPa, $\sigma_2=40$ MPa — correctly recovers the given values, since zero shear means the given axes are already principal.

Practice 8

At a point, $\sigma_x=0,\sigma_y=0,\tau_{xy}=25$ MPa (pure shear). Find the principal stresses.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=0$. Radius $=\sqrt{0+25^2}=25$ MPa. $\sigma_1=25$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-25$ MPa (equal and opposite — the classic pure-shear result, confirming that pure shear is equivalent to equal biaxial tension/compression at 45°).

Practice 9

At a point, $\sigma_x=60$ MPa, $\sigma_y=-10$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=20$ MPa. Find the principal stresses.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=25$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{35^2+20^2}=\sqrt{1625}=40.31$ MPa. $\sigma_1=65.3$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-15.3$ MPa.

Practice 10

For the stress state of the previous question, find the maximum in-plane shear stress.

Solution

$\tau_{\max}=40.31$ MPa (the same radius value computed above).

Practice 11

At a point, $\sigma_x=45$ MPa, $\sigma_y=45$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=15$ MPa (equal biaxial normal stress plus shear). Find the principal stresses.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=45$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{0^2+15^2}=15$ MPa. $\sigma_1=60$ MPa, $\sigma_2=30$ MPa.

Practice 12

Find the orientation angle $\theta_p$ of the principal planes for $\sigma_x=80$ MPa, $\sigma_y=20$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=30$ MPa (same stress state as the worked example).

Solution

$\tan2\theta_p=\dfrac{2(30)}{80-20}=\dfrac{60}{60}=1\Rightarrow2\theta_p=45\,{}^\circ\Rightarrow\theta_p=22.5\,{}^\circ$.

Practice 13

Explain why the maximum in-plane shear stress formula $\tau_{\max}=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}$ is numerically identical to the "radius" term in the principal stress formula, and what this implies about the relationship between the principal-stress planes and the maximum-shear planes.

Solution

Both quantities come from the exact same square-root expression in the transformation equations — the principal stresses are $\sigma_{\text{avg}}\pm(\text{radius})$, while the maximum shear stress is exactly that same radius value. This shared origin reflects that principal planes (zero shear, extreme normal stress) and maximum-shear planes are two different "extremes" of the very same underlying transformation, related geometrically by being oriented exactly 45° apart — a relationship made fully visually explicit by Mohr's circle, covered next.

2 Mohr’s Circle Construction
Jeff Hanson · Book Level 9

Lesson 50 — Mohr’s Circle for Stress Transformation

Hanson works the complete circle: plot the two stress points, find the centre and radius, then read principal and maximum-shear states. Pause before plotting and commit to the ENGG251 shear sign convention first.

Open on YouTube ↗

Mohr's circle is a graphical representation of the stress transformation equations: plot $\sigma$ on the horizontal axis and $\tau$ on the vertical axis. The stress state $(\sigma_x,\tau_{xy})$ and $(\sigma_y,-\tau_{xy})$ define two points that are diametrically opposite on a circle centred at $\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2,0\right)$ with radius $R=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2}$ — exactly the quantities from Section 1.

Key rule: a rotation of $\theta$ on the physical element corresponds to a rotation of $2\theta$ on Mohr's circle, in the same direction. Reading off the circle: principal stresses are where the circle crosses the $\sigma$-axis ($\tau=0$); maximum shear stress is the top/bottom of the circle (at $\sigma=\sigma_{\text{avg}}$); any other orientation's stresses can be read directly from the corresponding point on the circle, at twice the physical rotation angle.

Key idea

$$\text{Centre: } \left(\frac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2,0\right) \qquad \text{Radius: } R=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}2\right)^2+\tau_{xy}^2} \qquad \text{Physical } \theta \leftrightarrow \text{Mohr’s circle } 2\theta \ \text{(same sense)}$$

Worked example

Example. Using the same stress state as before ($\sigma_x=80$ MPa, $\sigma_y=20$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=30$ MPa), describe the Mohr’s circle and locate the principal stress points.

Centre $=(50,0)$ MPa. Radius $=42.43$ MPa (computed in Section 1). The circle spans from $\sigma=50-42.43=7.57$ to $\sigma=50+42.43=92.43$ MPa along the $\tau=0$ axis — these are exactly $\sigma_2$ and $\sigma_1$ found algebraically before.

The point $(\sigma_x,\tau_{xy})=(80,30)$ lies on the circle; rotating from that point to the nearest $\sigma$-axis crossing ($\sigma_1=92.43$ MPa) requires an angle of $2\theta_p=45\,{}^\circ$ on the circle, i.e. $\theta_p=22.5\,{}^\circ$ physically — matching Section 1’s result. ✓

Practice 14

For $\sigma_x=60$ MPa, $\sigma_y=0$, $\tau_{xy}=0$ (uniaxial), find the centre and radius of Mohr's circle.

Solution

Centre $=(30,0)$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{30^2+0}=30$ MPa.

Practice 15

For $\sigma_x=0,\sigma_y=0,\tau_{xy}=25$ MPa (pure shear), find the centre and radius of Mohr's circle, and confirm it matches the principal stresses found earlier ($\pm25$ MPa).

Solution

Centre $=(0,0)$. Radius $=\sqrt{0+25^2}=25$ MPa. Circle spans from $-25$ to $+25$ MPa on the $\sigma$-axis, matching $\sigma_2=-25,\sigma_1=25$ MPa found in Section 1. ✓

Practice 16

A Mohr's circle has centre at $(40,0)$ MPa and radius 25 MPa. Find the principal stresses $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ and the maximum shear stress.

Solution

$\sigma_1=40+25=65$ MPa. $\sigma_2=40-25=15$ MPa. $\tau_{\max}=25$ MPa (the radius itself).

Practice 17

A Mohr's circle has centre $(20,0)$ MPa, radius 35 MPa. If a physical rotation of $\theta=15\,{}^\circ$ is applied, through what angle does the corresponding point move on the circle?

Solution

$2\theta=30\,{}^\circ$ on the circle, in the same rotational sense as the physical rotation.

Practice 18

Explain why the point representing the $y$-face stresses, $(\sigma_y,-\tau_{xy})$, must be diametrically opposite the $x$-face point $(\sigma_x,\tau_{xy})$ on Mohr's circle, referencing the fact that the $x$- and $y$-faces of a stress element are physically $90\,{}^\circ$ apart.

Solution

Since the $x$-face and $y$-face are $90\,{}^\circ$ apart on the physical element, and physical rotations map to double the angle on Mohr's circle, these two faces must be separated by $2(90\,{}^\circ)=180\,{}^\circ$ on the circle — exactly diametrically opposite, which is precisely why the two given stress states can always be used to construct the full circle (a circle is uniquely determined once you know two diametrically opposite points, since together they fix both the centre, at their midpoint, and the radius, as half their separation distance).

Practice 19

For a stress state where $\sigma_x=\sigma_y$ (equal normal stresses) and $\tau_{xy}\ne0$, describe the resulting Mohr's circle's centre location, and explain what this implies about the principal stress orientation ($\theta_p$).

Solution

Centre $=\left(\dfrac{\sigma_x+\sigma_y}2,0\right)=(\sigma_x,0)$ since $\sigma_x=\sigma_y$. From $\tan2\theta_p=\dfrac{2\tau_{xy}}{\sigma_x-\sigma_y}$, the denominator is zero, making $2\theta_p=90\,{}^\circ$ exactly (regardless of $\tau_{xy}$'s value, as long as it's nonzero) — so $\theta_p=45\,{}^\circ$ always, whenever the two normal stresses are equal; this is a useful special case to recognise directly, without needing the full formula.

3 Mohr’s Circle Applications

Mohr's circle becomes especially powerful when a component experiences combined loading — e.g. a shaft carrying both bending (producing $\sigma_x$ via the flexure formula, Week 4) and torsion (producing $\tau_{xy}$ via the torsion formula, Week 9) simultaneously. Compute $\sigma_x$ from bending and $\tau_{xy}$ from torsion separately at the critical point (usually the outer surface), then combine them using the stress transformation/Mohr's circle framework to find the true principal stresses and maximum shear stress at that point.

This combined-loading analysis is the essential bridge to failure theories (Week 13): a component might be perfectly safe under bending stress alone or torsional stress alone, but the combined principal stress or maximum shear stress at a critical point could exceed the material's actual strength — which is exactly why Mohr's circle analysis is a mandatory step before applying any failure criterion to a multi-loaded real-world component.

Key idea

$$\text{Combined loading: find }\sigma_x\text{ (bending/axial), }\tau_{xy}\text{ (shear/torsion) at the critical point} \to \text{Mohr’s circle} \to \sigma_1,\sigma_2,\tau_{\max}$$

Worked example

Example. A solid circular shaft (diameter 50 mm) at its outer surface experiences a bending stress of $\sigma_x=60$ MPa (tension, from a bending moment) and a torsional shear stress of $\tau_{xy}=35$ MPa simultaneously (with $\sigma_y=0$, since there is no stress on the free surface in the other direction). Find the principal stresses.

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=\dfrac{60+0}2=30$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{30^2+35^2}=\sqrt{900+1225}=\sqrt{2125}=46.10$ MPa.

$\sigma_1=30+46.10=76.1$ MPa (tension). $\sigma_2=30-46.10=-16.1$ MPa (compression). $\tau_{\max}=46.10$ MPa — all three exceed either the bending stress or the torsional shear stress alone, illustrating why combined-loading analysis is essential.

Practice 20

A solid shaft, diameter 40 mm, has a bending stress of $\sigma_x=50$ MPa and a torsional shear stress of $\tau_{xy}=30$ MPa at its outer surface ($\sigma_y=0$). Find the principal stresses.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=25$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{25^2+30^2}=\sqrt{1525}=39.05$ MPa. $\sigma_1=64.1$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-14.1$ MPa.

Practice 21

For the same shaft, find the maximum shear stress.

Solution

$\tau_{\max}=39.05$ MPa (the radius).

Practice 22

A shaft carries a bending moment producing $\sigma_x=80$ MPa and a torque producing $\tau_{xy}=45$ MPa at the critical outer-surface point ($\sigma_y=0$). If the allowable principal (tensile) stress is 100 MPa, check whether the design is adequate.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=40$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{40^2+45^2}=\sqrt{3625}=60.21$ MPa. $\sigma_1=100.2$ MPa — marginally exceeds the 100 MPa allowable; the design is (just barely) inadequate and would need a slightly larger shaft diameter.

Practice 23

A pressure vessel wall element experiences $\sigma_x=60$ MPa (hoop stress), $\sigma_y=30$ MPa (axial stress), and $\tau_{xy}=0$ (no shear on this particular element, a common situation for a thin-walled cylindrical vessel away from any discontinuity). Find the maximum in-plane shear stress.

Solution

$\tau_{\max}=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{60-30}2\right)^2+0}=15$ MPa.

Practice 24

A component experiences $\sigma_x=-20$ MPa (compression), $\sigma_y=0$, $\tau_{xy}=25$ MPa. Find the principal stresses and comment on whether the maximum tensile principal stress is larger or smaller in magnitude than the applied compressive stress.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=-10$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{10^2+25^2}=\sqrt{725}=26.93$ MPa. $\sigma_1=16.9$ MPa (tension), $\sigma_2=-36.9$ MPa (compression) — the shear stress has actually introduced a tensile principal stress even though the only applied normal stress was compressive, an important and sometimes counter-intuitive result for combined loading, especially relevant for brittle materials weak in tension.

Practice 25

Explain why analysing only $\sigma_x$ (say, from bending alone) without accounting for the simultaneously present $\tau_{xy}$ (from torsion) could lead to an unsafe design, using the combined-loading worked example from this section as illustration.

Solution

In the worked example, the applied bending stress alone was only 60 MPa, yet the true maximum principal stress once torsion was properly combined via Mohr's circle rose to 76.1 MPa — over 25% higher than the bending stress considered in isolation. A designer checking only the bending stress against an allowable value could therefore significantly underestimate the actual peak stress at that point, potentially certifying a design as safe when it is not; this is precisely why combined-loading (Mohr's circle) analysis is mandatory whenever more than one type of loading acts simultaneously at a critical point.
