0 Beam Types, Loads & Sign Conventions

A beam carries loads primarily transverse (perpendicular) to its axis, resisted by internal shear force $V$ and bending moment $M$. Common types: simply supported (pin + roller), cantilever (fixed at one end, free at the other), overhanging (extends past a support), and beams with internal hinges.

Sign convention (standard, used throughout this subject): $V$ is positive when the internal shear tends to rotate a small beam element clockwise (left face up, right face down); $M$ is positive when it produces concave-up ("sagging", like a smile) bending — top fibres in compression, bottom fibres in tension.

Key idea

$$V>0:\text{ left face up, right face down (clockwise)} \qquad M>0:\text{ sagging (concave up), top in compression}$$

Worked example

Example. A simply supported beam AB (span 6 m) carries a downward point load $P=18$ kN at 2 m from A. Find the reactions first (needed before any internal-force analysis).

$\sum M_A=0$: $-18(2)+B_y(6)=0\Rightarrow B_y=6$ kN. $\sum F_y=0\Rightarrow A_y=12$ kN. (This reaction step precedes every shear/moment calculation in this and the next section.)

Practice 1

Classify each of the following as simply supported, cantilever, or overhanging: (a) fixed at A only, free at B; (b) pin at A, roller at B, load between them; (c) pin at A, roller at B (both interior), beam extends past B.

Solution

(a) Cantilever. (b) Simply supported. (c) Overhanging.

Practice 2

A simply supported beam (span 5 m) carries a point load of 20 kN at 3 m from the left support. Find the reactions.

Solution

$\sum M_A=0$: $-20(3)+B_y(5)=0\Rightarrow B_y=12$ kN. $A_y=8$ kN.

Practice 3

A cantilever beam (length 3 m, fixed at A) carries a uniformly distributed load of 8 kN/m over its full length. Find the reactions at the fixed support.

Solution

Total load $=24$ kN acting at 1.5 m from A. $A_y=24$ kN (up). $M_A=24\times1.5=36$ kN·m.

Practice 4

A beam is loaded such that the internal shear at a section causes the left portion to rotate clockwise relative to the right portion. Is this positive or negative shear by the standard sign convention?

Solution

Positive shear (left face up, right face down is exactly the clockwise-rotation-tendency description).

Practice 5

A beam segment bends so that its top fibres are in tension and bottom fibres in compression (concave down). State whether the bending moment is positive or negative there.

Solution

Negative ("hogging") — the opposite of the standard positive sagging convention.

Practice 6

An overhanging beam has a pin at A (x=0), roller at B (x=8 m), and carries a point load of 15 kN at x=10 m (on the overhang). Find the reactions.

Solution

$\sum M_A=0$: $-15(10)+B_y(8)=0\Rightarrow B_y=18.75$ kN. $\sum F_y=0\Rightarrow A_y=15-18.75=-3.75$ kN (downward).

Practice 7

Explain, using a small free body element cut from a beam, why positive shear on the left face of a cut points upward while positive shear on the right face points downward (for the same internal shear force $V$).

Solution

Cutting a beam exposes two faces at the same section; Newton's third law means the internal shear force acting on the left piece (from the material that was removed) is equal and opposite to the force acting on the right piece. By convention, positive $V$ is drawn as upward on the face whose outward normal points in the negative $x$-direction (the right-hand piece's left-facing cut face) and downward on the face whose outward normal points in the positive $x$-direction (the left-hand piece's right-facing cut face) — together these produce the clockwise "tendency to rotate" that defines positive shear, consistent regardless of which side of the cut you examine.

1 Shear Force & Bending Moment by the Method of Sections

To find $V$ and $M$ at a specific point, cut the beam there, isolate either side as a free body (whichever is simpler), and apply equilibrium to that piece alone, treating $V$ and $M$ at the cut as unknown internal reactions (drawn in their positive sense by convention, so a negative answer just means the true direction is opposite).

Shortcut, cutting from the left: $V(x)=$ (sum of all upward forces to the left of the cut) $-$ (sum of all downward forces to the left). $M(x)=$ (sum of moments of all forces to the left of the cut, taken about the cut point, with upward forces creating positive/sagging moment).

Key idea

$$V(x)=\sum(\text{upward forces left of cut}) \qquad M(x)=\sum(\text{moments about the cut, forces left of cut})$$

Worked example

Example. For the beam of the previous section ($A_y=12$ kN, $B_y=6$ kN, load $P=18$ kN at 2 m from A, span 6 m), find $V$ and $M$ just to the right of the load (at $x=2^+$ m).

Cut just past the load; forces to the left are $A_y=12$ kN (up) and $P=18$ kN (down). $V=12-18=-6$ kN.

$M=A_y(2)-P(0)=12(2)-18(0)=24$ kN·m (moment of $A_y$ about the cut, at 2 m away; $P$ acts right at the cut, zero lever arm).

Practice 8

For a simply supported beam (span 6 m, $A_y=12$ kN, $B_y=6$ kN, point load 18 kN at 2 m from A), find $V$ and $M$ at $x=1$ m (just left of the load).

Solution

Only $A_y$ acts left of the cut: $V=12$ kN. $M=A_y(1)=12$ kN·m.

Practice 9

For the same beam, find $V$ and $M$ at $x=4$ m (between the load and B).

Solution

$V=12-18=-6$ kN. $M=12(4)-18(2)=48-36=12$ kN·m.

Practice 10

A cantilever beam (fixed at A, length 4 m) carries a point load of 10 kN at the free end. Find $V$ and $M$ at $x=2$ m from the fixed end (cut and consider the right-hand free body, which is often simpler for cantilevers).

Solution

Right-hand piece (length 2 m) carries only the 10 kN downward end load. $V=-10$ kN (downward force with nothing above it on this side; by convention using the right piece flips the usual left-side sign logic — direct check: shear is constant $=-10$ kN throughout the span for this loading). $M=-10(2)=-20$ kN·m (hogging, consistent with a cantilever under end load).

Practice 11

A simply supported beam (span 8 m) carries a UDL of 5 kN/m over its full length ($A_y=B_y=20$ kN). Find $V$ and $M$ at $x=3$ m from A.

Solution

$V=A_y-5(3)=20-15=5$ kN. $M=A_y(3)-5(3)(1.5)=60-22.5=37.5$ kN·m (UDL resultant acts at its own centroid, 1.5 m from the cut).

Practice 12

For the same UDL beam (span 8 m, $w=5$ kN/m, $A_y=B_y=20$ kN), find $V$ and $M$ at the midspan, $x=4$ m.

Solution

$V=20-5(4)=0$ kN (zero shear at midspan, by symmetry — matches the location of maximum moment). $M=20(4)-5(4)(2)=80-40=40$ kN·m.

Practice 13

An overhanging beam has $A_y=15$ kN at x=0, $B_y=25$ kN at x=6 m, and a point load of 40 kN at x=8 m (downward, on the overhang, found from equilibrium: check $15+25=40$. ✓). Find $V$ and $M$ at $x=7$ m (on the overhang, between B and the load).

Solution

Forces left of the cut: $A_y=15$ kN up, $B_y=25$ kN up, total $=40$ kN up. $V=40$ kN. $M=15(7)+25(1)=105+25=130$ kN·m.

Practice 14

For a simply supported beam (span 10 m, point load 30 kN at 6 m from A, so $A_y=12$ kN, $B_y=18$ kN by moment balance), verify equilibrium of the whole beam by checking $\sum F_y=0$, then find $M$ at $x=6$ m (directly under the load) using the LEFT free body.

Solution

Check: $12+18-30=0$. ✓ $M(6)=A_y(6)=12(6)=72$ kN·m (using only the reaction, since the load itself acts exactly at the cut with zero lever arm from the left side).

Practice 15

Explain why, when using the method of sections, choosing to analyse the shorter/simpler side of the cut (rather than always defaulting to the left side) reduces the amount of algebra required, using the cantilever example from this section as illustration.

Solution

The method of sections only requires equilibrium of one chosen free body at the cut — either side gives the same correct $V$ and $M$ (Newton's third law guarantees consistency), but the side with fewer applied forces/reactions needs fewer terms summed. For the cantilever example, the free (right-hand) side had just one applied load, versus the fixed (left-hand) side which would have required first calculating the fixed-end reaction and moment before starting — choosing the simpler side saves an unnecessary step.

Rather than computing $V$ and $M$ at isolated points, write $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ as explicit functions valid over each region between load discontinuities (point loads, start/end of distributed loads, applied moments) — then plot these to get the full shear force diagram (SFD) and bending moment diagram (BMD) for the whole beam.

Key features to identify: point loads cause a jump (discontinuity) in the $V$ diagram equal to the load magnitude; distributed loads cause $V$ to vary continuously (linearly for constant $w$); the $M$ diagram is continuous everywhere except at applied point moments (which cause a jump in $M$); $M$ is maximum (or minimum) where $V=0$ (or changes sign).

Key idea

$$V(x),M(x) \text{ written piecewise, one equation per region between load discontinuities} \qquad M_{\max} \text{ occurs where } V=0$$

Write $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ for a cantilever beam (fixed at A, x=0), length $L$, carrying a single downward point load $P$ at the free end $x=L$ (use the right-hand free body for simplicity, valid for $0\le x

Solution

$V(x)=-P$ (constant, throughout). $M(x)=-P(L-x)$ (zero at the free end $x=L$, most negative at the fixed end $x=0$: $M(0)=-PL$).

Practice 17

Write $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ for a simply supported beam, span $L$, carrying a uniformly distributed load $w$ over its full length ($A_y=B_y=wL/2$).

Solution

$V(x)=\dfrac{wL}2-wx$. $M(x)=\dfrac{wL}2x-\dfrac{wx^2}2=\dfrac{wx}2(L-x)$.

Practice 18

For the UDL beam of the previous question, find the location and value of $M_{\max}$.

Solution

$V=0\Rightarrow x=L/2$ (midspan, by symmetry). $M_{\max}=M(L/2)=\dfrac{w(L/2)}2\left(L-\dfrac L2\right)=\dfrac{wL^2}8$ (a standard, frequently memorised result).

Practice 19

A simply supported beam, span 10 m, carries a UDL of 4 kN/m over its full length. Find $M_{\max}$ using the formula from the previous question.

Solution

$M_{\max}=\dfrac{wL^2}8=\dfrac{4(10)^2}8=50$ kN·m.

Practice 20

Solution

$V(x)=P$; $M(x)=Px$.

Practice 21

Continue the previous question: write $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ for the middle region $L/3

Solution

$V(x)=P-P=0$ (constant zero shear in the middle third — a classic "pure bending" region). $M(x)=Px-P(x-L/3)=PL/3$ (constant maximum moment throughout the middle third, since $V=0$ there continuously rather than at a single point).

Practice 22

A cantilever beam (fixed at A, x=0, length $L$) carries a UDL $w$ over its full length. Write $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ (using the right-hand free body of length $L-x$), and find the maximum bending moment.

Solution

$V(x)=-w(L-x)$. $M(x)=-w(L-x)\cdot\dfrac{L-x}2=-\dfrac{w(L-x)^2}2$. Maximum magnitude at the fixed end $x=0$: $M(0)=-\dfrac{wL^2}2$.

Practice 23

Explain why a point load causes a discontinuity (vertical jump) in the shear diagram but the bending moment diagram remains continuous (no jump) at that same point, using the physical meaning of $V$ and $M$ as internal force and internal moment respectively.

Solution

Shear $V$ is a force; a concentrated point load applied at a section directly adds (or subtracts) its full magnitude to the internal shear the instant you cross that point, causing an abrupt jump of exactly that load's magnitude. Bending moment $M$, by contrast, is the integral (accumulated effect) of shear over distance (physically, moment arises from forces acting over some lever arm); a single point load contributes zero additional moment-jump at its own location (zero lever arm to itself), so $M$ changes smoothly (continuously) even though its slope ($dM/dx=V$) jumps abruptly at that same point — the moment diagram has a kink (slope discontinuity) there, not a jump.

3 Relationships Between Load, Shear & Moment

The three quantities are linked by differentiation/integration: $\dfrac{dV}{dx}=-w(x)$ (the slope of the shear diagram equals the negative of the distributed load intensity at that point) and $\dfrac{dM}{dx}=V(x)$ (the slope of the moment diagram equals the shear at that point).

Equivalently, in integral form: $\Delta V=-\displaystyle\int w\,dx=-(\text{area under the load diagram})$, and $\Delta M=\displaystyle\int V\,dx=(\text{area under the shear diagram})$ between any two points. These relationships let you sketch $V$ and $M$ diagrams directly from the load diagram without re-deriving equations at every step — and confirm $M$ is stationary (max/min) exactly where $V=0$, since $dM/dx=V=0$ there.

Key idea

$$\frac{dV}{dx}=-w(x) \qquad \frac{dM}{dx}=V(x) \qquad \Delta V=-\int w\,dx \qquad \Delta M=\int V\,dx=\text{area under }V\text{ diagram}$$

Worked example

Example. A simply supported beam (span 6 m, $A_y=12$ kN, $B_y=6$ kN, point load 18 kN at 2 m from A) has $V=12$ kN for $0

$M(2)=M(0)+(\text{area of }V\text{ from }0\text{ to }2)=0+12(2)=24$ kN·m (matches Section 1’s direct calculation).

$M(6)=M(2)+(\text{area of }V\text{ from }2\text{ to }6)=24+(-6)(4)=24-24=0$ kN·m — correctly returns to zero at the roller support, as it must (no applied moment there). ✓

Practice 24

A beam segment has constant shear $V=8$ kN over a 3 m length, with $M=10$ kN·m at the start of the segment. Find $M$ at the end of the segment using the area-under-$V$ rule.

Solution

$\Delta M=V\times\text{length}=8\times3=24$ kN·m. $M_{\text{end}}=10+24=34$ kN·m.

Practice 25

Over a certain 2 m region, the shear diagram is a straight line decreasing from $V=15$ kN to $V=5$ kN (linearly, due to a UDL). Find $\Delta M$ over this region using the trapezoidal area.

Solution

Area (trapezoid) $=\dfrac{15+5}2\times2=20$ kN·m $=\Delta M$.

Practice 26

A beam has a UDL of $w=6$ kN/m over a certain region. Find the slope of the shear diagram there, and state whether $V$ is increasing or decreasing as $x$ increases (assuming the standard downward-load convention).

Solution

$dV/dx=-w=-6$ kN/m — the shear diagram has a constant negative slope, i.e. $V$ is decreasing as $x$ increases.

Practice 27

If the shear diagram has a slope of $-4$ kN/m over a certain region, what is the distributed load intensity $w$ there (magnitude and typical direction assumption)?

Solution

$dV/dx=-w\Rightarrow-4=-w\Rightarrow w=4$ kN/m (downward distributed load, by the standard sign convention).

Practice 28

A cantilever beam has zero shear over its outer 2 m (free end region, no load there) followed by a UDL region closer to the fixed end. Sketch (describe in words) the general shape of the moment diagram over the zero-shear region.

Solution

Since $dM/dx=V=0$ over that region, the moment diagram is perfectly flat (constant) there — no change in bending moment across the zero-shear zone, consistent with the "pure bending" region result seen in the two-point-load example of the previous section.

Practice 29

Using $dV/dx=-w(x)$, explain why a shear diagram is a straight (sloped) line under a uniformly distributed load, but a curved (parabolic) line under a linearly varying distributed load (e.g. a triangular load).

Solution

For constant $w$, $dV/dx=-w=\text{constant}$, so $V(x)$ is linear (constant slope) — a straight line. For a linearly varying $w(x)=kx$ (triangular load), $dV/dx=-kx$ is itself linear in $x$, so integrating gives $V(x)$ as a quadratic function of $x$ — a parabola. The degree of the shear-diagram curve is always one higher than the degree of the load intensity function, since $V$ is the (negative) integral of $w$.

Practice 30

A simply supported beam carries a UDL $w$ over its full span $L$. Using $dM/dx=V(x)=\frac{wL}2-wx$ (from Section 2), verify by direct integration that $M(x)=\frac{wL}2x-\frac{wx^2}2$, matching the earlier result.

Solution

$\int\left(\frac{wL}2-wx\right)dx=\frac{wL}2x-\frac{wx^2}2+C$; with $M(0)=0$ (no moment at a simple support), $C=0$, exactly matching the Section 2 result. ✓

Practice 31

Explain, using $dM/dx=V$, why the bending moment is always at a local maximum or minimum exactly where the shear force crosses zero (changes sign), and why this rule needs a slight modification at a point where $V$ jumps discontinuously through zero (e.g. under a point load) versus where it passes through zero continuously (e.g. under a UDL).

Solution

Since $dM/dx=V$, a stationary point of $M$ (where its slope is zero) occurs precisely where $V=0$ — standard calculus. When $V$ passes through zero continuously (as under a UDL), this identifies a single clean interior point of maximum moment. When $V$ instead jumps discontinuously across zero (as it might at a point load, going from positive to negative without passing smoothly through zero), the maximum moment still occurs at that same location (since $M$'s slope changes sign there, from positive just before to negative just after), but it corresponds to a kink in the $M$ diagram rather than a smooth turning point — the rule "$M$ extremum where $V=0$" should be understood as "$M$ extremum where $V$ changes sign," which covers both the continuous-crossing and discontinuous-jump cases.
