0 Stress-Strain Relationship & Hooke’s Law

Understanding Young’s Modulus

A concise visual explanation of the stress–strain curve and why its elastic slope is $E$. Use it as the preview, then calculate the worked example and Practice 1 below without replaying it.

Open on YouTube ↗

Normal strain $\varepsilon=\dfrac{\delta}{L}$ is the dimensionless ratio of elongation $\delta$ to original length $L$. Plotting stress $\sigma$ against strain $\varepsilon$ for a tension test produces the stress-strain diagram, the single most important experimental characterisation of a material.

In the initial linear-elastic region, stress is directly proportional to strain: $\sigma=E\varepsilon$ (Hooke's law), where $E$ is the modulus of elasticity (Young's modulus), a material property (steel: $\approx200$ GPa; aluminium: $\approx70$ GPa). Beyond the proportional limit, the curve departs from linear; beyond the yield point $\sigma_Y$, permanent (plastic) deformation remains after unloading.

Key idea

$$\varepsilon=\frac{\delta}{L} \qquad \sigma=E\varepsilon \quad \text{(Hooke's law, linear-elastic region)} \qquad E_{\text{steel}}\approx200\ \text{GPa},\ E_{\text{Al}}\approx70\ \text{GPa}$$

Worked example

Example. A steel rod ($E=200$ GPa), original length 2 m, stretches 0.6 mm under load. Find the strain and the stress (assuming linear-elastic behaviour).

$\varepsilon=\dfrac{0.6\times10^{-3}}{2}=3\times10^{-4}$ (dimensionless). $\sigma=E\varepsilon=200\times10^9\times3\times10^{-4}=60\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=60$ MPa.

Practice 1

A bar of length 3 m elongates 1.5 mm under load. Find the normal strain.

Solution

$\varepsilon=1.5\times10^{-3}/3=5\times10^{-4}$.

Practice 2

An aluminium rod ($E=70$ GPa) is subjected to a strain of $4\times10^{-4}$. Find the resulting stress.

Solution

$\sigma=70\times10^9\times4\times10^{-4}=28\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=28$ MPa.

Practice 3

A steel wire ($E=200$ GPa), length 5 m, is stressed to 100 MPa. Find the elongation.

Solution

$\varepsilon=\sigma/E=100\times10^6/200\times10^9=5\times10^{-4}$. $\delta=\varepsilon L=5\times10^{-4}\times5=2.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}=2.5$ mm.

Practice 4

A brass rod ($E=100$ GPa) of diameter 20 mm carries an axial load of 40 kN. If the original length is 1.5 m, find the elongation.

Solution

$A=\pi(0.01)^2=3.14\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $\sigma=40000/3.14\times10^{-4}=127.3$ MPa. $\varepsilon=127.3\times10^6/100\times10^9=1.273\times10^{-3}$. $\delta=1.273\times10^{-3}\times1.5=1.91$ mm.

Practice 5

A steel specimen yields at $\sigma_Y=250$ MPa. Find the strain at the onset of yielding, assuming linear-elastic behaviour up to that point ($E=200$ GPa).

Solution

$\varepsilon_Y=250\times10^6/200\times10^9=1.25\times10^{-3}$.

Practice 6

Two rods of the same material and length but different cross-sectional areas ($A_1=2A_2$) carry the same axial load $P$. Compare their elongations.

Solution

$\delta=\dfrac{PL}{AE}$ (from $\delta=\varepsilon L=\frac\sigma EL=\frac{P}{AE}L$), so $\delta\propto1/A$: rod 2 (smaller area) elongates twice as much as rod 1.

Practice 7

A tensile test on a 12 mm diameter, 50 mm gauge-length specimen records a load of 15 kN and an elongation of 0.05 mm at that load. Find the stress, strain, and the modulus of elasticity (assuming linear-elastic behaviour).

Solution

$A=113.1\ \text{mm}^2$. $\sigma=15000/113.1\times10^{-6}=132.6$ MPa. $\varepsilon=0.05/50=1\times10^{-3}$. $E=\sigma/\varepsilon=132.6\ \text{MPa}/10^{-3}=132.6$ GPa.

Practice 8

Explain, in terms of the stress-strain diagram, the practical difference between the proportional limit and the yield point, and why engineers often use a 0.2% offset method for materials without a sharply defined yield point.

Solution

The proportional limit is the stress at which the curve first departs from a straight line (Hooke's law starts to fail slightly); the yield point is the stress at which significant, largely irreversible plastic deformation begins. For many materials (e.g. aluminium alloys) these are close but the transition is gradual rather than a sharp kink, making the true yield point ambiguous to read directly. The 0.2% offset method draws a line parallel to the linear-elastic slope, offset by a strain of 0.002, and defines the yield stress as where this offset line intersects the actual stress-strain curve — a consistent, repeatable convention despite the gradual transition.

1 Elongation of Axially Loaded Members
Jeff Hanson · Book Level 4

Lesson 17 — Axial Elongation Due to Axial Load

Open on YouTube ↗

Combining $\sigma=P/A$ and $\sigma=E\varepsilon$ with $\varepsilon=\delta/L$ gives the fundamental axial deformation formula: $\delta=\dfrac{PL}{AE}$, valid for a prismatic (constant cross-section) member with constant axial force $P$, within the linear-elastic range.

For a member made of several segments (different $P$, $A$, or $E$ in each), total elongation is the algebraic sum: $\delta=\displaystyle\sum_i\dfrac{P_iL_i}{A_iE_i}$ (sign convention: tension positive, compression negative). For continuously varying load or area, use $\delta=\displaystyle\int_0^L\dfrac{P(x)}{A(x)E}\,dx$.

Key idea

$$\delta=\frac{PL}{AE} \qquad \delta_{\text{total}}=\sum_i\frac{P_iL_i}{A_iE_i} \qquad \delta=\int_0^L\frac{P(x)}{A(x)E}\,dx \quad \text{(varying section)}$$

Worked example

Example. A stepped steel bar ($E=200$ GPa) has segment AB (length 1 m, area 500 mm$^2$) carrying 40 kN tension, and segment BC (length 0.8 m, area 300 mm$^2$) carrying 40 kN tension (same load, in series). Find the total elongation.

$\delta_{AB}=\dfrac{40000\times1}{500\times10^{-6}\times200\times10^9}=4\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}=0.4$ mm.

$\delta_{BC}=\dfrac{40000\times0.8}{300\times10^{-6}\times200\times10^9}=5.33\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}=0.533$ mm. Total: $\delta=0.4+0.533=0.933$ mm.

Practice 9

A steel bar ($E=200$ GPa), length 2 m, cross-sectional area 400 mm$^2$, carries an axial tensile load of 60 kN. Find the elongation.

Solution

$\delta=\dfrac{60000\times2}{400\times10^{-6}\times200\times10^9}=1.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}=1.5$ mm.

Practice 10

An aluminium rod ($E=70$ GPa), length 1.2 m, diameter 25 mm, carries a compressive load of 30 kN. Find the shortening.

Solution

$A=490.9\ \text{mm}^2$. $\delta=\dfrac{30000\times1.2}{490.9\times10^{-6}\times70\times10^9}=1.048\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}=1.05$ mm (shortening).

Practice 11

A composite bar has segment 1 (steel, $E=200$ GPa, $L=1$ m, $A=600\ \text{mm}^2$) and segment 2 (brass, $E=100$ GPa, $L=0.5$ m, $A=600\ \text{mm}^2$), both carrying the same tensile load of 50 kN. Find the total elongation.

Solution

$\delta_1=\dfrac{50000\times1}{600\times10^{-6}\times200\times10^9}=4.17\times10^{-4}$ m. $\delta_2=\dfrac{50000\times0.5}{600\times10^{-6}\times100\times10^9}=4.17\times10^{-4}$ m. Total $=8.33\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}=0.833$ mm.

Practice 12

A bar carries a tensile load of 20 kN over the first metre and a tensile load of 35 kN over the next 1.5 m (constant area 500 mm$^2$, steel $E=200$ GPa). Find the total elongation.

Solution

$\delta_1=\dfrac{20000\times1}{500\times10^{-6}\times200\times10^9}=2\times10^{-4}$ m. $\delta_2=\dfrac{35000\times1.5}{500\times10^{-6}\times200\times10^9}=5.25\times10^{-4}$ m. Total $=7.25\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}=0.725$ mm.

Practice 13

A stepped bar has segment AB (tension 30 kN, $L=0.6$ m, $A=400\ \text{mm}^2$) and segment BC (compression 30 kN, $L=0.4$ m, $A=400\ \text{mm}^2$), same material, $E=200$ GPa. Find the net elongation of AC.

Solution

$\delta_{AB}=+\dfrac{30000\times0.6}{400\times10^{-6}\times200\times10^9}=+2.25\times10^{-4}$ m. $\delta_{BC}=-\dfrac{30000\times0.4}{400\times10^{-6}\times200\times10^9}=-1.5\times10^{-4}$ m. Net $=0.75\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}=0.075$ mm (net elongation).

Practice 14

A vertical hanging bar of length $L$, cross-sectional area $A$, and unit weight $\gamma$ per unit volume elongates under its own weight. Set up the integral $\delta=\int_0^L\dfrac{P(x)}{AE}dx$ where $P(x)$ is the weight of material below the point at distance $x$ from the top, and evaluate.

Solution

$P(x)=\gamma A(L-x)$ (weight of the portion below). $\delta=\int_0^L\dfrac{\gamma A(L-x)}{AE}dx=\dfrac{\gamma}{E}\int_0^L(L-x)dx=\dfrac{\gamma}{E}\cdot\dfrac{L^2}2=\dfrac{\gamma L^2}{2E}$ — equivalent to half the weight applied at the full length, $\dfrac{(\gamma AL)L}{2AE}$.

Practice 15

A tapered bar has area varying linearly from $A_0$ at $x=0$ to $2A_0$ at $x=L$, carrying constant tensile load $P$. Set up (do not evaluate) the integral for total elongation.

Solution

$A(x)=A_0\left(1+\dfrac{x}{L}\right)$. $\delta=\displaystyle\int_0^L\dfrac{P}{A_0(1+x/L)E}dx$ (evaluates via the natural log, a standard tapered-bar result).

Practice 16

Explain why the formula $\delta=PL/(AE)$ cannot be applied directly to a bar with continuously varying cross-sectional area without first setting up an integral, using the concept of strain varying along the bar's length.

Solution

The simple formula $\delta=PL/(AE)$ assumes a constant stress (and hence constant strain) along the entire length, valid only when $A$ (and $P$) don't change over the member. If $A$ varies with $x$, the stress $\sigma(x)=P/A(x)$ and hence the strain $\varepsilon(x)=\sigma(x)/E$ also vary along the length, so the total elongation must be found by summing (integrating) the infinitesimal elongations $\varepsilon(x)\,dx$ over each thin slice, rather than using a single average value across the whole length.

2 Statically Indeterminate Axial Members

When the number of unknown reactions/internal forces exceeds the number of independent equilibrium equations, the structure is statically indeterminate — equilibrium alone cannot solve it. The extra equation(s) needed come from compatibility (geometric constraints on how the parts must deform together, e.g. "total elongation of the assembly must equal zero if both ends are fixed").

General method: (1) write equilibrium equation(s) with more unknowns than equations, (2) write a compatibility equation relating the deformations (using $\delta=PL/AE$ for each part), (3) solve the two sets of equations simultaneously for the unknown forces.

Key idea

$$\text{Equilibrium (not enough alone)} + \text{Compatibility } (\delta_1+\delta_2=\text{constraint}) \implies \text{solve simultaneously}$$

Worked example

Example. A rod is fixed at both ends (A and C) with a point load $P=60$ kN applied at B, where AB $=0.4$ m and BC $=0.6$ m (same material and area throughout, $E,A$ constant). Find the reactions at A and C.

Equilibrium: $R_A+R_C=P=60$ kN (one equation, two unknowns — indeterminate).

Compatibility: total elongation AC $=0$ (both ends fixed). Taking $R_A$ as tension in AB, $R_C$ as tension in BC (with $R_C=P-R_A$, and force in BC $=-R_C$ acting from B towards C... using standard indeterminate-bar method): $\dfrac{R_A(0.4)}{AE}=\dfrac{R_C(0.6)}{AE}\Rightarrow0.4R_A=0.6R_C$. With $R_A+R_C=60$: $R_A=36$ kN, $R_C=24$ kN.

Practice 17

A rod fixed at both ends, length 1 m, carries a point load $P=100$ kN at the midpoint (uniform $A,E$). Find the reactions at each end.

Solution

By symmetry (equal segment lengths), $R_A=R_C=50$ kN.

Practice 18

A rod fixed at both ends has a point load $P=90$ kN applied at a point dividing the rod into segments of 0.3 m and 0.7 m (uniform $A,E$). Find the reaction at the end nearer the load.

Solution

Compatibility gives $R_{\text{near}}\cdot0.3=R_{\text{far}}\cdot0.7$ (elongation of each segment equal and opposite), with $R_{\text{near}}+R_{\text{far}}=90$. Solving: $R_{\text{near}}=63$ kN (the reaction at the shorter segment's end is larger, since it deforms less for the same force, carrying proportionally more load).

Practice 19

Two rods (both fixed at their outer ends, connected at a common point B where a load $P=50$ kN is applied) have equal lengths but different areas: rod 1 has area $2A_0$, rod 2 has area $A_0$ (same material). Find the fraction of load carried by rod 1.

Solution

Compatibility (equal elongation, equal length, same $E$) requires equal stress-times-nothing... actually for equal $\delta=PL/(AE)$ with equal $L,E$: $P_1/A_1=P_2/A_2\Rightarrow P_1=2P_2$. With $P_1+P_2=50$: $P_1=33.3$ kN (rod 1, the stiffer/larger one, carries twice the load of rod 2).

Practice 20

A steel bolt passes through a copper tube; both are compressed between rigid washers by tightening the nut, inducing an initial force. If the bolt area is $A_b$, tube area $A_t$, both length $L$: state the compatibility condition relating bolt elongation and tube shortening.

Solution

Since the assembly is rigid at both ends (washers don't separate), the bolt's elongation must equal the tube's shortening in magnitude: $\delta_{\text{bolt}}=\delta_{\text{tube}}$, i.e. $\dfrac{P_bL}{A_bE_b}=\dfrac{P_tL}{A_tE_t}$, combined with equilibrium $P_b=P_t$ (equal and opposite internal forces, since no external load is yet applied — this is the pretensioning condition).

Practice 21

A rigid bar is supported by two vertical rods (rod 1 and rod 2, both length $L$, same material $E$, areas $A_1=A_0$ and $A_2=3A_0$) hanging from a fixed ceiling, and carries a load such that both rods stretch by the same amount (rigid bar remains horizontal). Find the ratio $P_1:P_2$.

Solution

Equal elongation $\delta_1=\delta_2$ with equal $L,E$: $P_1/A_1=P_2/A_2\Rightarrow P_1/A_0=P_2/(3A_0)\Rightarrow P_2=3P_1$. Ratio $P_1:P_2=1:3$.

Practice 22

A composite column consists of a steel core ($E_s=200$ GPa, area $A_s$) surrounded by a concrete casing ($E_c=25$ GPa, area $A_c$), both the same length, compressed together by a total load $P$ (rigid end plates ensure equal shortening). Derive the ratio $P_s/P_c$ in terms of the areas and moduli.

Solution

Equal shortening $\delta_s=\delta_c$ with equal $L$: $\dfrac{P_s}{A_sE_s}=\dfrac{P_c}{A_cE_c}\Rightarrow\dfrac{P_s}{P_c}=\dfrac{A_sE_s}{A_cE_c}$ — the stiffer material (steel, much higher $E$) carries a disproportionately large share of the total load relative to its area, the fundamental principle behind reinforced-concrete/composite column design.

Practice 23

Explain, conceptually, why in a statically indeterminate axial assembly the stiffer member always attracts a larger share of the total applied load, even if the members have equal cross-sectional area.

Solution

In an indeterminate assembly, compatibility forces all connected members to deform by the same (or a related) amount. Since $\delta=PL/(AE)$, for a given elongation $\delta$, a stiffer member (larger $E$, or larger $A$) requires a larger force $P$ to produce that same deformation. So members with greater stiffness naturally end up carrying more of the total load, purely as a geometric consequence of being forced to deform together — independent of any "choice" about how load is distributed.

3 Thermal Effects & Thermal Stress

A temperature change $\Delta T$ causes a free (unrestrained) member to expand or contract by $\delta_T=\alpha L\Delta T$, where $\alpha$ is the material's coefficient of thermal expansion (steel: $\approx12\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$; aluminium: $\approx23\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$). This produces strain but no stress, since the member is free to move.

If the member is restrained (fully or partially prevented from expanding/contracting, e.g. fixed at both ends), the restraint develops an internal force that would produce an elongation exactly cancelling the free thermal expansion — this is a compatibility problem exactly like the indeterminate case: $\delta_{\text{mechanical}}+\delta_{\text{thermal}}=\text{total allowed movement (often }0\text{)}$, giving thermal stress $\sigma_T=E\alpha\Delta T$ for a bar fully restrained against any length change.

Key idea

$$\delta_T=\alpha L\,\Delta T \quad \text{(free expansion, no stress)} \qquad \sigma_T=E\alpha\,\Delta T \quad \text{(fully restrained, no length change allowed)}$$

Worked example

Example. A steel rod ($E=200$ GPa, $\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$) is fixed rigidly at both ends and heated by $\Delta T=40\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Find the thermal stress developed.

$\sigma_T=E\alpha\Delta T=200\times10^9\times12\times10^{-6}\times40=96\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=96$ MPa (compressive, since heating a restrained bar induces compression).

Practice 24

A steel rail ($\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$), length 20 m, is free to expand and experiences a temperature rise of 30 °C. Find the free expansion.

Solution

$\delta_T=12\times10^{-6}\times20\times30=7.2\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}=7.2$ mm.

Practice 25

An aluminium rod ($E=70$ GPa, $\alpha=23\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$), fully restrained at both ends, is cooled by 25 °C. Find the thermal stress and its sense (tension/compression).

Solution

$\sigma_T=70\times10^9\times23\times10^{-6}\times25=40.25\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=40.25$ MPa; cooling a restrained bar induces tension (the bar "wants" to shrink but can't).

Practice 26

A steel bar ($E=200$ GPa, $\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$), fully restrained, must not exceed an allowable stress of 120 MPa. Find the maximum permissible temperature change.

Solution

$\Delta T_{\max}=\sigma_{\text{allow}}/(E\alpha)=120\times10^6/(200\times10^9\times12\times10^{-6})=50\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.

Practice 27

A steel rod, length 2 m, area 300 mm$^2$ ($E=200$ GPa, $\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$), is heated by 60 °C while restrained at both ends. Find the compressive force induced.

Solution

$\sigma_T=200\times10^9\times12\times10^{-6}\times60=144\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=144$ MPa. $P=\sigma_TA=144\times10^6\times300\times10^{-6}=43.2$ kN (compressive).

Practice 28

A rod is restrained by a gap of 0.5 mm at one end (i.e. it can expand freely by 0.5 mm before contacting the wall). If the free thermal expansion at the given $\Delta T$ would be 2 mm (rod length 3 m, $E=200$ GPa, $\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$), find the stress induced once contact occurs.

Solution

Only $2-0.5=1.5$ mm of the expansion is restrained (converted to mechanical compression). Equivalent "restrained" strain $=1.5\times10^{-3}/3=5\times10^{-4}$. $\sigma=E\varepsilon=200\times10^9\times5\times10^{-4}=100\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=100$ MPa (compressive).

Practice 29

A bimetallic assembly has a steel rod and an aluminium rod of equal length $L$, connected end-to-end and fixed at both outer ends, both experiencing the same $\Delta T$ rise. Explain (without full derivation) why the two rods do NOT develop the same thermal stress despite the same temperature change and same restraint.

Solution

The two materials have different coefficients of thermal expansion ($\alpha_{\text{steel}}\ne\alpha_{\text{Al}}$) and different moduli of elasticity ($E_{\text{steel}}\ne E_{\text{Al}}$); the compatibility condition (total elongation of the two-segment assembly $=0$) couples their individual mechanical and thermal strains together through a single shared internal force (equilibrium requires equal and opposite forces at the junction), so the resulting stress in each segment depends on both materials' properties jointly, not simply on each material's own $\sigma_T=E\alpha\Delta T$ formula in isolation (that formula only applies to a single-material bar restrained on its own).

Practice 30

A steel bar 4 m long is installed with its ends 3 mm apart from rigid supports (i.e. there's a 3 mm gap at one end). If $E=200$ GPa, $\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\,{}^\circ\text{C}^{-1}$, find the temperature rise required to just close the gap, and the temperature rise beyond that needed to induce a stress of 50 MPa.

Solution

Gap closes when free expansion $=3$ mm: $\Delta T_1=\delta/(\alpha L)=3\times10^{-3}/(12\times10^{-6}\times4)=62.5\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Additional $\Delta T_2$ needed for 50 MPa: $\Delta T_2=\sigma/(E\alpha)=50\times10^6/(200\times10^9\times12\times10^{-6})=20.8\,{}^\circ\text{C}$. Total temperature rise $\approx83.3\,{}^\circ\text{C}$.
