Understanding Buckling

Open on YouTube ↗

A slender column under increasing axial compression eventually becomes unstable and buckles sideways at a critical load $P_{cr}$, well before the material's compressive strength is reached — a fundamentally different failure mode from simple crushing, governed by stiffness and geometry rather than material strength alone.

For an ideal pin-ended column (both ends free to rotate, no moment resistance), solving the governing beam equation with the compressive axial load produces Euler's formula: $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2EI}{L^2}$. Below $P_{cr}$, the column remains straight (stable equilibrium); at $P_{cr}$, it can buckle into a sideways bowed shape at no further increase in load (in idealised theory) — any imperfection or slight load increase beyond $P_{cr}$ leads to rapid, large lateral deflection.

Key idea

$$P_{cr}=\frac{\pi^2EI}{L^2} \qquad \text{(Euler buckling load, ideal pin-ended column)}$$

Worked example

Example. A pin-ended steel column, length 3 m, $E=200$ GPa, $I=8\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$ (about the weaker axis). Find the Euler buckling load.

$P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)(8\times10^{-6})}{3^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2\times1.6\times10^6}{9}=1.754\times10^6\ \text{N}=1754$ kN.

Practice 1

A pin-ended column, length 2.5 m, $E=200$ GPa, $I=5\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$. Find $P_{cr}$.

Solution

$P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)(5\times10^{-6})}{2.5^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2(1\times10^6)}{6.25}=1579$ kN.

Practice 2

A pin-ended aluminium column ($E=70$ GPa), length 4 m, $I=2\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$. Find $P_{cr}$.

Solution

$P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(70\times10^9)(2\times10^{-6})}{4^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2(1.4\times10^5)}{16}=86.4$ kN.

Practice 3

A column has $P_{cr}=500$ kN, $E=200$ GPa, $I=4\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$. Find its length (pin-ended).

Solution

$L=\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi^2EI}{P_{cr}}}=\sqrt{\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)(4\times10^{-6})}{500000}}=\sqrt{15.79}=3.97\ \text{m}$.

Practice 4

Two pin-ended columns of the same material and length have $I_1=2I_2$. Compare their Euler buckling loads.

Solution

Since $P_{cr}\propto I$ (all else equal), $P_{cr,1}=2P_{cr,2}$ — the stiffer column (larger $I$) buckles at twice the load.

Practice 5

A pin-ended column's length is doubled (all else unchanged). By what factor does $P_{cr}$ change?

Solution

$P_{cr}\propto1/L^2$, so doubling $L$ reduces $P_{cr}$ to $1/4$ of its original value — buckling capacity is extremely sensitive to length.

Practice 6

A pin-ended column with a circular cross-section (diameter 60 mm), length 2 m, $E=200$ GPa. Find $P_{cr}$ (recall $I=\pi d^4/64$).

Solution

$I=\dfrac{\pi(0.060)^4}{64}=6.362\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$. $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)(6.362\times10^{-7})}{2^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2(1.272\times10^5)}4=313.9$ kN.

Practice 7

Explain, conceptually, why Euler's formula uses the smaller moment of inertia of an unsymmetric cross-section (e.g. a rectangular column, where $I$ differs about its two principal axes) when predicting the buckling load, rather than the larger one.

Solution

A column will buckle in whichever direction it is least stiff against bending, since that is the path of least resistance to instability; the smaller $I$ corresponds to the weaker bending direction, and Euler's formula (being a stability criterion) always identifies the lowest critical load at which buckling first becomes possible — using the larger $I$ would incorrectly predict a higher, unsafe buckling capacity that the column would never actually reach before buckling about its weaker axis first.

1 Effective Length & End Conditions

Real columns rarely have ideal pin-pin ends. The effective length $L_e=KL$ generalises Euler's formula to any end condition, where $K$ is the effective length factor capturing how restrained the ends are against rotation: $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2EI}{L_e^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2EI}{(KL)^2}$.

Standard theoretical values: pin-pin $K=1$ (baseline case); fixed-free (cantilever column) $K=2$ (weakest, most prone to buckling for a given $L$); fixed-fixed $K=0.5$ (strongest); fixed-pin $K\approx0.7$. Design codes often specify slightly conservative $K$ values above these idealised theoretical minimums to account for real, imperfect end restraint.

Key idea

$$P_{cr}=\frac{\pi^2EI}{(KL)^2} \qquad K_{\text{pin-pin}}=1 \quad K_{\text{fixed-free}}=2 \quad K_{\text{fixed-fixed}}=0.5 \quad K_{\text{fixed-pin}}\approx0.7$$

Worked example

Example. A fixed-free column (cantilever, $K=2$), length 2 m, $E=200$ GPa, $I=3\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$. Find $P_{cr}$ and compare to the same column pin-ended.

$L_e=KL=2(2)=4$ m. $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)(3\times10^{-6})}{4^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2(6\times10^5)}{16}=370.1$ kN.

Pin-ended equivalent ($K=1,L_e=2$ m): $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(6\times10^5)}{4}=1480.4$ kN — four times higher (matches $K^2=4$ ratio), confirming a cantilever column is far more buckling-prone than a pin-ended one of the same actual length.

Practice 8

A fixed-fixed column, length 3 m, $E=200$ GPa, $I=6\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$ ($K=0.5$). Find $P_{cr}$.

Solution

$L_e=0.5(3)=1.5$ m. $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)(6\times10^{-6})}{1.5^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2(1.2\times10^6)}{2.25}=5262$ kN.

Practice 9

A fixed-pin column, length 4 m, $E=200$ GPa, $I=4\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$ ($K=0.7$). Find $P_{cr}$.

Solution

$L_e=0.7(4)=2.8$ m. $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)(4\times10^{-6})}{2.8^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2(8\times10^5)}{7.84}=1007.7$ kN.

Practice 10

A cantilever column (fixed-free, $K=2$), length 2.5 m, $E=70$ GPa (aluminium), $I=1.5\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$. Find $P_{cr}$.

Solution

$L_e=2(2.5)=5$ m. $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(70\times10^9)(1.5\times10^{-6})}{5^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2(1.05\times10^5)}{25}=41.5$ kN.

Practice 11

For the same physical column length $L$ and cross-section, rank the four standard end conditions (pin-pin, fixed-free, fixed-fixed, fixed-pin) from lowest to highest $P_{cr}$.

Solution

Lowest to highest: fixed-free ($K=2$) < pin-pin ($K=1$) < fixed-pin ($K\approx0.7$) < fixed-fixed ($K=0.5$) — since $P_{cr}\propto1/K^2$, smaller $K$ gives higher buckling capacity.

Practice 12

A column originally pin-ended has $P_{cr}=200$ kN. If its end conditions are changed to fixed-fixed (same $L,E,I$), find the new $P_{cr}$.

Solution

$P_{cr}\propto1/K^2$; going from $K=1$ to $K=0.5$ multiplies $P_{cr}$ by $(1/0.5)^2=4$. New $P_{cr}=800$ kN.

Practice 13

A designer needs a fixed-free (cantilever) column to have the same buckling capacity as a pin-ended column of length 3 m (same $E,I$). Find the required length of the cantilever column.

Solution

Equal $P_{cr}$ requires equal $L_e$: for pin-pin, $L_e=3$ m. For fixed-free, $L_e=2L\Rightarrow L=1.5$ m — the cantilever must be half as long to match the pin-ended column's buckling strength.

Practice 14

Explain physically why a fixed-fixed column has a smaller effective length than its actual physical length ($K=0.5<1$), using the shape of its buckled mode.

Solution

A fixed-fixed column, when it buckles, forms an S-shaped (double-curvature) deflected shape with inflection points (points of zero curvature/moment) located a quarter of the way in from each end; the central portion of the column, between these two inflection points, behaves exactly like a pin-ended column of that shorter length (zero moment at each inflection point mimics a pin support there). Since that effective "pin-ended-equivalent" segment is only half the total physical length, the effective length is correspondingly smaller, giving a higher buckling capacity for the same total material length.

2 Slenderness Ratio & Critical Stress

The radius of gyration $r=\sqrt{I/A}$ packages a cross-section's bending stiffness per unit area into a single length-scale. The slenderness ratio $\lambda=\dfrac{L_e}{r}$ is the key parameter distinguishing buckling-governed columns (large $\lambda$, "slender") from crushing-governed columns (small $\lambda$, "short/stocky").

Dividing Euler's formula by area gives the critical (average) stress: $\sigma_{cr}=\dfrac{P_{cr}}{A}=\dfrac{\pi^2E}{(L_e/r)^2}=\dfrac{\pi^2E}{\lambda^2}$. Euler's formula is only valid while $\sigma_{cr}$ remains below the material's proportional limit (elastic range); for stocky columns with small $\lambda$, the predicted $\sigma_{cr}$ would exceed the material's yield strength, meaning the column actually fails by yielding/crushing rather than elastic buckling, and Euler's formula no longer applies.

Key idea

$$r=\sqrt{I/A} \qquad \lambda=\frac{L_e}{r} \qquad \sigma_{cr}=\frac{\pi^2E}{\lambda^2} \quad \text{(valid only while } \sigma_{cr}\le\sigma_{\text{proportional limit}}\text{)}$$

Worked example

Example. A pin-ended column, circular section diameter 40 mm, length 2 m, $E=200$ GPa. Find the slenderness ratio and critical stress; check validity if $\sigma_Y=250$ MPa.

$A=\pi(0.020)^2=1.257\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$; $I=\dfrac{\pi(0.040)^4}{64}=1.257\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$; $r=\sqrt{I/A}=\sqrt{1\times10^{-4}}=0.010$ m (equivalently $r=d/4$ for a solid circle).

$\lambda=L_e/r=2/0.010=200$. $\sigma_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)}{200^2}=49.3\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=49.3$ MPa $<250$ MPa — well below yield, so Euler buckling genuinely governs (elastic buckling is valid here).

Practice 15

A rectangular column cross-section, $b=100$ mm, $h=200$ mm. Find the radius of gyration about the weaker axis (using the smaller $I$, i.e. bending about the axis where $h=200$ mm is the width dimension for that bending direction — use $I_{\min}=hb^3/12$).

Solution

$I_{\min}=\dfrac{0.200(0.100)^3}{12}=1.667\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$. $A=0.020\ \text{m}^2$. $r_{\min}=\sqrt{1.667\times10^{-5}/0.020}=0.02887\ \text{m}=28.9$ mm.

Practice 16

A pin-ended column, length 5 m, has $r_{\min}=40$ mm. Find its slenderness ratio.

Solution

$\lambda=L_e/r=5/0.040=125$.

Practice 17

Find the critical stress for a pin-ended steel column ($E=200$ GPa) with slenderness ratio $\lambda=150$.

Solution

$\sigma_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)}{150^2}=87.7\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=87.7$ MPa.

Practice 18

A steel column ($E=200$ GPa, $\sigma_Y=250$ MPa) has slenderness ratio $\lambda=80$. Check whether Euler's formula is valid (i.e. whether $\sigma_{cr}<\sigma_Y$).

Solution

$\sigma_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)}{80^2}=308.4\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=308.4$ MPa $>250$ MPa (yield). Euler's formula is NOT valid here — the column would yield before elastic buckling occurs; this is a "short/intermediate" column governed by material strength, not Euler theory.

Practice 19

Find the slenderness ratio at which a steel column ($E=200$ GPa) has $\sigma_{cr}$ exactly equal to $\sigma_Y=250$ MPa (the boundary between "Euler-valid" and "not valid").

Solution

$250\times10^6=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)}{\lambda^2}\Rightarrow\lambda^2=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)}{250\times10^6}=7896\Rightarrow\lambda=88.9$ — columns with $\lambda>88.9$ are genuinely governed by Euler buckling for this steel; columns with $\lambda<88.9$ would yield first.

Practice 20

A pin-ended column, circular section diameter 30 mm, length 1.5 m, $E=200$ GPa, $\sigma_Y=300$ MPa. Find $\lambda$, $\sigma_{cr}$, and determine whether Euler's formula governs.

Solution

$r=d/4=0.0075$ m. $\lambda=1.5/0.0075=200$. $\sigma_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2(200\times10^9)}{200^2}=49.3$ MPa $\ll300$ MPa. Euler's formula governs (very slender column, elastic buckling well before yielding).

Practice 21

Explain why the concept of slenderness ratio, rather than length alone, is the correct parameter for classifying a column as "slender" (buckling-prone) versus "stocky" (crushing-prone), using the definition $\lambda=L_e/r$.

Solution

Two columns of the identical physical length can behave completely differently depending on their cross-sectional shape: a long, thin column (small $r$, since $I$ is small relative to $A$) is far more prone to buckling than a short, stout column of the same length but a much larger $r$ (bulkier cross-section). By normalising length against $r$ (a measure of the section's own resistance to bending), the slenderness ratio $\lambda$ captures the true geometric tendency toward buckling instability independent of absolute size, making it the physically meaningful classification parameter rather than raw length.

3 Column Design Considerations

Practical column design applies a factor of safety to the critical load: $P_{\text{allow}}=\dfrac{P_{cr}}{FS}$, with $FS$ typically in the range 1.5–3.5 for columns (often higher than for simple tension members, reflecting the sensitivity of buckling to imperfections, eccentric loading, and initial crookedness not captured by the idealised Euler model).

Real columns deviate from Euler's idealised prediction at low slenderness ratios (as seen in Section 2) — design codes therefore use empirical column formulas (e.g. parabolic/Johnson formulas for intermediate columns, straight-line formulas for very short columns) that transition smoothly from the yield-governed regime at small $\lambda$ to the Euler-governed regime at large $\lambda$, rather than relying on Euler's formula (or a simple yield check) alone across the whole range.

Key idea

$$P_{\text{allow}}=\frac{P_{cr}}{FS} \qquad \text{Short columns: yield/crushing governs} \qquad \text{Long/slender columns: Euler buckling governs}$$

Worked example

Example. A pin-ended column has $P_{cr}=800$ kN (Euler). Using $FS=2.5$, find the allowable load, and the resulting allowable axial stress if $A=5\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$.

$P_{\text{allow}}=800/2.5=320$ kN. $\sigma_{\text{allow}}=320000/5\times10^{-3}=64\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=64$ MPa.

Practice 22

A pin-ended column has $P_{cr}=1200$ kN. Using $FS=3$, find the allowable load.

Solution

$P_{\text{allow}}=1200/3=400$ kN.

Practice 23

A column must safely carry an actual load of 150 kN using $FS=2.5$. Find the required Euler critical load $P_{cr}$.

Solution

$P_{cr}=FS\times P_{\text{actual}}=2.5\times150=375$ kN.

Practice 24

A pin-ended steel column, length 3.5 m, $E=200$ GPa, must carry an actual load of 200 kN with $FS=3$. Find the required $I$.

Solution

$P_{cr,\text{req}}=3\times200=600$ kN. $I=\dfrac{P_{cr}L^2}{\pi^2E}=\dfrac{600000(3.5)^2}{\pi^2(200\times10^9)}=\dfrac{7.35\times10^6}{1.974\times10^{12}}=3.723\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$.

Practice 25

A fixed-free (cantilever) column, length 2 m, $E=200$ GPa, must carry 50 kN with $FS=3$. Find the required $I$ (recall $K=2$ for fixed-free).

Solution

$P_{cr,\text{req}}=150$ kN. $L_e=2(2)=4$ m. $I=\dfrac{P_{cr}L_e^2}{\pi^2E}=\dfrac{150000(4)^2}{\pi^2(200\times10^9)}=\dfrac{2.4\times10^6}{1.974\times10^{12}}=1.216\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$.

Practice 26

Explain why column design typically uses a higher factor of safety than tension-member design for the same material, even though both might use the same nominal strength basis.

Solution

Tension-member failure (yielding/fracture) is a comparatively well-predicted, controlled failure mode with limited sensitivity to small imperfections. Column buckling, by contrast, is highly sensitive to factors the idealised Euler formula does not capture — initial crookedness, load eccentricity, imperfect end restraint, and residual stresses — all of which can significantly reduce the actual buckling capacity below the theoretical prediction. The larger safety factor compensates for this greater real-world uncertainty and the potentially sudden, catastrophic nature of buckling failure.

Practice 27

A column design table gives an allowable stress of 90 MPa for a certain slenderness ratio and material. If the column's actual cross-sectional area is $4\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$, find the maximum allowable axial load.

Solution

$P_{\text{allow}}=\sigma_{\text{allow}}A=90\times10^6\times4\times10^{-3}=360$ kN.

Practice 28

Explain, in general terms, why empirical column formulas (rather than Euler's formula alone) are used for design across the full practical range of slenderness ratios, referencing the earlier finding that Euler's formula overestimates capacity for short/stocky columns.

Solution

As shown in Section 2, Euler's formula predicts a critical stress that can exceed the material's actual yield strength for columns with low slenderness ratios (short, stocky columns) — an unphysical result, since the column would yield (or crush) well before reaching that theoretical elastic-buckling stress. Empirical formulas (such as parabolic/Johnson-type formulas) are calibrated to blend smoothly between a yield-strength-governed prediction at low $\lambda$ and the genuine Euler prediction at high $\lambda$, giving realistic, safe capacity estimates across the entire practical range of column slenderness encountered in real structures, rather than relying on a formula that is only valid in the slender-column regime.
