0 Pure Bending & the Flexure Formula

When a beam segment is subjected to a constant bending moment $M$ with zero shear (pure bending), plane cross-sections remain plane and rotate about a neutral axis (a line of zero stress and zero strain passing through the centroid of the cross-section, for a linear-elastic, homogeneous material). Longitudinal strain varies linearly with distance $y$ from the neutral axis.

Combining this linear strain variation with Hooke's law gives the flexure formula: $\sigma=-\dfrac{My}{I}$, where $I$ is the second moment of area (moment of inertia) of the cross-section about the neutral axis. The negative sign reflects the standard convention (positive $M$, positive $y$ above the neutral axis $\Rightarrow$ compressive/negative stress there); many texts drop the sign and reason about tension/compression directly from the physical picture instead.

Key idea

$$\sigma=-\frac{My}{I} \qquad \text{(flexure formula, linear-elastic, homogeneous beam, pure bending)}$$

Worked example

Example. A beam cross-section has $I=8\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$ about its neutral axis, and carries a bending moment $M=15$ kN·m (sagging, positive). Find the bending stress at $y=60$ mm above the neutral axis.

$\sigma=-\dfrac{My}{I}=-\dfrac{15000\times0.060}{8\times10^{-6}}=-112.5\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=-112.5$ MPa (compressive, as expected above the neutral axis under sagging/positive moment).

Practice 1

For a beam with $I=5\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$ carrying $M=10$ kN·m (sagging), find the stress at $y=40$ mm above the neutral axis, and state whether it is tension or compression.

Solution

$\sigma=-\dfrac{10000\times0.040}{5\times10^{-6}}=-80\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=-80$ MPa (compression, above the N.A. under sagging).

Practice 2

For the same beam and moment, find the stress at $y=-40$ mm (below the neutral axis).

Solution

$\sigma=-\dfrac{10000\times(-0.040)}{5\times10^{-6}}=+80\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=80$ MPa (tension, below the N.A. under sagging — consistent with the concave-up shape).

Practice 3

A beam ($I=3\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$) carries a hogging moment $M=-8$ kN·m. Find the stress at $y=30$ mm above the neutral axis, and state tension or compression.

Solution

$\sigma=-\dfrac{(-8000)(0.030)}{3\times10^{-6}}=+80\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=80$ MPa (tension — hogging puts the top fibres in tension, opposite to sagging).

Practice 4

Explain, in one or two sentences, why the flexure formula requires the cross-section to be symmetric about the plane of bending (or, more generally, requires bending about a principal axis) for the simple form $\sigma=-My/I$ to apply.

Solution

The derivation assumes bending occurs purely about one axis with no twisting or out-of-plane coupling; if the section is not symmetric about the loading plane, bending about that axis generally induces additional stresses (or the neutral axis is no longer perpendicular to the plane of loading), requiring the more general unsymmetric-bending formula rather than the simple single-axis flexure formula.

Practice 5

A rectangular beam ($I=4\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$, total depth 200 mm so extreme fibre $y=\pm100$ mm) carries $M=20$ kN·m sagging. Find the maximum tensile and maximum compressive stress.

Solution

Extreme fibres at $y=\pm0.100$ m. $\sigma=-\dfrac{20000\times(\pm0.100)}{4\times10^{-6}}=\mp500\times10^6\ \text{Pa}$. Maximum compression $=500$ MPa (top), maximum tension $=500$ MPa (bottom) — equal magnitude since the section is symmetric about the N.A.

Practice 6

A beam with an asymmetric T-section has the neutral axis located 150 mm from the top fibre and 250 mm from the bottom fibre ($I=6\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$). It carries $M=25$ kN·m sagging. Find the maximum tensile and maximum compressive stresses, and explain which is larger.

Solution

Top: $\sigma_{\text{top}}=-\dfrac{25000(0.150)}{6\times10^{-6}}=-625$ MPa (compression, smaller distance). Bottom: $\sigma_{\text{bottom}}=-\dfrac{25000(-0.250)}{6\times10^{-6}}=+1041.7$ MPa (tension, larger distance). The bottom fibre, being farther from the N.A., experiences the larger-magnitude stress — a key reason asymmetric sections are often oriented with the larger material mass toward the side of the larger design stress.

Practice 7

Explain why the neutral axis of a linear-elastic, homogeneous beam always passes through the centroid of the cross-section, referencing the requirement that pure bending involves zero net axial force.

Solution

Under pure bending (no axial load), the internal normal force resultant across the section must be zero: $\int\sigma\,dA=0$. Since $\sigma=-Ey/\rho$ (linear in $y$, from strain compatibility, where $\rho$ is the radius of curvature), this condition becomes $-\dfrac{E}{\rho}\int y\,dA=0$, i.e. $\int y\,dA=0$ — which is precisely the definition of $y$ being measured from the centroid. So the neutral axis must coincide with the centroidal axis for the net force to vanish.

1 Centroid & Moment of Inertia of Common Sections

The centroid locates the neutral axis; for a composite section, find it via $\bar y=\dfrac{\sum A_i\bar y_i}{\sum A_i}$ (areas weighted by their own centroid distances from a convenient reference axis). Standard formulas: rectangle ($b\times h$) about its own centroidal axis: $I=\dfrac{bh^3}{12}$; circle (diameter $d$): $I=\dfrac{\pi d^4}{64}$.

For a composite section, the parallel axis theorem lets you combine sub-shapes: $I=\sum\left(I_i+A_id_i^2\right)$, where $I_i$ is each sub-shape's own centroidal moment of inertia, $A_i$ its area, and $d_i$ the distance from its own centroid to the overall composite centroid.

Key idea

$$\bar y=\frac{\sum A_i\bar y_i}{\sum A_i} \qquad I_{\text{rect}}=\frac{bh^3}{12} \qquad I_{\text{circle}}=\frac{\pi d^4}{64} \qquad I=\sum\left(I_i+A_id_i^2\right) \ \text{(parallel axis theorem)}$$

Worked example

Example. Find $I$ about the centroidal axis for a rectangular section $b=100$ mm, $h=200$ mm.

$I=\dfrac{bh^3}{12}=\dfrac{0.100\times0.200^3}{12}=6.667\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$.

Practice 8

Find $I$ about the centroidal axis for a rectangular section $b=80$ mm, $h=160$ mm.

Solution

$I=\dfrac{0.080\times0.160^3}{12}=2.731\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$.

Practice 9

Find $I$ for a solid circular section of diameter 100 mm.

Solution

$I=\dfrac{\pi(0.100)^4}{64}=4.909\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$.

Practice 10

Find $I$ for a hollow circular tube: outer diameter 120 mm, inner diameter 90 mm.

Solution

$I=\dfrac{\pi}{64}(0.120^4-0.090^4)=\dfrac{\pi}{64}(2.0736-0.6561)\times10^{-4}=6.968\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$.

Practice 11

A T-section is made of two rectangles: flange 150 mm × 20 mm (top) and web 20 mm × 130 mm (below, centred). Taking the bottom of the web as the reference axis, find the centroid location $\bar y$.

Solution

Flange: $A_1=3000\ \text{mm}^2$, $\bar y_1=130+10=140$ mm. Web: $A_2=2600\ \text{mm}^2$, $\bar y_2=65$ mm. $\bar y=\dfrac{3000(140)+2600(65)}{3000+2600}=\dfrac{420000+169000}{5600}=105.2$ mm from the bottom.

Practice 12

For the T-section of the previous question, find the moment of inertia of the flange about its own centroidal axis and its parallel-axis contribution to the composite $I$ (distance from flange centroid to overall centroid: $140-105.2=34.8$ mm).

Solution

$I_{\text{flange,own}}=\dfrac{150(20)^3}{12}=1\times10^5\ \text{mm}^4$. Parallel-axis term: $A_1d_1^2=3000(34.8)^2=3.633\times10^6\ \text{mm}^4$. Total flange contribution $\approx3.733\times10^6\ \text{mm}^4$.

Practice 13

For the same T-section, find the web's own centroidal $I$ and its parallel-axis contribution (distance from web centroid to overall centroid: $105.2-65=40.2$ mm), then sum both parts for the total composite $I$.

Solution

$I_{\text{web,own}}=\dfrac{20(130)^3}{12}=3.66\times10^6\ \text{mm}^4$. Parallel-axis: $A_2d_2^2=2600(40.2)^2=4.202\times10^6\ \text{mm}^4$. Web contribution $\approx7.862\times10^6\ \text{mm}^4$. Total composite $I\approx3.733\times10^6+7.862\times10^6=1.16\times10^7\ \text{mm}^4$.

Practice 14

Explain why the parallel axis theorem always adds a positive quantity ($Ad^2$) rather than subtracting, even when the sub-shape's centroid is "below" the composite centroid rather than "above" it.

Solution

The term $Ad^2$ depends on $d$ only through its square, so the sign (direction) of the offset between the sub-shape's own centroid and the overall composite centroid never matters — moving a shape's centroid away from the reference axis in either direction always increases its contribution to the moment of inertia, which is why the theorem is always additive regardless of whether the shape sits above or below the composite centroid.

2 Bending Stress Distribution & Maximum Stress

Because $\sigma=-My/I$ is linear in $y$, the bending stress distribution across a cross-section is a straight line through zero at the neutral axis, reaching its extreme (maximum magnitude) values at the fibres farthest from the neutral axis — typically the top and bottom of the section.

For beam design, the critical section is where $|M|$ is largest (found from the bending moment diagram, Week 3); the critical stress at that section occurs at the extreme fibre farthest from the N.A. Always check both the maximum tensile and maximum compressive stresses, especially for unsymmetric sections where the two extreme-fibre distances differ (Section 0's T-section-style example).

Key idea

$$\sigma_{\max}=\frac{|M|_{\max}\,c}{I} \qquad c=\text{distance from N.A. to the extreme (farthest) fibre}$$

Worked example

Example. A simply supported beam, span 6 m, rectangular section $b=150$ mm, $h=300$ mm, carries a UDL of $w=10$ kN/m. Find the maximum bending stress.

$M_{\max}=\dfrac{wL^2}8=\dfrac{10(6)^2}8=45$ kN·m (Week 3 result). $I=\dfrac{0.150(0.300)^3}{12}=3.375\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^4$. $c=0.150$ m.

$\sigma_{\max}=\dfrac{45000\times0.150}{3.375\times10^{-4}}=20\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=20$ MPa.

Practice 15

A rectangular beam, $b=100$ mm, $h=250$ mm, carries a maximum bending moment of 30 kN·m. Find the maximum bending stress.

Solution

$I=\dfrac{0.100(0.250)^3}{12}=1.302\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^4$. $c=0.125$ m. $\sigma_{\max}=\dfrac{30000(0.125)}{1.302\times10^{-4}}=28.8$ MPa.

Practice 16

A circular shaft, diameter 80 mm, carries a bending moment of 5 kN·m. Find the maximum bending stress.

Solution

$I=\dfrac{\pi(0.080)^4}{64}=2.011\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$. $c=0.040$ m. $\sigma_{\max}=\dfrac{5000(0.040)}{2.011\times10^{-6}}=99.5$ MPa.

Practice 17

A simply supported beam, span 4 m, carries a central point load of 40 kN. Cross-section: rectangular, $b=120$ mm, $h=200$ mm. Find the maximum bending stress.

Solution

$M_{\max}=PL/4=40(4)/4=40$ kN·m. $I=\dfrac{0.120(0.200)^3}{12}=8\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$. $c=0.100$ m. $\sigma_{\max}=\dfrac{40000(0.100)}{8\times10^{-5}}=50$ MPa.

Practice 18

A cantilever beam, length 3 m, carries a UDL of $w=15$ kN/m. Cross-section: rectangular $b=90$ mm, $h=180$ mm. Find the maximum bending stress.

Solution

$M_{\max}=wL^2/2=15(3)^2/2=67.5$ kN·m (at the fixed end). $I=\dfrac{0.090(0.180)^3}{12}=4.374\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$. $c=0.090$ m. $\sigma_{\max}=\dfrac{67500(0.090)}{4.374\times10^{-5}}=138.9$ MPa.

Practice 19

A beam must carry $M_{\max}=25$ kN·m without exceeding an allowable bending stress of 150 MPa. If the section is rectangular with $h=2b$, find the required dimensions.

Solution

$I=\dfrac{bh^3}{12}=\dfrac{b(2b)^3}{12}=\dfrac{8b^4}{12}=\dfrac{2b^4}3$; $c=h/2=b$. $\sigma_{\max}=\dfrac{Mc}{I}=\dfrac{M\cdot b}{2b^4/3}=\dfrac{3M}{2b^3}\le150\times10^6\Rightarrow b^3\ge\dfrac{3(25000)}{2(150\times10^6)}=2.5\times10^{-4}\Rightarrow b\ge0.0629\ \text{m}=62.9$ mm; $h=125.8$ mm.

Practice 20

A T-section beam (from Section 1: composite $I\approx1.16\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$, N.A. at 105.2 mm from the bottom, overall depth 150 mm so top fibre is 44.8 mm from N.A.) carries a sagging moment of 18 kN·m. Find the maximum tensile stress (bottom) and maximum compressive stress (top).

Solution

Bottom ($c=0.1052$ m, tension): $\sigma=\dfrac{18000(0.1052)}{1.16\times10^{-5}}=163.2$ MPa (tension). Top ($c=0.0448$ m, compression): $\sigma=\dfrac{18000(0.0448)}{1.16\times10^{-5}}=69.5$ MPa (compression) — the tensile fibre, being farther from the N.A., governs the design here.

Practice 21

Explain why, for a beam material with equal tensile and compressive allowable stress (e.g. structural steel), a symmetric section (equal $c$ top and bottom) is the most material-efficient shape, whereas for a material with very different tensile and compressive strength (e.g. cast iron, much weaker in tension), an asymmetric section can be advantageous.

Solution

With equal allowable stress in tension and compression, a symmetric section reaches its allowable stress simultaneously at both the top and bottom fibre for a given moment, using the material as efficiently as possible on both sides. For a material weak in tension, an asymmetric section can be shaped (like a T-section) to keep the neutral axis closer to the tension side (smaller $c$ there) and farther from the compression side (larger $c$ there, where the material is naturally stronger) — matching the section's stress distribution to the material's differing capacities rather than wasting strength.

3 Section Modulus & Beam Design

The section modulus $S=\dfrac{I}{c}$ packages the geometric properties needed for bending-stress design into a single number: $\sigma_{\max}=\dfrac{M_{\max}}{S}$. For symmetric sections there is one value of $S$; for unsymmetric sections there are two values ($S_{\text{top}}=I/c_{\text{top}}$, $S_{\text{bottom}}=I/c_{\text{bottom}}$), and design must satisfy both allowable-stress checks.

Design procedure: (1) find $M_{\max}$ from the bending moment diagram, (2) find the required section modulus $S_{\text{req}}=M_{\max}/\sigma_{\text{allow}}$, (3) select (or size) a cross-section whose actual $S\ge S_{\text{req}}$. Standard steel sections (I-beams, channels) are tabulated with $S$ values precisely so this last step is a lookup rather than a fresh calculation.

Key idea

$$S=\frac{I}{c} \qquad \sigma_{\max}=\frac{M_{\max}}{S} \qquad S_{\text{req}}=\frac{M_{\max}}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}} \quad \text{(design criterion)}$$

Worked example

Example. A simply supported beam, span 5 m, carries a UDL of $w=12$ kN/m. If the allowable bending stress is 165 MPa, find the required section modulus and a suitable rectangular section with $h=2b$.

$M_{\max}=wL^2/8=12(5)^2/8=37.5$ kN·m. $S_{\text{req}}=37500/165\times10^6=2.273\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3$.

For $h=2b$: $S=\dfrac{I}{c}=\dfrac{bh^3/12}{h/2}=\dfrac{bh^2}6=\dfrac{b(2b)^2}6=\dfrac{2b^3}3$. Set $=2.273\times10^{-4}\Rightarrow b^3=3.41\times10^{-4}\Rightarrow b=0.0699\ \text{m}\approx70$ mm; $h\approx140$ mm.

Practice 22

Find the section modulus of a rectangular section $b=100$ mm, $h=250$ mm.

Solution

$I=\dfrac{0.100(0.250)^3}{12}=1.302\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^4$. $c=0.125$ m. $S=1.302\times10^{-4}/0.125=1.042\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^3$.

Practice 23

A beam has $M_{\max}=60$ kN·m and allowable stress 140 MPa. Find the required section modulus.

Solution

$S_{\text{req}}=60000/140\times10^6=4.286\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3$.

Practice 24

A circular shaft must resist $M_{\max}=8$ kN·m without exceeding 100 MPa. Find the required section modulus, and hence the required diameter (using $S=\pi d^3/32$ for a solid circle).

Solution

$S_{\text{req}}=8000/100\times10^6=8\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^3$. $d^3=32S/\pi=32(8\times10^{-5})/\pi=8.15\times10^{-4}\Rightarrow d=0.0935\ \text{m}=93.5$ mm.

Practice 25

A simply supported beam, span 8 m, carries a central point load of 50 kN. Allowable bending stress is 160 MPa. Find the required section modulus.

Solution

$M_{\max}=PL/4=50(8)/4=100$ kN·m. $S_{\text{req}}=100000/160\times10^6=6.25\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3$.

Practice 26

A cantilever beam, length 2.5 m, carries a UDL of $w=20$ kN/m. Allowable stress 175 MPa. Find the required section modulus and a suitable square section ($b=h$).

Solution

$M_{\max}=wL^2/2=20(2.5)^2/2=62.5$ kN·m. $S_{\text{req}}=62500/175\times10^6=3.571\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3$. For a square, $S=b^3/6\Rightarrow b^3=6S=2.143\times10^{-3}\Rightarrow b=0.1288\ \text{m}\approx129$ mm.

Practice 27

A T-section beam has $S_{\text{top}}=2\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3$ (compression side) and $S_{\text{bottom}}=1\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^3$ (tension side), where the allowable compressive stress is 180 MPa and allowable tensile stress is 100 MPa. Find the maximum moment the section can carry (governed by whichever check is more restrictive).

Solution

Compression check: $M\le S_{\text{top}}\sigma_{\text{allow,c}}=2\times10^{-4}(180\times10^6)=36$ kN·m. Tension check: $M\le S_{\text{bottom}}\sigma_{\text{allow,t}}=1\times10^{-4}(100\times10^6)=10$ kN·m. The tension check governs (smaller allowable moment): $M_{\max}=10$ kN·m.

Practice 28

Explain why, for a beam design problem, it is standard practice to check the bending-stress criterion at the section of maximum moment only, rather than at every section along the beam.

Solution

Since $\sigma_{\max}=M(x)/S$ at any given section, and $S$ is typically constant along a prismatic (uniform cross-section) beam, the bending stress is directly proportional to the local moment $M(x)$. The largest stress anywhere along the beam must therefore occur exactly where $|M(x)|$ is largest — so checking (or designing for) the single critical section of maximum moment automatically ensures every other section (with smaller moment, same $S$) is also within the allowable stress, making a check at every section redundant for a prismatic beam.
