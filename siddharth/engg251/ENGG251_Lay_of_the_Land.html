01 The one idea behind the subject

You already know the first half of this subject, from DPEN102: draw a free-body diagram, cut the structure open wherever you want to know what is happening inside it, and use equilibrium to find the forces at that cut. DPEN102 Week 4 even went one step further and introduced stress, strain and internal actions, and Week 5 had you drawing shear force and bending moment diagrams. ENGG251 keeps that exact method and adds one question DPEN102 only opened the door on. DPEN102 stopped once it had the reactions, the axial force in a truss member, or the internal force at a chosen section. Mechanics of solids asks what that internal force actually does to the material it passes through — how it is distributed as a stress across the cross-section, how much the member stretches or bends because of it, and whether the material survives it.

Every method in this subject is the same three-step move, run on a different internal effect each time. Cut the member at the point of interest and use equilibrium to find the internal axial force, shear force, bending moment or torque there. Apply a stress formula that converts that internal force or moment into a distribution of stress across the section — the formula changes ($\sigma=N/A$ for axial force, $\sigma=My/I$ for bending, $\tau=VQ/(Ib)$ for shear, $\tau=T\rho/J$ for torsion) but the move that produced it, assuming the cross-section deforms in a known, simple pattern, is identical every time. Then check the resulting stress against what the material can take, with a factor of safety built in.

The idea in one paragraph

Statics gave you the internal force at a cut and stopped. Mechanics of solids spreads that force out over the area it acts on — a stress — using a formula built from one assumption about how the cross-section deforms, then compares the largest stress produced to the material's yield or ultimate strength, divided by a factor of safety. Everything from Week 2 to Week 13 is that same sentence, said about a different internal force.

02 Five things every structural member does

This is the mental model worth carrying into every week. Any real load-bearing member — a haul truck chassis rail, a conveyor shaft, a mine roof bolt, a pressure vessel — does five things, and ENGG251 gives you one body of technique for each.

One

It stretches or compresses

A straight pull or push produces a uniform stress across the section and a uniform elongation along its length. This is the simplest case, and every later stress formula is compared back to it.

Week 2 · axial load, $\sigma=N/A$
Two

It bends

Shear force and bending moment vary along the length, and bending stress varies linearly through the depth of the section, tension on one face and compression on the other.

Weeks 3–6 · shear, bending, deflection, buckling
Three

It twists

A torque produces a shear stress that varies radially across the section, zero at the centre and maximum at the outer surface — the rotational counterpart of axial load.

Weeks 8–9 · shear flow, torsion
Four

Its stress depends on which way you cut it

The same point in a loaded body shows a different normal and shear stress depending on the orientation of the imaginary cut you take through it, and there is always one orientation where the stress is worst.

Weeks 10–11 · Mohr's circle, principal stresses
Five

It can fail under more than one load at once

Real components rarely see just one type of loading. A pressure vessel wall, or a shaft under bending and torque together, combines every stress into one state before a failure criterion is applied.

Weeks 12–13 · combined loading, failure theories
Why the topics come in this order

Axial load comes first because it is the one case with a uniform, obvious stress distribution, and it is where the $\sigma$–$\varepsilon$ relationship from Week 1 first gets used to compute an actual deformation. Bending is introduced next because it reuses the same free-body method on a less uniform distribution, and deflection and buckling are both consequences of that same bending relationship — one integrated, one made unstable. Torsion mirrors axial load's simplicity but on a shear stress instead of a normal one. Mohr's circle is delayed until Week 10 deliberately: it only becomes useful once you have several different stress formulas whose results need to be combined and compared, which is exactly the position you are in after torsion.

03 Week 1: statics review and the meaning of stress

The week opens with a deliberate recap of the free-body diagrams and equilibrium DPEN102 Weeks 1–3 built, because every stress formula for the rest of the session begins with an internal force found exactly that way: cut the member, isolate one side, and sum forces and moments on the cut piece to find what the removed material was providing. DPEN102 Week 4 already named that internal force and called it an "internal action"; this week gives it its full treatment. That internal force is a single number (or vector); stress is what you get once you ask how that force is shared out over the area it acts across, $\sigma$ or $\tau = \text{force}/\text{area}$, in units of pascals.

The week also separates normal stress $\sigma$ (perpendicular to the cut face, pulling or pushing) from shear stress $\tau$ (parallel to the cut face, sliding), and introduces strain $\varepsilon$ as the fractional change in length a stress produces, related through the material's modulus of elasticity $E$ by Hooke's law, $\sigma=E\varepsilon$. This is the single relationship that turns a stress formula into a deformation formula everywhere else in the subject.

Engineering application
  • Roof bolt and rock bolt load rating (mining). The rated capacity of a bolt securing an underground excavation is a stress limit stated as this week defines it — a force divided by the bolt's cross-sectional area.
  • Wire rope and haulage chain (mining). The safe working load on a hoist rope or dragline chain is set from exactly the stress-versus-area argument introduced here, before any bending or torsion enters the picture.
  • Steel connection design (general). Any bolted or welded steel connection in a building or bridge is first checked against the same normal- and shear-stress definitions, before more specialised formulas are applied.
04 Week 2: allowable stress and axial loads

Axial loading is the case worked in full for the first time: a force $N$ acting along the centroidal axis of a straight member produces a uniform stress $\sigma=N/A$ and, through Hooke's law, a total elongation $\delta=NL/(AE)$. The week introduces allowable stress and the factor of safety, $\sigma_{allow}=\sigma_{yield}/FS$, which is the design check every later stress formula in this subject is ultimately compared against. Statically indeterminate axial systems — more supports or members than equilibrium alone can solve — are also introduced here, using compatibility of deformation (every member's elongation must fit the geometry) as the extra equation equilibrium cannot supply.

Quick example

A 50 mm diameter steel rod carries an axial load of 200 kN. The stress is $\sigma=N/A=200{,}000/(\pi(0.025)^2)\approx102$ MPa. If the steel yields at 250 MPa and a factor of safety of 1.7 is required, the allowable stress is $250/1.7\approx147$ MPa — the rod is adequate, with roughly 30 percent of its capacity in reserve.

Engineering application
  • Shovel and dragline boom tie rods (mining). Tie rods and pendants on mining excavators are sized directly from $\sigma=N/A$ against an allowable stress with a conservative factor of safety.
  • Conveyor gantry hangers (mining and general). Hanger rods supporting an overhead conveyor gantry are a textbook statically indeterminate axial system once more than one hanger shares the load.
  • Anchor bolts and foundation rods (general). Anchor bolts holding down a crusher, mill or any vibrating equipment base are checked with the same axial stress and allowable-stress logic.
05 Weeks 3–4: shear force, bending moment, bending stress

A beam loaded sideways develops an internal shear force $V$ and bending moment $M$ that both vary along its length, found the same way as any internal force: cut the beam at a chosen point and apply equilibrium to one side. Plotting $V(x)$ and $M(x)$ across the whole length produces the shear force and bending moment diagrams that dominate Week 3 — this part is not new material, it is DPEN102 Week 5 revisited, and the key relationships $dV/dx=-w(x)$ and $dM/dx=V(x)$ you use to sketch one diagram from the other are the same ones from that week.

Week 4 is where ENGG251 actually goes further than DPEN102 did: it turns the bending moment into a stress. Assuming plane sections remain plane under bending — the same kind of simplifying assumption that produced $\sigma=N/A$ for axial load — produces the flexure formula, $\sigma=My/I$, where $y$ is the distance from the neutral axis and $I$ is the second moment of area of the cross-section, the quantity DPEN102 Week 8 taught you to compute. Bending stress is zero at the neutral axis, maximum in tension on one face and maximum in compression on the other, which is exactly why beam cross-sections such as an I-beam put most of their material as far from the neutral axis as possible.

Engineering application
  • Truck chassis and axle bending (mining). The bending moment diagram of a loaded haul truck chassis or axle is exactly this week's method, and the flexure formula is what sizes the section against it.
  • Conveyor idler shafts (mining). Idler shafts supporting a loaded conveyor belt are simple beams under distributed load, sized from the same $V$, $M$ and $\sigma=My/I$ sequence.
  • Structural steel beams and girders (general). Every I-beam or girder in a building or bridge is selected using exactly this week's bending moment diagram and flexure formula.
06 Week 5: beam deflection — the elastic curve

Knowing the bending stress does not yet tell you how much a beam sags. The elastic curve equation, $EI\,y''(x)=M(x)$, connects the bending moment diagram from Week 3 directly to the shape of the deflected beam, $y(x)$. Integrating once gives the slope, integrating again gives the deflection, with the two constants of integration fixed by the beam's boundary conditions — zero deflection at a pin support, zero slope at a fixed support, and so on. For beams too complicated to integrate directly, the week also introduces the method of superposition: since the governing equation is linear, the deflection due to several loads acting together is just the sum of the deflections each load would cause acting alone.

Engineering application
  • Conveyor deflection limits (mining). Excess sag in a conveyor structure between supports is a serviceability limit checked directly with this week's elastic-curve equation, separate from the strength check of Week 4.
  • Crane and jib deflection (mining and general). The tip deflection of a loaded jib or crane boom, which affects positioning accuracy, is a superposition problem built from standard beam-deflection cases.
  • Floor and bridge deck serviceability (general). Building codes limit deflection, not just stress, for exactly the reason this week introduces: a beam can be strong enough and still deflect more than is acceptable to use.
07 Week 6: column buckling

Buckling is the elastic curve equation from Week 5 pointed at a new question. A slender member under axial compression does not simply shorten uniformly the way Week 2 assumed — past a critical load it suddenly bows sideways, and the same $EI\,y''=M(x)$ relationship, with the bending moment now caused by the axial load acting on the member's own small sideways deflection, produces the Euler buckling load, $P_{cr}=\dfrac{\pi^2 EI}{(KL)^2}$, where $K$ accounts for how the ends are supported. The slenderness ratio decides whether a compression member fails this way, by elastic instability, well before the material stress from Week 2 would ever reach yield, or whether it is short and stocky enough that the Week 2 stress check governs instead.

Engineering application
  • Drill string buckling (mining). A long, slender drill string in compression is a direct Euler buckling problem, and it is why drill strings are usually kept in tension wherever possible.
  • Support props and jacks (mining). Temporary roof supports and hydraulic props are compression members whose safe load is governed by buckling, not by material yield, exactly as this week distinguishes.
  • Truss and lattice tower members (general). Compression members in a transmission tower or roof truss are sized against buckling first, which is why they are braced at intervals to shorten the effective length $KL$.
08 Weeks 8–9: shear flow and torsion

Week 8 finishes the beam story from Weeks 3–4 by finding the shear stress the bending shear force $V$ produces, $\tau=\dfrac{VQ}{Ib}$, where $Q$ is the first moment of the area above (or below) the point of interest. The same result, expressed as force per unit length rather than stress, is shear flow, and it is what tells you how much load a weld, a row of bolts, or a glued joint between two pieces of a built-up beam must actually carry.

Week 9 applies the same cut-and-formula method to a circular shaft under a torque $T$. Assuming, as before, that cross-sections remain plane and simply rotate rigidly, produces $\tau=\dfrac{T\rho}{J}$, shear stress varying linearly from zero at the centre to a maximum at the outer radius, where $J$ is the polar second moment of area — the torsional counterpart of $I$ from Week 4. The angle of twist, $\phi=TL/(GJ)$, is torsion's version of Week 2's axial elongation formula, with shear modulus $G$ in place of $E$.

Engineering application
  • Mill and crusher drive shafts (mining). The drive shaft of a ball mill, crusher or conveyor gearbox is sized almost entirely from this week's torsion formula and angle-of-twist limit.
  • Built-up beam welds (mining and general). The weld or bolt spacing joining plates into a fabricated beam or crane girder is sized directly from the shear flow result, not from the bending stress alone.
  • Drill rod and downhole tooling (mining). Torque transmitted down a drill string to the bit is a torsion problem, and the angle of twist matters for how responsive the tool feels at the surface.
09 Weeks 10–11: Mohr's circle for stress and strain

By Week 10 a single point in a loaded component can carry a normal stress from bending, a shear stress from torsion, and another normal stress from axial load, all at once. Mohr's circle is a graphical solution to the stress-transformation equations that answers the question every one of the previous formulas has been avoiding: what does that combined stress state look like on a cut taken at some other angle? The circle's highest and lowest points give the principal stresses $\sigma_1,\sigma_2$ — the orientation where shear vanishes and normal stress is at its extreme — and the circle's radius gives the maximum shear stress, which is often what actually governs failure in a ductile material.

Week 11 draws the identical circle for strain instead of stress, which matters because strain, not stress, is what a strain gauge on a real structure actually measures. A strain gauge rosette — three gauges at fixed angles at one point — gives three strain readings that Mohr's circle converts into the principal strains and, through Hooke's law, back into the principal stresses at that point on the real, physical structure.

Engineering application
  • Strain-gauge instrumentation on mining equipment (mining). Rosettes bonded to a shovel boom, dragline structure or truck chassis in the field are read using exactly this week's strain circle, to back out the real stress the structure is experiencing in service.
  • Weld toe and fatigue-critical points (mining and general). The worst-case orientation for a crack to initiate at a weld or stress concentration is the principal stress direction found from Mohr's circle, not necessarily the direction of the applied load.
  • Rock and soil stress state (mining). In-situ stress measurements around an excavation are interpreted with the same stress-transformation logic, generalised to three dimensions.
10 Week 12: pressure vessels and combined loading

A thin-walled pressure vessel is introduced as a clean, self-contained combined-stress problem: internal pressure $p$ produces a hoop stress $\sigma_\theta=pr/t$ and a longitudinal stress $\sigma_l=pr/(2t)$ acting at right angles to each other on the same element of the vessel wall, exactly the two-normal-stress state Mohr's circle was built to handle. More generally, combined loading takes a component under several load types at once — axial force, bending, torsion, internal pressure, in any combination — and uses superposition to add up every stress contribution from every formula in Weeks 2–9 at the one critical point, producing the full stress state that Weeks 10–11 then resolve with Mohr's circle.

Quick example

A shaft under a bending moment and a torque at the same section has a normal stress from $\sigma=My/I$ and a shear stress from $\tau=T\rho/J$ acting at the same point simultaneously. Neither formula alone tells you whether the shaft is safe; only after both are combined into one stress state can Mohr's circle from Week 10 find the actual maximum shear stress the material experiences.

Engineering application
  • Slurry and gas pipelines under pressure (mining and general). Wall thickness for a pressurised pipeline is set from the hoop-stress formula, then checked again once bending from its own weight or thermal expansion is added on top.
  • Hydraulic cylinders (mining). A hydraulic ram barrel on mobile mining equipment combines internal pressure hoop stress with axial stress from the load the ram is pushing, a direct combined-loading problem.
  • Compressed air receivers (general). Any air receiver or storage vessel on a mine or plant site is designed to the same thin-wall pressure vessel formulas developed this week.

14 What you will be able to solve

By the end of the session, given a realistic engineering scenario and no instruction about which method to apply, you should be able to do all of the following.

  • Cut a loaded member at any section, use equilibrium to find the internal axial force, shear force, bending moment or torque there, and identify which stress formula applies.
  • Compute the axial, bending and torsional stress at a point, and check it against an allowable stress with a stated factor of safety.
  • Sketch shear force and bending moment diagrams for a loaded beam, and use them to find the beam's deflected shape.
  • Recognise when a compression member should be checked for buckling rather than, or in addition to, material yield.
  • Combine several stresses acting at one point into a single stress state, and use Mohr's circle to find the principal stresses and maximum shear stress.
  • Apply a failure theory to decide whether a combined stress state causes a ductile or brittle material to fail.

Together those steps amount to finding the internal force, converting it to stress, and checking the result against the material's actual strength — which is the design-verification loop this subject exists to train.

Six things to carry into Week 1
  • Every stress formula starts with a cut. DPEN102 gives you the internal force there; this subject tells you what that force does to the material.
  • The same assumption pattern repeats. Axial, bending and torsion formulas all come from assuming the cross-section deforms in one simple, known way.
  • Deflection and buckling are the same equation used twice. $EIy''=M(x)$ integrated gives shape; the same equation under axial compression gives an instability load.
  • Mohr's circle is not a new topic, it is bookkeeping. It exists purely to combine stresses you already know how to compute individually.
  • Strain, not stress, is what gets measured. A real stress value on a real structure comes from a strain gauge reading converted through Hooke's law.
  • Failure theories are the final design check. Everything from Week 2 onward exists to produce the stress state that Week 13 compares against the material's actual strength.
