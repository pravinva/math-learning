Lay of the land — this block's toolkit
  • Strain transformation equations mirror the stress equations exactly, with $\varepsilon\leftrightarrow\sigma$ and $\gamma/2\leftrightarrow\tau$.

0 Strain Transformation Equations

Just as stress transforms with orientation (Week 10), so does strain. At a point, normal strains $\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y$ and shear strain $\gamma_{xy}$ transform to a rotated set of axes via equations with exactly the same structure as the stress transformation equations, with $\varepsilon\leftrightarrow\sigma$ and $\dfrac{\gamma}2\leftrightarrow\tau$ (note the factor of 2 — a subtlety that trips up many students):

$$\varepsilon_{x'}=\dfrac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}2+\dfrac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\cos2\theta+\dfrac{\gamma_{xy}}2\sin2\theta \qquad \dfrac{\gamma_{x'y'}}2=-\dfrac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\sin2\theta+\dfrac{\gamma_{xy}}2\cos2\theta$$

The reason for the factor of 2: engineering shear strain $\gamma_{xy}$ (the total angular distortion) is twice the tensor shear strain that transforms exactly like $\tau_{xy}$ mathematically — so every formula from Week 10 carries over perfectly as long as you consistently use $\gamma/2$ in place of $\tau$.

Key idea

$$\varepsilon_{x'}=\frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}2+\frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\cos2\theta+\frac{\gamma_{xy}}2\sin2\theta \qquad \text{(same structure as stress, with } \gamma/2 \leftrightarrow \tau\text{)}$$

Worked example

Example. At a point, $\varepsilon_x=400\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=100\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\gamma_{xy}=300\ \mu\text{rad}$. Find $\varepsilon_{x'}$ at $\theta=30\,{}^\circ$ ($\mu\varepsilon=$ microstrain $=10^{-6}$).

$\varepsilon_{x'}=\dfrac{400+100}2+\dfrac{400-100}2\cos60\,{}^\circ+\dfrac{300}2\sin60\,{}^\circ=250+150(0.5)+150(0.866)=250+75+129.9=454.9\ \mu\varepsilon$.

Practice 1

At a point, $\varepsilon_x=600\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=0$, $\gamma_{xy}=0$ (uniaxial strain). Find $\varepsilon_{x'}$ at $\theta=45\,{}^\circ$.

Solution

$\varepsilon_{x'}=\dfrac{600+0}2+\dfrac{600-0}2\cos90\,{}^\circ+0=300+0=300\ \mu\varepsilon$.

Practice 2

At a point, $\varepsilon_x=0,\varepsilon_y=0,\gamma_{xy}=500\ \mu\text{rad}$ (pure shear strain). Find $\varepsilon_{x'}$ at $\theta=45\,{}^\circ$.

Solution

$\varepsilon_{x'}=0+0+\dfrac{500}2\sin90\,{}^\circ=250\ \mu\varepsilon$ (pure shear strain transforms into pure normal strain at 45°, exactly paralleling the stress case).

Practice 3

At a point, $\varepsilon_x=350\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=150\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\gamma_{xy}=200\ \mu\text{rad}$. Find $\varepsilon_{x'}$ at $\theta=0\,{}^\circ$ and verify it recovers $\varepsilon_x$.

Solution

At $\theta=0$: $\varepsilon_{x'}=\dfrac{350+150}2+\dfrac{350-150}2(1)+0=250+100=350\ \mu\varepsilon=\varepsilon_x$. ✓

Practice 4

Explain why the strain transformation equations use $\gamma_{xy}/2$ rather than $\gamma_{xy}$ directly, in terms of the mathematical requirement for the transformation to have the same form as the stress transformation.

Solution

Stress and strain, despite being physically different quantities, both transform under rotation as components of a mathematical tensor. The stress tensor's off-diagonal component is simply $\tau_{xy}$, but the strain tensor's off-diagonal component is actually $\gamma_{xy}/2$ (not the full engineering shear strain $\gamma_{xy}$, which by convention represents the total angular change between two originally perpendicular lines, double the tensor definition). Using $\gamma_{xy}/2$ in the transformation equations restores the correct tensor-consistent quantity, which is precisely why the stress and strain transformation formulas share an identical mathematical structure once this substitution is made.

Practice 5

At a point, $\varepsilon_x=250\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=50\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\gamma_{xy}=100\ \mu\text{rad}$. Find $\gamma_{x'y'}$ at $\theta=30\,{}^\circ$ (recall the shear-strain transformation formula gives $\gamma_{x'y'}/2$ directly, so double the result).

Solution

$\dfrac{\gamma_{x'y'}}2=-\dfrac{250-50}2\sin60\,{}^\circ+\dfrac{100}2\cos60\,{}^\circ=-100(0.866)+50(0.5)=-86.6+25=-61.6$. $\gamma_{x'y'}=-123.2\ \mu\text{rad}$.

Practice 6

Explain, in one or two sentences, why the strain transformation equations are directly useful for interpreting the output of electrical resistance strain gauges, which measure normal strain along a single fixed direction on a surface.

Solution

A strain gauge can only directly measure the normal strain $\varepsilon_{x'}$ along its own bonded orientation on the surface, not the full strain state (which requires three independent pieces of information: $\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y,\gamma_{xy}$). Using multiple gauges at different, known orientations and the strain transformation equations (or equivalently, working backward through them) allows the full strain state at that point to be reconstructed from several single-direction readings — the basis of the strain gauge rosette technique covered later in this week.

1 Principal Strains & Maximum Shear Strain

Exactly paralleling Week 10: principal strains $\varepsilon_1,\varepsilon_2$ are the maximum and minimum normal strains, occurring where shear strain is zero: $\varepsilon_{1,2}=\dfrac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}2\pm\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\right)^2+\left(\dfrac{\gamma_{xy}}2\right)^2}$, at orientation $\tan2\theta_p=\dfrac{\gamma_{xy}}{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}$.

Maximum in-plane shear strain: $\dfrac{\gamma_{\max}}2=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\right)^2+\left(\dfrac{\gamma_{xy}}2\right)^2}$ (same radius quantity as the principal strain formula), occurring on planes $45\,{}^\circ$ from the principal planes — the exact strain-domain mirror of everything covered for stress last week.

Key idea

$$\varepsilon_{1,2}=\frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}2\pm\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\right)^2+\left(\frac{\gamma_{xy}}2\right)^2} \qquad \frac{\gamma_{\max}}2=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\right)^2+\left(\frac{\gamma_{xy}}2\right)^2}$$

Worked example

Example. At a point, $\varepsilon_x=400\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=100\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\gamma_{xy}=300\ \mu\text{rad}$ (same as Section 0). Find the principal strains.

$\varepsilon_{\text{avg}}=\dfrac{400+100}2=250\ \mu\varepsilon$. Radius $=\sqrt{150^2+150^2}=\sqrt{45000}=212.1\ \mu\varepsilon$.

$\varepsilon_1=250+212.1=462.1\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_2=250-212.1=37.9\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\gamma_{\max}=2(212.1)=424.2\ \mu\text{rad}$.

Practice 7

At a point, $\varepsilon_x=500\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=200\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\gamma_{xy}=0$. Confirm the principal strains directly equal the given values.

Solution

Radius $=\sqrt{150^2+0}=150\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_1=500\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_2=200\ \mu\varepsilon$ — correctly recovers the given values, since zero shear strain means the axes are already principal.

Practice 8

At a point, $\varepsilon_x=0,\varepsilon_y=0,\gamma_{xy}=400\ \mu\text{rad}$ (pure shear strain). Find the principal strains.

Solution

Radius $=\sqrt{0+200^2}=200\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_1=200\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_2=-200\ \mu\varepsilon$.

Practice 9

At a point, $\varepsilon_x=600\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=-100\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\gamma_{xy}=250\ \mu\text{rad}$. Find the principal strains.

Solution

$\varepsilon_{\text{avg}}=250\ \mu\varepsilon$. Radius $=\sqrt{350^2+125^2}=\sqrt{122500+15625}=\sqrt{138125}=371.7\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_1=621.7\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_2=-121.7\ \mu\varepsilon$.

Practice 10

For the strain state of the previous question, find the maximum in-plane shear strain.

Solution

$\gamma_{\max}=2(371.7)=743.4\ \mu\text{rad}$.

Practice 11

Explain the analogous role that the maximum in-plane shear strain plays relative to the principal strains, using the same geometric relationship established for stress in Week 10 (planes 45° apart).

Solution

Exactly as with stress, the maximum shear strain occurs on planes oriented $45\,{}^\circ$ from the principal strain planes (where normal strain is extreme and shear strain is zero); the value of $\gamma_{\max}/2$ is numerically identical to the "radius" term in the principal strain formula, meaning the same underlying transformation produces both extremes, just read at different points around Mohr's circle for strain (covered next).

2 Mohr’s Circle for Strain

Mohr's circle for strain is constructed identically to Mohr's circle for stress (Week 10), with the substitution $\sigma\to\varepsilon$ and $\tau\to\gamma/2$: plot $\varepsilon$ horizontally and $\gamma/2$ vertically. Centre at $\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}2,0\right)$, radius $R=\sqrt{\left(\dfrac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\right)^2+\left(\dfrac{\gamma_{xy}}2\right)^2}$ — every geometric rule from Week 10 (physical $\theta\to$ circle $2\theta$, same sense; principal strains at the $\varepsilon$-axis crossings; max shear strain at top/bottom) carries over unchanged.

The plotted vertical coordinate is always $\gamma/2$, never $\gamma$ itself — the single most common mistake in this topic. Forgetting this factor of 2 throws off every subsequent reading taken from the circle.

Key idea

$$\text{Centre: } \left(\frac{\varepsilon_x+\varepsilon_y}2,0\right) \qquad \text{Radius: } R=\sqrt{\left(\frac{\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y}2\right)^2+\left(\frac{\gamma_{xy}}2\right)^2} \qquad \text{Plot } \gamma/2 \text{ vertically, NOT } \gamma$$

Worked example

Example. Using the same strain state as before ($\varepsilon_x=400\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=100\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\gamma_{xy}=300\ \mu\text{rad}$), describe the Mohr’s circle for strain.

Centre $=(250,0)\ \mu\varepsilon$. The $x$-face point plots at $(\varepsilon_x,\gamma_{xy}/2)=(400,150)$. Radius (distance from centre to this point) $=\sqrt{150^2+150^2}=212.1\ \mu\varepsilon$, matching Section 1’s calculation exactly. ✓

The circle crosses the horizontal axis at $\varepsilon=250\pm212.1$, giving $\varepsilon_1=462.1\ \mu\varepsilon$ and $\varepsilon_2=37.9\ \mu\varepsilon$ — identical to the algebraic results.

Practice 12

For $\varepsilon_x=300\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_y=0$, $\gamma_{xy}=0$, find the centre and radius of Mohr's circle for strain.

Solution

Centre $=(150,0)\ \mu\varepsilon$. Radius $=\sqrt{150^2+0}=150\ \mu\varepsilon$.

Practice 13

For $\varepsilon_x=0,\varepsilon_y=0,\gamma_{xy}=400\ \mu\text{rad}$, find the vertical plotting coordinate for the $x$-face point (not $\gamma_{xy}$ itself, but the correctly halved value).

Solution

Plot at $(\varepsilon_x,\gamma_{xy}/2)=(0,200)\ \mu\varepsilon$ — note that $200$, not $400$, is the correct vertical coordinate.

Practice 14

A Mohr's circle for strain has centre $(180,0)\ \mu\varepsilon$ and radius $120\ \mu\varepsilon$. Find the principal strains and maximum shear strain.

Solution

$\varepsilon_1=180+120=300\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_2=180-120=60\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\gamma_{\max}=2(120)=240\ \mu\text{rad}$.

Practice 15

Explain the single most common numerical error students make when constructing Mohr's circle for strain (as opposed to stress), and why it happens.

Solution

The most common error is plotting the raw engineering shear strain $\gamma_{xy}$ directly on the vertical axis, rather than the correctly halved value $\gamma_{xy}/2$. This happens because Mohr's circle for stress plots $\tau_{xy}$ directly (no halving needed there), so students familiar with the stress version often carry that habit over to strain without realising the extra factor-of-2 relationship between engineering shear strain and the tensor shear-strain quantity that actually transforms like stress.

Practice 16

A Mohr's circle for strain has centre $(500,0)\ \mu\varepsilon$, and the $x$-face point plots at $(700,80)$ (already correctly using $\gamma_{xy}/2$). Find the radius and hence $\gamma_{xy}$ itself (the original, un-halved value).

Solution

Radius $=\sqrt{(700-500)^2+80^2}=\sqrt{40000+6400}=\sqrt{46400}=215.4\ \mu\varepsilon$ (used for principal strains, not needed for this specific question). Since the plotted vertical value $80=\gamma_{xy}/2$, the original $\gamma_{xy}=160\ \mu\text{rad}$.

Practice 17

A strain state has $\varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_y$ (equal normal strains). Using the direct strain-domain analogue of the stress result from Week 10, state the orientation of the principal strain axes without further calculation.

Solution

Exactly as with the equal-normal-stress case, $\theta_p=45\,{}^\circ$ whenever $\varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_y$ and $\gamma_{xy}\ne0$, since the denominator $\varepsilon_x-\varepsilon_y$ in the $\tan2\theta_p$ formula becomes zero, forcing $2\theta_p=90\,{}^\circ$.

3 Strain Gauge Rosettes

Since a single strain gauge measures only one normal strain value along its own fixed orientation, and the full 2D strain state has three independent unknowns ($\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y,\gamma_{xy}$), a strain rosette — three gauges bonded at known, different angles at essentially the same point — is used to fully determine the strain state experimentally.

The most common configuration is the 45° rosette (gauges at $0\,{}^\circ,45\,{}^\circ,90\,{}^\circ$, often labelled $\varepsilon_a,\varepsilon_b,\varepsilon_c$). Directly, $\varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_a$ (the $0\,{}^\circ$ gauge reads $\varepsilon_x$ exactly) and $\varepsilon_y=\varepsilon_c$ (the $90\,{}^\circ$ gauge reads $\varepsilon_y$ exactly); the $45\,{}^\circ$ gauge reading combines with the transformation equation to solve for $\gamma_{xy}$: $\gamma_{xy}=2\varepsilon_b-\varepsilon_a-\varepsilon_c$.

Key idea

$$\text{45° rosette: } \varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_a \quad \varepsilon_y=\varepsilon_c \quad \gamma_{xy}=2\varepsilon_b-\varepsilon_a-\varepsilon_c \quad \text{(gauges at 0°,45°,90°)}$$

Worked example

Example. A 45° rosette reads $\varepsilon_a=300\ \mu\varepsilon$ ($0\,{}^\circ$), $\varepsilon_b=200\ \mu\varepsilon$ ($45\,{}^\circ$), $\varepsilon_c=-50\ \mu\varepsilon$ ($90\,{}^\circ$). Find $\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y,\gamma_{xy}$, then the principal strains.

$\varepsilon_x=300\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_y=-50\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\gamma_{xy}=2(200)-300-(-50)=400-300+50=150\ \mu\text{rad}$.

$\varepsilon_{\text{avg}}=\dfrac{300-50}2=125\ \mu\varepsilon$. Radius $=\sqrt{175^2+75^2}=\sqrt{30625+5625}=\sqrt{36250}=190.4\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_1=315.4\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_2=-65.4\ \mu\varepsilon$.

Practice 18

A 45° rosette reads $\varepsilon_a=400\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_b=100\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_c=0$. Find $\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y,\gamma_{xy}$.

Solution

$\varepsilon_x=400\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_y=0$. $\gamma_{xy}=2(100)-400-0=-200\ \mu\text{rad}$.

Practice 19

For the strain state found in the previous question, find the principal strains.

Solution

$\varepsilon_{\text{avg}}=200\ \mu\varepsilon$. Radius $=\sqrt{200^2+100^2}=\sqrt{50000}=223.6\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_1=423.6\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_2=-23.6\ \mu\varepsilon$.

Practice 20

A 45° rosette reads $\varepsilon_a=500\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_b=500\ \mu\varepsilon$, $\varepsilon_c=500\ \mu\varepsilon$ (all equal readings). Find $\gamma_{xy}$ and interpret physically.

Solution

$\gamma_{xy}=2(500)-500-500=0$. Combined with $\varepsilon_x=\varepsilon_y=500\ \mu\varepsilon$, this describes a state of pure equal biaxial strain (like uniform expansion in all directions, with zero shear) — consistent with all three gauges reading identically regardless of their orientation, since there is no directional variation at all.

Practice 21

Explain why a strain rosette requires (at minimum) three gauges at three different orientations, rather than two, to fully characterise the 2D strain state at a point.

Solution

The general 2D strain state has three independent unknowns: $\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y,\gamma_{xy}$. Each gauge reading provides exactly one equation (its own normal strain, expressed via the transformation equation, in terms of these three unknowns at its known orientation). Three independent equations are needed to solve for three unknowns, so a minimum of three gauges at three distinct orientations is required; two gauges would leave the system underdetermined (one equation short), unable to uniquely resolve the shear strain component.

Practice 22

A rectangular rosette has gauges at $0\,{}^\circ,45\,{}^\circ,90\,{}^\circ$ reading $\varepsilon_a=250,\varepsilon_b=-50,\varepsilon_c=150$ (all $\mu\varepsilon$). Find $\varepsilon_x,\varepsilon_y,\gamma_{xy}$.

Solution

$\varepsilon_x=250\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\varepsilon_y=150\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\gamma_{xy}=2(-50)-250-150=-100-400=-500\ \mu\text{rad}$.

Practice 23

For the rosette data of the previous question, find the maximum in-plane shear strain.

Solution

$\varepsilon_{\text{avg}}=200\ \mu\varepsilon$. Radius $=\sqrt{50^2+250^2}=\sqrt{2500+62500}=\sqrt{65000}=254.9\ \mu\varepsilon$. $\gamma_{\max}=2(254.9)=509.9\ \mu\text{rad}$.
