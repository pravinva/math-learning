This turns the bending moment diagrams from Week 3 into actual beam deflections and slopes. Exam-level worked examples and practice throughout. Solutions stay hidden until you click.

Lay of the land — this block's toolkit
  • Governing equation: $EI\,d^2v/dx^2=M(x)$ — integrate twice for slope then deflection.
  • Boundary conditions come from the actual supports (e.g. $v=0$ at a pin/roller, $v=\theta=0$ at a fixed support).
  • Multiple regions: separate $M(x)$ per region, plus continuity of both slope and deflection at region boundaries.
  • Standard results: simply-supported UDL $v_{\max}=-5wL^4/384EI$; central point load $v_{\max}=-PL^3/48EI$; cantilever tip load $v_{\max}=-PL^3/3EI$.
  • Superposition: because the governing equation is linear, sum individual-load deflections for the same beam/support conditions.

Open on YouTube ↗

Combining the flexure formula with the geometry of bending gives the moment-curvature relationship: $\dfrac1\rho=\dfrac{M}{EI}$, where $\rho$ is the radius of curvature of the deformed beam axis. For small deflections (the usual engineering assumption), curvature $\dfrac1\rho\approx\dfrac{d^2v}{dx^2}$, where $v(x)$ is the beam's transverse deflection.

This gives the differential equation of the elastic curve: $EI\dfrac{d^2v}{dx^2}=M(x)$. Since $M(x)$ is known from the bending moment diagram (Week 3), integrating this equation twice gives the slope $\theta(x)=dv/dx$ (first integration) and the deflection $v(x)$ (second integration), with constants of integration found from boundary conditions (known deflection/slope values at supports).

Key idea

$$\frac1\rho=\frac{M}{EI} \qquad EI\frac{d^2v}{dx^2}=M(x) \qquad \theta(x)=\frac{dv}{dx}=\frac1{EI}\int M\,dx \qquad v(x)=\int\theta\,dx$$

Worked example

Example. A beam segment has constant $M=20$ kN·m, $E=200$ GPa, $I=4\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$. Find the radius of curvature.

$\rho=\dfrac{EI}{M}=\dfrac{200\times10^9\times4\times10^{-5}}{20000}=400\ \text{m}$ (a very gentle curve, as expected for realistic elastic deflections).

Practice 1

A beam has $EI=6\times10^6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$ and carries a constant moment $M=3$ kN·m over some region. Find the radius of curvature there.

Solution

$\rho=EI/M=6\times10^6/3000=2000\ \text{m}$.

Practice 2

State the correct sign relationship: if $M(x)>0$ (sagging) throughout a region, is the curvature $d^2v/dx^2$ positive or negative there (using the standard convention that $v$ is measured positive upward)?

Solution

Positive (in the standard convention where downward deflection is treated with $v$ measured upward and sagging bends the beam concave up, matching $d^2v/dx^2>0$ — note some texts use the opposite sign convention with $v$ positive downward, so always check the convention stated in the problem).

Practice 3

A cantilever beam (fixed at $x=0$) has $EI\dfrac{d^2v}{dx^2}=M(x)=-P(L-x)$ for a tip load $P$. Integrate once to find $EI\,\theta(x)$, applying the boundary condition $\theta(0)=0$ (no rotation at the fixed end).

Solution

$EI\theta(x)=\int-P(L-x)dx=-P\left(Lx-\dfrac{x^2}2\right)+C_1$. At $x=0$: $\theta(0)=0\Rightarrow C_1=0$. So $EI\theta(x)=-P\left(Lx-\dfrac{x^2}2\right)$.

Practice 4

Continuing the previous question, integrate once more to find $EI\,v(x)$, applying $v(0)=0$ (no deflection at the fixed end).

Solution

$EIv(x)=\int-P\left(Lx-\dfrac{x^2}2\right)dx=-P\left(\dfrac{Lx^2}2-\dfrac{x^3}6\right)+C_2$. At $x=0$: $v(0)=0\Rightarrow C_2=0$. So $EIv(x)=-P\left(\dfrac{Lx^2}2-\dfrac{x^3}6\right)$.

Practice 5

Using the result from the previous two questions, find the tip deflection $v(L)$ of the cantilever (a standard, frequently memorised result).

Solution

$EIv(L)=-P\left(\dfrac{L^3}2-\dfrac{L^3}6\right)=-P\cdot\dfrac{L^3}3=-\dfrac{PL^3}3$. So $v(L)=-\dfrac{PL^3}{3EI}$ (downward, matching physical expectation for a downward tip load).

Practice 6

Explain why the two constants of integration arising from $EI\,d^2v/dx^2=M(x)$ must be determined from boundary conditions (physical constraints at the supports) rather than being arbitrary, and give the boundary conditions for a simply supported beam (pin at each end).

Solution

The differential equation alone only determines the shape of the deflected curve up to two free constants (matching the two integrations performed); the beam's actual physical supports pin down where the curve sits and how it's oriented, exactly analogous to needing initial conditions for any 2nd-order ODE. For a simply supported beam, both end supports (pins) prevent vertical deflection but allow rotation, so the boundary conditions are $v(0)=0$ and $v(L)=0$ (zero deflection at each support, but $\theta$ unrestricted).

Practice 7

For a fixed-fixed beam (both ends built in), state all four boundary conditions needed (in terms of $v$ and $\theta$ at each end).

Solution

$v(0)=0,\ \theta(0)=0,\ v(L)=0,\ \theta(L)=0$ — both deflection and rotation are prevented at each fixed end.

1 Double Integration Method — Single-Region Beams

For a beam where $M(x)$ has a single algebraic expression over the entire span (no discontinuities from point loads/reactions partway along), the double integration method is direct: write $M(x)$, integrate twice with $EI$, apply the two boundary conditions to find the constants, then evaluate $v(x)$ anywhere — especially at the point of maximum deflection (where $\theta=dv/dx=0$).

For a simply supported beam under a full-span UDL $w$, this gives the classic result $v(x)=\dfrac{wx}{24EI}\left(L^3-2Lx^2+x^3\right)$, with maximum deflection at midspan: $v_{\max}=-\dfrac{5wL^4}{384EI}$ (downward) — one of the most frequently used formulas in beam design.

Key idea

$$v_{\max}=-\frac{5wL^4}{384EI} \ \text{(simply supported, full UDL)} \qquad v_{\max}=-\frac{PL^3}{48EI} \ \text{(simply supported, central point load)}$$

Worked example

Example. A simply supported beam, span $L=4$ m, $EI=5\times10^6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, carries a central point load $P=10$ kN. Verify the maximum deflection formula by direct integration (region $0\le x\le L/2$, where $M(x)=\dfrac{P}2x$).

$EI\theta=\int\dfrac P2x\,dx=\dfrac P4x^2+C_1$. By symmetry, $\theta=0$ at midspan ($x=L/2$): $0=\dfrac P4\left(\dfrac L2\right)^2+C_1\Rightarrow C_1=-\dfrac{PL^2}{16}$.

$EIv=\int\left(\dfrac P4x^2-\dfrac{PL^2}{16}\right)dx=\dfrac P{12}x^3-\dfrac{PL^2}{16}x+C_2$; with $v(0)=0\Rightarrow C_2=0$. At $x=L/2$: $EIv_{\max}=\dfrac P{12}\left(\dfrac L2\right)^3-\dfrac{PL^2}{16}\cdot\dfrac L2=\dfrac{PL^3}{96}-\dfrac{PL^3}{32}=-\dfrac{PL^3}{48}$, matching the standard formula. ✓

Practice 8

A simply supported beam, span 6 m, $EI=8\times10^6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, carries a full-span UDL of $w=8$ kN/m. Find the maximum deflection using the standard formula.

Solution

$v_{\max}=-\dfrac{5(8000)(6)^4}{384(8\times10^6)}=-\dfrac{5(8000)(1296)}{3.072\times10^9}=-0.01688\ \text{m}=-16.9$ mm.

Practice 9

A simply supported beam, span 5 m, $E=200$ GPa, $I=6\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$, carries a central point load of 15 kN. Find the maximum deflection.

Solution

$EI=1.2\times10^7\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$. $v_{\max}=-\dfrac{15000(5)^3}{48(1.2\times10^7)}=-\dfrac{1875000}{5.76\times10^8}=-0.003255\ \text{m}=-3.26$ mm.

Practice 10

A simply supported beam must have a maximum deflection no greater than 10 mm under a central point load of 20 kN, span 4 m. If $E=200$ GPa, find the required $I$.

Solution

$0.010=\dfrac{20000(4)^3}{48(200\times10^9)I}\Rightarrow I=\dfrac{20000(64)}{48(200\times10^9)(0.010)}=\dfrac{1.28\times10^6}{9.6\times10^{10}}=1.333\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^4$.

Practice 11

A cantilever beam, length 3 m, $EI=4\times10^6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, carries a UDL of $w=6$ kN/m over its full length. Using the standard cantilever-UDL formula $v_{\max}=-\dfrac{wL^4}{8EI}$ (at the free end), find the tip deflection.

Solution

$v_{\max}=-\dfrac{6000(3)^4}{8(4\times10^6)}=-\dfrac{6000(81)}{3.2\times10^7}=-0.01519\ \text{m}=-15.2$ mm.

Practice 12

A cantilever beam, length 2 m, $EI=3\times10^6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, carries a tip point load of 5 kN. Using the standard formula $v_{\max}=-\dfrac{PL^3}{3EI}$, find the tip deflection.

Solution

$v_{\max}=-\dfrac{5000(2)^3}{3(3\times10^6)}=-\dfrac{40000}{9\times10^6}=-0.00444\ \text{m}=-4.44$ mm.

Practice 13

For a simply supported beam under a full-span UDL, find the slope at the left support ($x=0$) using $\theta(0)=\pm\dfrac{wL^3}{24EI}$ (standard result), for $w=10$ kN/m, $L=8$ m, $EI=1\times10^7\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$.

Solution

$\theta(0)=\dfrac{10000(8)^3}{24(1\times10^7)}=\dfrac{10000(512)}{2.4\times10^8}=0.02133\ \text{rad}$ (about 1.22°, a small angle consistent with the small-deflection assumption).

Practice 14

Explain why the maximum deflection of a simply supported beam under a UDL occurs exactly at midspan, using symmetry (rather than re-deriving the full double-integration solution).

Solution

The loading (full-span UDL) and the support conditions (equal pins at each end) are both symmetric about the beam's midpoint; a symmetric loading on a symmetric structure must produce a symmetric deflected shape, meaning the slope $\theta=dv/dx$ must be zero exactly at the centre (by the mirror symmetry, the deflection curve has zero slope at its own axis of symmetry). Since maximum deflection occurs where $\theta=0$, and symmetry forces that to be midspan, no further calculation is needed to locate it.

2 Double Integration Method — Multiple Regions & Continuity

When $M(x)$ has different expressions in different regions (e.g. a point load partway along a simply supported beam creates two regions), write a separate $M(x)$ and integrate separately in each region, producing two sets of constants ($C_1,C_2$ for region 1; $C_3,C_4$ for region 2).

Solve for all four constants using: (1) the boundary conditions at the actual supports (as before), plus (2) continuity conditions at the boundary between regions — physically, the beam is one continuous piece, so both the slope and the deflection computed from each region's equation must agree exactly at the shared point: $\theta_1(a)=\theta_2(a)$ and $v_1(a)=v_2(a)$, where $x=a$ is the region boundary.

Key idea

$$\text{Region 1: } EIv_1''=M_1(x) \qquad \text{Region 2: } EIv_2''=M_2(x) \qquad \text{Continuity at }x=a:\ \theta_1(a)=\theta_2(a),\ v_1(a)=v_2(a)$$

Worked example

Example (setup only). A simply supported beam, span $L$, carries a point load $P$ at $x=a$ (with $b=L-a$). Set up the four boundary/continuity conditions needed to solve for $v(x)$ in both regions.

Region 1 ($0\le x\le a$): $M_1(x)=\dfrac{Pb}Lx$. Region 2 ($a\le x\le L$): $M_2(x)=\dfrac{Pb}Lx-P(x-a)$.

Four conditions: $v_1(0)=0$ (left support), $v_2(L)=0$ (right support), $\theta_1(a)=\theta_2(a)$ (slope continuity at the load), $v_1(a)=v_2(a)$ (deflection continuity at the load) — solving these four equations simultaneously gives all four constants of integration.

Practice 15

For a simply supported beam with a point load at midspan ($a=b=L/2$), explain why the continuity conditions at $x=L/2$ combined with symmetry allow you to solve using only region 1 (as done in Section 1's worked example), rather than needing the full four-constant system.

Solution

When the load is exactly at midspan, the problem is symmetric, so $\theta(L/2)=0$ is already known directly from symmetry — this replaces one of the two continuity conditions with a simpler boundary-type condition, and by symmetry the region-2 solution is just the mirror image of region 1, so there's no need to solve for region 2's constants separately.

Practice 16

State, in words, why the deflection continuity condition ($v_1(a)=v_2(a)$) is physically necessary for a beam under a point load (i.e. why the beam couldn't have a "jump" in deflection at the load point).

Solution

A beam is a single continuous physical member; it cannot have a discontinuity (jump) in its own deflected position at any interior point, since that would imply the beam has physically torn apart at that section. The mathematics must reflect this physical continuity, hence $v_1(a)=v_2(a)$ is required.

Practice 17

State, in words, why the slope continuity condition ($\theta_1(a)=\theta_2(a)$) is physically necessary at a point load (as opposed to at an internal hinge, where slope continuity would NOT be required).

Solution

Away from any hinge, the beam is rigidly continuous through the cross-section at the load point — the tangent to the deflected beam axis must be the same whether approached from the left or the right, since there's no mechanism (like a pin/hinge) allowing a sudden kink in the beam's own shape. At an internal hinge, by contrast, the beam is deliberately allowed to rotate independently on each side, so slope continuity would NOT apply there (though deflection continuity still would, since a hinge doesn't allow the beam to separate).

Practice 18

For a simply supported beam with a point load $P$ at $a=2$ m from the left support (span $L=6$ m, so $b=4$ m), write $M_1(x)$ for region 1 ($0\le x\le2$) using the reaction $A_y=Pb/L=2P/3$.

Solution

$M_1(x)=A_yx=\dfrac{2P}3x$.

Practice 19

For the same beam, write $M_2(x)$ for region 2 ($2\le x\le6$).

Solution

$M_2(x)=\dfrac{2P}3x-P(x-2)$.

Practice 20

Explain the general strategy for finding the location of maximum deflection when a point load is NOT at midspan (i.e. why you generally cannot assume $x=L/2$ gives the maximum, and what condition instead locates it).

Solution

Without symmetry, the point of maximum deflection is simply wherever $\theta(x)=dv/dx=0$ occurs — but you must first determine which region this happens in (usually the longer segment, since that side has more "room" to deflect and typically governs), then solve $\theta(x)=0$ within that region's specific equation to find the exact location; it is generally close to, but not exactly at, midspan unless the load is symmetric.

Practice 21

Set up (do not solve) the four boundary/continuity conditions needed for a simply supported beam, span $L$, carrying a UDL over only the left half of the span (0 to $L/2$) and no load on the right half.

Solution

Region 1 ($0\le x\le L/2$, under the UDL) and region 2 ($L/2\le x\le L$, unloaded) each need their own $M(x)$. Conditions: $v_1(0)=0$, $v_2(L)=0$, $\theta_1(L/2)=\theta_2(L/2)$, $v_1(L/2)=v_2(L/2)$ — four equations for the four constants.

3 Method of Superposition

Because the governing differential equation $EIv''=M(x)$ is linear, deflections from several separate loads can be found individually (using standard tabulated formulas for each simple load case) and then added to get the total deflection under the combined loading — the principle of superposition. This avoids re-deriving multi-region double-integration solutions for every new combination of loads.

Practically: break a complex loading into simple cases you already know the formula for (point load, UDL, etc, each applied to the same beam and support conditions), look up (or recall) each case's deflection formula at the point of interest, then sum them algebraically (respecting sign/direction). Standard tabulated beam-deflection formulas (found in any mechanics-of-materials reference) are built specifically to support this method.

Key idea

$$v_{\text{total}}(x)=\sum_i v_i(x) \quad \text{(sum of individual-load deflections, same beam/support conditions, linear superposition)}$$

Worked example

Example. A simply supported beam, span $L=6$ m, $EI=1\times10^7\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, carries BOTH a full-span UDL $w=5$ kN/m AND a central point load $P=10$ kN. Find the total midspan deflection by superposition.

UDL alone: $v_1=-\dfrac{5wL^4}{384EI}=-\dfrac{5(5000)(6)^4}{384(1\times10^7)}=-\dfrac{5(5000)(1296)}{3.84\times10^9}=-0.008438\ \text{m}$.

Point load alone: $v_2=-\dfrac{PL^3}{48EI}=-\dfrac{10000(6)^3}{48(1\times10^7)}=-\dfrac{2160000}{4.8\times10^8}=-0.0045\ \text{m}$. Total: $v=v_1+v_2=-0.008438-0.0045=-0.01294\ \text{m}=-12.9$ mm.

Practice 22

A simply supported beam, span 5 m, $EI=9\times10^6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, carries a full-span UDL of $w=6$ kN/m and a central point load of $P=8$ kN. Find the total midspan deflection.

Solution

UDL: $v_1=-\dfrac{5(6000)(5)^4}{384(9\times10^6)}=-\dfrac{5(6000)(625)}{3.456\times10^9}=-0.005425$ m. Point load: $v_2=-\dfrac{8000(5)^3}{48(9\times10^6)}=-\dfrac{1000000}{4.32\times10^8}=-0.002315$ m. Total $=-0.00774\ \text{m}=-7.74$ mm.

Practice 23

A cantilever beam, length 3 m, $EI=5\times10^6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$, carries a tip point load of 4 kN AND a full-length UDL of 3 kN/m. Using $v_{\text{tip,point}}=-\dfrac{PL^3}{3EI}$ and $v_{\text{tip,UDL}}=-\dfrac{wL^4}{8EI}$, find the total tip deflection.

Solution

Point: $v_1=-\dfrac{4000(3)^3}{3(5\times10^6)}=-0.0072$ m. UDL: $v_2=-\dfrac{3000(3)^4}{8(5\times10^6)}=-\dfrac{3000(81)}{4\times10^7}=-0.006075$ m. Total $=-0.01328\ \text{m}=-13.3$ mm.

Practice 24

A simply supported beam, span 4 m, carries two separate point loads: 15 kN at midspan, and (by superposition) an additional UDL of 4 kN/m over the full span. $EI=7\times10^6\ \text{N}\cdot\text{m}^2$. Find the total midspan deflection.

Solution

Point load: $v_1=-\dfrac{15000(4)^3}{48(7\times10^6)}=-\dfrac{960000}{3.36\times10^8}=-0.002857$ m. UDL: $v_2=-\dfrac{5(4000)(4)^4}{384(7\times10^6)}=-\dfrac{5(4000)(256)}{2.688\times10^9}=-0.001905$ m. Total $=-0.004762\ \text{m}=-4.76$ mm.

Practice 25

Explain why superposition CANNOT be directly applied to combine deflections from two different beams with different support conditions (e.g. adding a cantilever's deflection formula to a simply supported beam's formula for the "same" physical beam), and what condition must hold for superposition to be valid.

Solution

Superposition relies on the linearity of $EIv''=M(x)$ for a fixed beam and fixed support configuration — each individual-load solution must be evaluated for that same physical beam, so that the resulting deflected shapes are directly compatible functions of $x$ that can be added pointwise. Mixing formulas derived for genuinely different support conditions (different boundary conditions entirely) produces results with no physical meaning, since they don't share the same underlying differential-equation boundary-value problem; superposition is only valid for combining multiple loads applied to one single, unchanging beam/support system.

Practice 26

A simply supported beam, span $L$, carries a point load $P$ at midspan and a point moment $M_0$ applied at the right support. Given $v_{\text{point}}(L/2)=-\dfrac{PL^3}{48EI}$ and (a standard tabulated result) $v_{\text{moment}}(L/2)=\dfrac{M_0L^2}{16EI}$ (deflection at midspan due to an end moment alone), find the total midspan deflection in terms of $P,M_0,L,EI$.

Solution

$v_{\text{total}}(L/2)=-\dfrac{PL^3}{48EI}+\dfrac{M_0L^2}{16EI}$ (algebraic sum, respecting the sign convention of each tabulated formula).

Practice 27

Explain the main practical advantage of the superposition method over directly solving the full multi-region double-integration problem for a beam with several different loads applied simultaneously.

Solution

Directly solving a beam with, say, three different loads (point loads and a UDL, at different locations) via double integration could require splitting the beam into multiple regions and solving a large simultaneous system for many constants of integration. Superposition instead lets you look up (or recall) the well-known, already-solved deflection formula for each individual load case separately (each far simpler, often a single clean formula) and just add the results — dramatically reducing the algebra involved for complex, multi-load real-world beams.
