0 Thin-Walled Cylindrical Pressure Vessels

A thin-walled cylindrical vessel (wall thickness $t\ll$ inner radius $r$, typically $r/t>10$) under internal pressure $p$ develops two distinct normal stresses in its wall. The hoop (circumferential) stress $\sigma_h=\dfrac{pr}{t}$ resists the pressure trying to split the cylinder along its length; the longitudinal (axial) stress $\sigma_l=\dfrac{pr}{2t}$ resists the pressure trying to blow the end caps off — exactly half the hoop stress.

Since $\sigma_h=2\sigma_l$, the hoop stress is always the more critical (larger) design stress for a cylindrical vessel — which is exactly why longitudinal seams on cylindrical pressure vessels (welds running parallel to the axis) must be stronger than circumferential seams: the longitudinal seam resists the larger hoop stress trying to pull it apart.

Key idea

$$\sigma_h=\frac{pr}{t} \quad \text{(hoop/circumferential)} \qquad \sigma_l=\frac{pr}{2t} \quad \text{(longitudinal/axial)} \qquad \sigma_h=2\sigma_l$$

Worked example

Example. A cylindrical pressure vessel, inner radius 500 mm, wall thickness 8 mm, internal pressure 2 MPa. Find the hoop and longitudinal stresses.

$\sigma_h=\dfrac{pr}{t}=\dfrac{2\times10^6(0.500)}{0.008}=125\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=125$ MPa.

$\sigma_l=\dfrac{\sigma_h}2=62.5$ MPa.

Practice 1

A cylindrical tank, inner radius 300 mm, wall thickness 6 mm, internal pressure 1.5 MPa. Find the hoop stress.

Solution

$\sigma_h=\dfrac{1.5\times10^6(0.300)}{0.006}=75\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=75$ MPa.

Practice 2

For the same tank, find the longitudinal stress.

Solution

$\sigma_l=75/2=37.5$ MPa.

Practice 3

A cylindrical vessel must not exceed an allowable hoop stress of 100 MPa. If the internal pressure is 3 MPa and the radius is 400 mm, find the minimum required wall thickness.

Solution

$t\ge\dfrac{pr}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}}=\dfrac{3\times10^6(0.400)}{100\times10^6}=0.012\ \text{m}=12$ mm.

Practice 4

A pipe (inner radius 250 mm, wall thickness 10 mm) carries a fluid at pressure $p$. If the allowable hoop stress is 120 MPa, find the maximum allowable pressure.

Solution

$p\le\dfrac{\sigma_{\text{allow}}t}{r}=\dfrac{120\times10^6(0.010)}{0.250}=4.8\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=4.8$ MPa.

Practice 5

Verify (using the ratio $r/t$) that a cylindrical vessel of radius 600 mm, wall thickness 15 mm, qualifies as "thin-walled" by the standard $r/t>10$ criterion.

Solution

$r/t=600/15=40>10$ — comfortably qualifies as thin-walled, so the simple membrane formulas $\sigma_h=pr/t$ and $\sigma_l=pr/2t$ are valid (thick-walled vessels would need the more complex Lamé equations instead).

Practice 6

Explain, using a free body diagram argument (cutting the cylinder along its length vs cutting it with a transverse plane), why the hoop stress formula has no factor of 2 in the denominator while the longitudinal stress formula does.

Solution

Cutting the cylinder along its length exposes two thin wall strips (each of area $t\times L$ for a length $L$) resisting the internal pressure acting over the projected rectangular area $2rL$ (diameter times length): $2(\sigma_h\,tL)=p(2rL)\Rightarrow\sigma_h=pr/t$. Cutting the cylinder with a transverse plane instead exposes the full wall's circular cross-sectional area (approximately $2\pi rt$ for a thin wall) resisting the pressure acting over the full circular end-cap area $\pi r^2$: $\sigma_l(2\pi rt)=p\pi r^2\Rightarrow\sigma_l=pr/(2t)$ — the extra factor of 2 in the longitudinal case arises directly from the different areas exposed by each type of cut, not from any arbitrary convention.

Practice 7

A cylindrical vessel has hoop stress 90 MPa. Using Mohr's circle for the biaxial stress state on the vessel's outer surface (with $\sigma_x=\sigma_h=90$ MPa, $\sigma_y=\sigma_l=45$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=0$, no shear on this element), find the maximum in-plane shear stress in the wall.

Solution

Using Week 10's method: $\tau_{\max}=\dfrac{\sigma_h-\sigma_l}2=\dfrac{90-45}2=22.5$ MPa.

1 Thin-Walled Spherical Pressure Vessels

A thin-walled spherical vessel under internal pressure develops a single normal stress value that is the same in every direction within the wall (by symmetry, there is no preferred "hoop" vs "longitudinal" direction on a sphere): $\sigma=\dfrac{pr}{2t}$ — exactly equal to the cylindrical vessel's longitudinal stress, and half the cylindrical vessel's hoop stress.

This is precisely why spherical pressure vessels are the most material-efficient shape for containing pressure: for the same radius, thickness, and pressure, a sphere's wall stress is only half that of an equivalent cylinder's peak (hoop) stress — which is why large gas-storage tanks are often spherical despite the more complex fabrication involved.

Key idea

$$\sigma_{\text{sphere}}=\frac{pr}{2t} \quad \text{(same in every in-plane direction, by symmetry)} \qquad \sigma_{\text{sphere}}=\sigma_{l,\text{cylinder}}=\tfrac12\sigma_{h,\text{cylinder}}$$

Worked example

Example. A spherical tank, inner radius 800 mm, wall thickness 10 mm, internal pressure 2.5 MPa. Find the wall stress.

$\sigma=\dfrac{pr}{2t}=\dfrac{2.5\times10^6(0.800)}{2(0.010)}=100\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=100$ MPa.

Practice 8

A spherical tank, inner radius 600 mm, wall thickness 8 mm, internal pressure 2 MPa. Find the wall stress.

Solution

$\sigma=\dfrac{2\times10^6(0.600)}{2(0.008)}=75\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=75$ MPa.

Practice 9

A spherical vessel must not exceed an allowable stress of 150 MPa under an internal pressure of 5 MPa, radius 500 mm. Find the minimum wall thickness.

Solution

$t\ge\dfrac{pr}{2\sigma_{\text{allow}}}=\dfrac{5\times10^6(0.500)}{2(150\times10^6)}=0.00833\ \text{m}=8.33$ mm.

Practice 10

Compare the required wall thickness for a spherical vessel versus a cylindrical vessel of the same radius (500 mm) and same internal pressure (4 MPa), both with allowable stress 120 MPa (using each vessel's respective governing/maximum stress formula).

Solution

Sphere: $t_{\text{sphere}}=\dfrac{pr}{2\sigma_{\text{allow}}}=\dfrac{4\times10^6(0.500)}{2(120\times10^6)}=8.33$ mm. Cylinder (governed by hoop stress): $t_{\text{cyl}}=\dfrac{pr}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}}=\dfrac{4\times10^6(0.500)}{120\times10^6}=16.67$ mm — the cylinder needs exactly twice the wall thickness of the sphere for the same pressure, radius, and allowable stress, directly illustrating the sphere's material efficiency.

Practice 11

A spherical tank has a wall stress of 80 MPa under a pressure of 1.6 MPa. Find its radius-to-thickness ratio $r/t$.

Solution

$\sigma=pr/2t\Rightarrow r/t=2\sigma/p=2(80\times10^6)/(1.6\times10^6)=100$.

Practice 12

Explain, in terms of stress distribution, why a spherical shape is the theoretically optimal (most material-efficient) shape for a pressure vessel, referencing the uniform stress state found in Section 1.

Solution

Because a sphere's wall stress is exactly uniform in every direction at every point (by the shape's perfect symmetry), all of the wall material is stressed to precisely the same level everywhere — there is no "wasted" material at a lower stress than the critical design value, unlike a cylinder, where the longitudinal stress is only half the hoop stress, meaning the wall could theoretically be thinner in the longitudinal-stress-resisting sense than what the (higher) hoop-stress requirement actually demands. This perfectly uniform utilisation of material is exactly why a sphere achieves the lowest possible wall thickness (and hence the lowest weight) for a given pressure, radius, and allowable stress.

2 Combined Axial, Bending & Torsional Loading

Real structural and machine components frequently experience several load types simultaneously — e.g. a shaft that is both bent (by a side load) and twisted (by a transmitted torque), possibly with an additional axial load (from a coupling or thrust bearing). The design approach: compute each stress component separately at the critical point using the formula appropriate to that load type (axial: $\sigma=P/A$; bending: $\sigma=-My/I$; torsion: $\tau=T\rho/J$; transverse shear: $\tau=VQ/Ib$), then algebraically combine all normal stresses (they simply add, since they act on the same plane) and use Mohr's circle (Week 10) to find the resulting principal stresses and maximum shear stress.

The critical point on a cross-section is not always obvious — it is typically found by checking the outer surface at the location(s) where bending stress is largest (the extreme fibre in the plane of bending) and where the combined effect of all stress types is most severe; for a circular shaft under bending plus torsion, this is almost always a point on the outer surface, in the plane of the bending moment.

Key idea

$$\sigma_{\text{total}}=\sigma_{\text{axial}}+\sigma_{\text{bending}} \ \text{(same point, same plane)} \qquad \tau_{\text{total}}=\tau_{\text{torsion}}+\tau_{\text{transverse shear}} \ \text{(if coincident)} \to \text{Mohr’s circle}$$

Worked example

Example. A solid circular shaft, diameter 60 mm, simultaneously carries a bending moment $M=1.2$ kN·m and a torque $T=1.5$ kN·m. Find the principal stresses at the critical outer-surface point.

$I=\dfrac{\pi(0.030)^4}4=6.362\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$. $\sigma_x=\dfrac{Mc}{I}=\dfrac{1200(0.030)}{6.362\times10^{-7}}=56.6\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=56.6$ MPa.

$J=2I=1.272\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^4$. $\tau_{xy}=\dfrac{Tc}{J}=\dfrac{1500(0.030)}{1.272\times10^{-6}}=35.4\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=35.4$ MPa. Using Mohr’s circle ($\sigma_y=0$): $\sigma_{\text{avg}}=28.3$ MPa, Radius $=\sqrt{28.3^2+35.4^2}=45.3$ MPa. $\sigma_1=73.6$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-17.0$ MPa.

Practice 13

A solid circular shaft, diameter 50 mm, carries a bending moment $M=800$ N·m and a torque $T=1000$ N·m. Find $\sigma_x$ (bending) and $\tau_{xy}$ (torsion) at the critical outer-surface point.

Solution

$I=\dfrac{\pi(0.025)^4}4=3.068\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$. $\sigma_x=\dfrac{800(0.025)}{3.068\times10^{-7}}=65.2$ MPa. $J=2I=6.136\times10^{-7}\ \text{m}^4$. $\tau_{xy}=\dfrac{1000(0.025)}{6.136\times10^{-7}}=40.7$ MPa.

Practice 14

For the shaft in the previous question, find the principal stresses using Mohr's circle ($\sigma_y=0$).

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=32.6$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{32.6^2+40.7^2}=52.1$ MPa. $\sigma_1=84.7$ MPa, $\sigma_2=-19.5$ MPa.

Practice 15

For the same shaft, find the maximum in-plane shear stress.

Solution

$\tau_{\max}=52.1$ MPa.

Practice 16

A shaft carries an axial tensile load $P=20$ kN (producing $\sigma=P/A$) in addition to a bending moment producing $\sigma_{\text{bend}}=40$ MPa at the same critical point, diameter 40 mm. Find the total normal stress at that point (assuming both stresses act on the same face, adding algebraically).

Solution

$A=\pi(0.020)^2=1.257\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$. $\sigma_{\text{axial}}=20000/1.257\times10^{-3}=15.9$ MPa. Total $\sigma_x=15.9+40=55.9$ MPa (both tensile, on the same side of the shaft where bending also produces tension).

Practice 17

A component experiences $\sigma_x=70$ MPa (combined axial + bending, already summed), $\sigma_y=0$, and $\tau_{xy}=25$ MPa (torsion). Find the maximum shear stress at that point.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{avg}}=35$ MPa. Radius $=\sqrt{35^2+25^2}=\sqrt{1225+625}=\sqrt{1850}=43.0$ MPa. $\tau_{\max}=43.0$ MPa.

Practice 18

Explain why, for a shaft under combined bending and torsion, the critical point for stress analysis is typically taken at the outer surface, in the plane of the applied bending moment, rather than at some other point on the circumference.

Solution

Bending stress $\sigma=-My/I$ is maximum at the extreme fibre farthest from the neutral axis, which for a circular shaft under bending in a fixed plane occurs exactly at the outer surface, in that same plane (i.e. directly "above" or "below" the shaft centre, aligned with the bending moment vector's perpendicular direction). Torsional shear stress $\tau=T\rho/J$ is maximum at the outer surface everywhere around the circumference (constant with angular position, since it depends only on radial distance $\rho$, not angular location). Since bending stress is angularly-dependent (maximum only at one specific point around the circumference) while torsional stress is angularly-uniform at the outer surface, the point where bending stress peaks is also where the combined stress state is generally most severe — making it the natural critical point to check.

3 Pressure Vessels with Additional Combined Loading

Real pressure vessels often experience combined loading beyond internal pressure alone — e.g. a vertical cylindrical tank also supports its own weight and contents (adding axial compressive stress), or a pipe under pressure is also subjected to an external bending moment (from its own weight span, thermal expansion constraints, or seismic loads). The hoop and longitudinal pressure stresses (Sections 0–1) combine algebraically with any additional axial or bending stress at the same point, exactly as in Section 2, before applying Mohr's circle.

For a cylindrical vessel element, the two in-plane normal stresses are typically hoop ($\sigma_h$, one direction) and longitudinal-plus-any-additional-axial/bending stress (the perpendicular direction), usually with zero shear on that particular element (unless torsion is also present, e.g. from an off-axis support or seismic torque) — a common and important special case of Mohr's circle where $\tau_{xy}=0$ from the start.

Key idea

$$\sigma_{\text{longitudinal,total}}=\sigma_l\pm\sigma_{\text{axial}}\pm\sigma_{\text{bending}} \qquad \text{(algebraic sum, then combine with } \sigma_h \text{ via Mohr’s circle)}$$

Worked example

Example. A vertical cylindrical tank, inner radius 1 m, wall thickness 12 mm, internal pressure 0.8 MPa, also carries a compressive axial stress of 15 MPa from its own weight and supported contents. Find the total longitudinal stress and the maximum in-plane shear stress at a point on the tank wall.

$\sigma_h=\dfrac{0.8\times10^6(1.0)}{0.012}=66.7$ MPa. $\sigma_l=\sigma_h/2=33.3$ MPa (tension, from pressure). Total longitudinal $=33.3-15=18.3$ MPa (tension, after subtracting the compressive weight-induced stress).

With $\sigma_x=\sigma_h=66.7$ MPa, $\sigma_y=18.3$ MPa, $\tau_{xy}=0$: $\tau_{\max}=\dfrac{66.7-18.3}2=24.2$ MPa.

Practice 19

A cylindrical tank, radius 800 mm, wall thickness 10 mm, pressure 1 MPa, also carries an additional tensile longitudinal stress of 10 MPa from thermal expansion restraint. Find the total longitudinal stress.

Solution

$\sigma_l=\dfrac{1\times10^6(0.800)}{2(0.010)}=40$ MPa (from pressure). Total $=40+10=50$ MPa.

Practice 20

For the tank in the previous question, find the hoop stress and hence the maximum in-plane shear stress (assuming $\tau_{xy}=0$ on this element).

Solution

$\sigma_h=\dfrac{1\times10^6(0.800)}{0.010}=80$ MPa. $\tau_{\max}=\dfrac{80-50}2=15$ MPa.

Practice 21

A pipe under internal pressure (hoop stress 60 MPa, longitudinal stress from pressure alone 30 MPa) also experiences an additional bending stress of 20 MPa (compressive) at the bottom of the pipe due to its own weight spanning between supports. Find the total longitudinal stress at that point.

Solution

Total longitudinal $=30-20=10$ MPa (tension, reduced by the compressive bending contribution).

Practice 22

A vertical tank carries pressure-induced hoop stress 50 MPa and longitudinal stress 25 MPa, plus an additional axial compressive stress of 25 MPa from the weight of the tank and its contents. Find the resulting net longitudinal stress, and comment on whether the wall could actually be in net compression at this point.

Solution

Net longitudinal $=25-25=0$ MPa — the pressure-induced tension and weight-induced compression exactly cancel at this particular point, leaving the wall in a state of pure hoop stress alone (uniaxial in that direction) with zero net longitudinal stress; a slightly larger weight-induced compression would indeed put the wall into net longitudinal compression.

Practice 23

Explain why a pressure vessel design that only checks the hoop and longitudinal stresses from internal pressure alone (ignoring self-weight, wind, seismic, or thermal loading) could be dangerously incomplete for a tall vertical tank, referencing the combined-stress approach of this section.

Solution

As shown throughout this section, additional loads (self-weight, bending from wind or seismic action, thermal restraint) directly add to or subtract from the pressure-induced longitudinal stress at any given point, and can also introduce shear stress if not perfectly aligned with the vessel's axis. A design that considers pressure stresses alone would miss these potentially significant additional contributions — in the wall-thickness-sizing example from Section 0, even a modest additional stress (10–20 MPa) shifted the total stress by a meaningful fraction of the pressure-only value, meaning a vessel that appeared safe under pressure alone could actually be over-stressed once all realistic combined loading is properly accounted for, particularly near the base of a tall tank where bending moments from wind/seismic loads are largest.
