0 Statics Review — Equilibrium & Free Body Diagrams

Every mechanics-of-solids problem starts the same way it did in Statics: isolate the body (or part of it) with a free body diagram (FBD), replace every removed support or connection with the force(s)/moment(s) it exerts, then apply the equilibrium equations $\sum F_x=0,\ \sum F_y=0,\ \sum M=0$ (in 2D) to solve for unknown reactions or internal forces.

Support types you'll see constantly: a pin provides a reaction force in any direction (2 unknowns: $R_x,R_y$) but no moment; a roller provides one reaction force perpendicular to the rolling surface (1 unknown); a fixed support provides a force in any direction plus a reaction moment (3 unknowns). Counting unknowns against available equilibrium equations tells you immediately whether a structure is statically determinate.

Key idea

$$\sum F_x=0 \qquad \sum F_y=0 \qquad \sum M_{\text{any point}}=0 \qquad \text{(2D rigid-body equilibrium)}$$

Worked example

Example. A simply supported beam (pin at A, roller at B), length 6 m, carries a downward point load $P=12$ kN at 2 m from A. Find the reactions.

$\sum M_A=0$: $-12(2)+B_y(6)=0\Rightarrow B_y=4$ kN. $\sum F_y=0$: $A_y+4-12=0\Rightarrow A_y=8$ kN. $\sum F_x=0\Rightarrow A_x=0$ (no horizontal load).

Practice 1

A simply supported beam, span 8 m, carries a central point load of 20 kN. Find the support reactions (pin at A, roller at B).

Solution

By symmetry, $A_y=B_y=10$ kN; $A_x=0$.

Practice 2

A cantilever beam of length 4 m is fixed at A and carries a downward point load of 10 kN at its free end. Find the reactions at the fixed support.

Solution

$\sum F_y=0\Rightarrow A_y=10$ kN (up). $\sum M_A=0\Rightarrow M_A=10(4)=40$ kN·m (counteracting the load's moment).

Practice 3

A simply supported beam (span 10 m) carries a uniformly distributed load of 5 kN/m over its full length. Find the reactions.

Solution

Total load $=50$ kN acting at midspan; by symmetry each reaction $=25$ kN.

Practice 4

A beam is supported by a pin at A and a roller at B (6 m apart) and carries a point load of 15 kN at 4 m from A. Find $A_y$ and $B_y$.

Solution

$\sum M_A=0$: $-15(4)+B_y(6)=0\Rightarrow B_y=10$ kN. $\sum F_y=0\Rightarrow A_y=5$ kN.

Practice 5

Classify each support type (pin, roller, fixed) by the number of reaction unknowns it introduces, and state the total unknowns for a beam with a fixed support at one end only.

Solution

Pin: 2 unknowns ($R_x,R_y$). Roller: 1 unknown. Fixed: 3 unknowns ($R_x,R_y,M$). A beam fixed at one end (cantilever) has exactly 3 unknowns, matching the 3 available 2D equilibrium equations — statically determinate.

Practice 6

A simply supported beam (span 12 m) carries two point loads: 8 kN at 3 m from A, and 12 kN at 9 m from A. Find the reactions.

Solution

$\sum M_A=0$: $-8(3)-12(9)+B_y(12)=0\Rightarrow B_y=(24+108)/12=11$ kN. $\sum F_y=0\Rightarrow A_y=20-11=9$ kN.

Practice 7

A beam overhangs its right support: pin at A (x=0), roller at B (x=6 m), with a 10 kN downward load at x=8 m (beyond B). Find the reactions.

Solution

$\sum M_A=0$: $-10(8)+B_y(6)=0\Rightarrow B_y=13.33$ kN. $\sum F_y=0\Rightarrow A_y=10-13.33=-3.33$ kN (i.e. 3.33 kN downward — the overhang lifts support A).

Practice 8

Explain, using unknown-counting, why a beam with a pin at one end and a pin at the other end (both providing 2 unknowns each, 4 total) is statically indeterminate in 2D.

Solution

2D rigid-body equilibrium provides only 3 independent equations ($\sum F_x,\sum F_y,\sum M$), but two pins introduce 4 unknowns. With more unknowns than equations, the reactions cannot be found from statics alone — the structure is statically indeterminate to the first degree, requiring compatibility (deformation) equations to solve, a theme that returns in Week 2.

1 Normal Stress on a Cross-Section

When an axial force $P$ acts through the centroid of a straight, prismatic member's cross-section, the average normal stress is $\sigma=\dfrac{P}{A}$, where $A$ is the cross-sectional area. Tensile $P$ gives positive (tensile) stress; compressive $P$ gives negative (compressive) stress.

This is only an average — true stress distribution can vary across the section (especially near load application points or geometric discontinuities, Saint-Venant's principle says these local effects die out within roughly one member-width of the disturbance). Units: Pa ($\text{N/m}^2$) in SI, commonly expressed in MPa ($\text{N/mm}^2$) for engineering-scale problems.

Key idea

$$\sigma=\frac{P}{A} \qquad \text{(average normal stress, axial loading, SI units: Pa or MPa)}$$

Worked example

Example. A steel rod of diameter 20 mm carries an axial tensile load of 50 kN. Find the average normal stress.

$A=\dfrac{\pi(0.020)^2}{4}=3.14\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $\sigma=\dfrac{50\times10^3}{3.14\times10^{-4}}=159.2\times10^6\ \text{Pa}=159.2\ \text{MPa}$.

Practice 9

A square bar, 25 mm × 25 mm cross-section, carries an axial tensile load of 80 kN. Find the normal stress.

Solution

$A=625\ \text{mm}^2=625\times10^{-6}\ \text{m}^2$. $\sigma=80000/625\times10^{-6}=128\ \text{MPa}$.

Practice 10

A circular rod of diameter 15 mm must carry an axial load of 25 kN without exceeding 150 MPa. Is it adequate?

Solution

$A=\pi(0.0075)^2=1.767\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $\sigma=25000/1.767\times10^{-4}=141.5$ MPa $<150$ MPa. Adequate.

Practice 11

A hollow circular tube (outer diameter 50 mm, inner diameter 40 mm) carries an axial compressive load of 100 kN. Find the normal stress.

Solution

$A=\frac\pi4(0.05^2-0.04^2)=7.07\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $\sigma=100000/7.07\times10^{-4}=141.4$ MPa (compressive).

Practice 12

Find the minimum diameter of a solid circular rod required to carry an axial tensile load of 60 kN without exceeding an allowable stress of 120 MPa.

Solution

$A\ge P/\sigma_{\text{allow}}=60000/120\times10^6=5\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $d=\sqrt{4A/\pi}=\sqrt{4(5\times10^{-4})/\pi}=0.0252\ \text{m}=25.2$ mm.

Practice 13

A rectangular bar 40 mm × 60 mm carries an axial load $P$. If the allowable stress is 90 MPa, find the maximum allowable $P$.

Solution

$A=2400\ \text{mm}^2=2.4\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$. $P_{\max}=\sigma_{\text{allow}}A=90\times10^6\times2.4\times10^{-3}=216$ kN.

Practice 14

A stepped bar has two sections: section 1 (diameter 30 mm) and section 2 (diameter 20 mm), both carrying the same axial load $P=40$ kN. Compare the stresses in each section.

Solution

$A_1=706.9\ \text{mm}^2\Rightarrow\sigma_1=56.6$ MPa. $A_2=314.2\ \text{mm}^2\Rightarrow\sigma_2=127.3$ MPa. The smaller section carries more than double the stress — the critical (governing) section for design.

Practice 15

Three cables, each of diameter 8 mm, support a load of 30 kN shared equally. Find the normal stress in each cable.

Solution

Load per cable $=10$ kN. $A=\pi(0.004)^2=5.03\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2$. $\sigma=10000/5.03\times10^{-5}=198.8$ MPa.

Practice 16

Explain why Saint-Venant's principle justifies using the simple formula $\sigma=P/A$ even though the true stress distribution near a bolted or pinned connection is highly non-uniform.

Solution

Saint-Venant's principle states that the specific manner in which a load is applied only affects the stress distribution significantly within a region roughly one characteristic member-dimension away from the load point; sufficiently far from such disturbances, the stress distribution settles down to the smooth, uniform average $P/A$ predicted by simple theory — which is why the formula remains valid for most of a member's length despite localised complications near connections.

2 Shear Stress on a Cross-Section

When an internal force acts parallel (tangent) to a cross-section rather than perpendicular to it, it produces average shear stress $\tau=\dfrac{V}{A}$, where $V$ is the shear force and $A$ is the area being sheared.

Two common connection scenarios: single shear (a bolt/pin resists load across one cross-sectional plane, e.g. a lap joint) gives $\tau=V/A$ directly with $V=P$; double shear (a pin resists load across two planes, e.g. a clevis/fork connection) shares the load over two areas, so $\tau=\dfrac{P}{2A}$ — roughly half the stress of the equivalent single-shear case for the same load and pin size.

Key idea

$$\tau=\frac{V}{A} \quad \text{(single shear: } V=P\text{)} \qquad \tau=\frac{P}{2A} \quad \text{(double shear)}$$

Worked example

Example. A 12 mm diameter bolt in single shear carries a load of 15 kN. Find the shear stress.

$A=\pi(0.006)^2=1.131\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $\tau=15000/1.131\times10^{-4}=132.6$ MPa.

Practice 17

A 10 mm diameter rivet in single shear carries a load of 8 kN. Find the shear stress.

Solution

$A=\pi(0.005)^2=7.854\times10^{-5}\ \text{m}^2$. $\tau=8000/7.854\times10^{-5}=101.9$ MPa.

Practice 18

A clevis pin (double shear), diameter 16 mm, carries a total load of 40 kN. Find the shear stress in the pin.

Solution

$A=\pi(0.008)^2=2.011\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $\tau=40000/(2\times2.011\times10^{-4})=99.5$ MPa.

Practice 19

Find the minimum bolt diameter (single shear) to carry a 25 kN load without exceeding an allowable shear stress of 80 MPa.

Solution

$A\ge25000/80\times10^6=3.125\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $d=\sqrt{4A/\pi}=0.01995\ \text{m}\approx20$ mm.

Practice 20

A double-shear pin connection carries 60 kN. If the allowable shear stress is 100 MPa, find the minimum pin diameter.

Solution

Each shear plane carries 30 kN. $A\ge30000/100\times10^6=3\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $d=\sqrt{4A/\pi}=19.5$ mm.

Practice 21

A lap joint uses a single 14 mm diameter bolt to transmit 18 kN. Find the shear stress and compare to an allowable of 110 MPa.

Solution

$A=1.539\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $\tau=18000/1.539\times10^{-4}=117$ MPa $>110$ MPa — inadequate, bolt would need to be larger.

Practice 22

A punch is used to punch a 20 mm diameter hole through a 10 mm thick steel plate. If the ultimate shear strength of the plate is 300 MPa, find the force required.

Solution

The sheared area is the cylindrical surface: $A=\pi d t=\pi(0.020)(0.010)=6.283\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $P=\tau_{\text{ult}}A=300\times10^6\times6.283\times10^{-4}=188.5$ kN.

Practice 23

Two plates are joined by two identical bolts in single shear sharing a load of 32 kN equally. Find the minimum bolt diameter if allowable shear stress is 90 MPa.

Solution

Load per bolt $=16$ kN. $A\ge16000/90\times10^6=1.778\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $d=\sqrt{4A/\pi}=15.0$ mm.

Practice 24

Explain, using a free body diagram argument, why a clevis (fork) pin connection is in double shear while a simple lap-joint bolt is in single shear.

Solution

In a clevis connection, the pin passes through three plates (the fork's two outer plates and the central plate carrying the load); cutting a free body around the central plate shows the pin resists the load across two internal cross-sectional planes (one on each side of the central member), sharing the load and halving the stress per plane. In a lap joint, only two plates overlap and the bolt is cut by a single plane between them, so the full load acts across just one shear plane.

3 Allowable Stress Design & Factor of Safety

Real materials fail at some ultimate stress $\sigma_u$ (or yield stress $\sigma_Y$ for ductile materials, where permanent deformation begins). To design safely, engineers apply a factor of safety $FS=\dfrac{\sigma_{\text{failure}}}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}}>1$, so the allowable stress used in design is $\sigma_{\text{allow}}=\dfrac{\sigma_{\text{failure}}}{FS}$.

$FS$ accounts for uncertainties: material property variability, approximations in load estimation, simplifications in analysis, and consequences of failure. Typical values range from 1.5–2 (well-understood loads, ductile materials, low consequence of failure) up to 4–8+ (uncertain loads, brittle materials, high consequence of failure, e.g. life-safety structures). Allowable stress design (ASD) requires $\sigma_{\text{actual}}\le\sigma_{\text{allow}}$ everywhere in the structure.

Key idea

$$FS=\frac{\sigma_{\text{failure}}}{\sigma_{\text{allow}}} \quad (FS>1) \qquad \sigma_{\text{allow}}=\frac{\sigma_{\text{failure}}}{FS} \qquad \text{design requires } \sigma_{\text{actual}}\le\sigma_{\text{allow}}$$

Worked example

Example. A steel bar has yield stress $\sigma_Y=250$ MPa. Using $FS=2$, find the allowable stress, then the maximum load for a 30 mm diameter bar.

$\sigma_{\text{allow}}=250/2=125$ MPa. $A=\pi(0.015)^2=7.069\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $P_{\max}=\sigma_{\text{allow}}A=125\times10^6\times7.069\times10^{-4}=88.4$ kN.

Practice 25

An aluminium member has ultimate stress $\sigma_u=310$ MPa. Using $FS=3$, find the allowable stress.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{allow}}=310/3=103.3$ MPa.

Practice 26

A cable has a breaking strength of 120 kN and carries an actual working load of 30 kN. Find the factor of safety.

Solution

$FS=120/30=4$.

Practice 27

A steel rod (yield stress 300 MPa, $FS=2.5$) must carry an axial load of 90 kN. Find the required diameter.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{allow}}=300/2.5=120$ MPa. $A=90000/120\times10^6=7.5\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $d=\sqrt{4A/\pi}=30.9$ mm.

Practice 28

A structural component made of a material with $\sigma_u=400$ MPa carries an actual stress of 100 MPa. Find the factor of safety.

Solution

$FS=400/100=4$.

Practice 29

Compare two design choices for a tension member: Material A ($\sigma_Y=250$ MPa, $FS=2$) vs Material B ($\sigma_Y=500$ MPa, $FS=2.5$). Which gives the higher allowable stress?

Solution

Material A: $\sigma_{\text{allow}}=125$ MPa. Material B: $\sigma_{\text{allow}}=200$ MPa. Material B gives the higher allowable stress despite its larger factor of safety, because its yield stress is proportionally much higher.

Practice 30

A bolt group must carry 200 kN total, shared by 4 bolts in single shear. If the allowable shear stress is 90 MPa, find the required bolt diameter (assume equal load sharing).

Solution

Load per bolt $=50$ kN. $A\ge50000/90\times10^6=5.556\times10^{-4}\ \text{m}^2$. $d=\sqrt{4A/\pi}=26.6$ mm.

Practice 31

A design code specifies $FS=1.67$ for a ductile steel with yield stress 345 MPa. Find the allowable stress, and the maximum axial load for a 50 mm × 50 mm square bar.

Solution

$\sigma_{\text{allow}}=345/1.67=206.6$ MPa. $A=2500\ \text{mm}^2=2.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{m}^2$. $P_{\max}=206.6\times10^6\times2.5\times10^{-3}=516.5$ kN.

Practice 32

Explain why a higher factor of safety is typically chosen for brittle materials (e.g. cast iron, concrete) compared to ductile materials (e.g. structural steel), even when their ultimate strengths might be comparable.

Solution

Ductile materials give visible warning before failure — they yield and deform substantially, allowing inspection or load-shedding before final fracture, and they can redistribute stress around local overloads (stress concentrations) through plastic flow. Brittle materials fail suddenly with little to no warning, and cannot relieve local stress concentrations plastically, so a larger safety margin is needed to compensate for this greater uncertainty and the more catastrophic nature of brittle failure.
