00Roadmap: from weak to strong

Every sufficient condition proved so far — Chapter 5's whole apparatus of Legendre and Jacobi conditions — guarantees only a weak minimum: $J[y]\le J[y^*]$ for curves $y^*$ close to $y$ in slope as well as position (close in the $\mathscr{D}_1$ norm). That leaves the door open for some wilder, sharply-oscillating curve — one that stays close to $y$ in height but swings its slope around violently — to sneak in underneath. Ruling that out is what a strong extremum demands: $J[y]\le J[y^*]$ for every curve $y^*$ merely close to $y$ in position (the $\mathscr{C}$ norm), with no restriction on the slope at all.

This is a strictly harder question, and it needs machinery Chapter 5 never had to reach for. The key new idea, foreshadowed all the way back at the start of Chapter 5's discussion of conjugate points, is to stop studying one extremal in isolation and instead study an entire family of extremals sweeping out a region — a field. Here's the itinerary:

  • Section 31 gives a general-purpose definition of a field for any system of second-order differential equations (not yet tied to a variational problem), built around the idea of boundary conditions that are consistent no matter which point you impose them at.
  • Section 32 specializes this to the Euler equations of a functional, introduces the notion of a self-adjoint boundary condition, and shows a field of a functional is governed by its own Hamilton–Jacobi-flavored system.
  • Section 33 builds Hilbert's invariant integral: a cleverly chosen line integral, defined using a field, whose value depends only on its endpoints — not on the path taken between them. This path-independence is the single fact the whole chapter has been building toward.
  • Section 34 cashes it in: comparing the original functional to Hilbert's invariant integral produces the Weierstrass E-function, and a sign condition on that one function turns out to be exactly the sufficient condition for a strong extremum that weak-extremum theory could never deliver.

Keep an eye, throughout, on how directly this generalizes an idea you already know: the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus says $\int_a^bf'(x)\,dx$ depends only on the endpoints $a,b$, not on how you get from one to the other (there's only one way to "get" from $a$ to $b$ in one dimension, but the principle — an integral of an exact differential is path-independent — is exactly what generalizes here to a genuine two-dimensional path-independence statement).

31Consistent Boundary Conditions. General Definition of a Field

This section deliberately steps back from the calculus of variations to set up a piece of pure ODE theory that variational problems will need. Consider a system of $n$ second-order differential equations, solved explicitly for the highest derivatives:
$$y_i'' = f_i(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n') \qquad (i=1,\dots,n). \tag{1}$$
A particular solution is picked out by $2n$ conditions — commonly, boundary conditions of the form
$$y_i' = \psi_i(y_1,\dots,y_n) \qquad (i=1,\dots,n) \tag{2}$$
imposed at two separate values $x_1,x_2$ (this is exactly the shape boundary conditions take in many variational problems — a relation pinning down the slope in terms of the position, at each end). If instead conditions (2) are imposed at just one point, they cut the $2n$-parameter family of all solutions of (1) down to an $n$-parameter family — one parameter's worth of freedom left over for each unknown function, exactly matching the freedom in choosing the initial positions $y_1,\dots,y_n$ themselves.

Definition 1

Boundary conditions $y_i'=\psi_i^{(1)}(y_1,\dots,y_n)$ prescribed at $x=x_1$, and $y_i'=\psi_i^{(2)}(y_1,\dots,y_n)$ prescribed at $x=x_2$, are mutually consistent if every solution of (1) satisfying the first set at $x_1$ automatically satisfies the second set at $x_2$, and conversely.

The word "conversely" is doing real work here: consistency is symmetric, and one way to think about it (a footnote in the book makes this explicit) is that the boundary condition at $x_1$ could equally well be replaced by the one at $x_2$ — each one, on its own, already carries the same information about which solution you've picked out. This is worth internalizing with a physical picture: imagine you're studying a vibrating string, but you only care about one piece of it. The rest of the string, plus whatever it's attached to, exerts some influence on your piece — and a consistent boundary condition is exactly a way of packaging "the effect of everything outside my region of interest" into a single relation at the cut point, without needing to model that outside world in any more detail.

Definition 2

Suppose boundary conditions $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ (with $\psi_i$ continuously differentiable) are prescribed at every $x$ in $[a,b]$, and suppose they're mutually consistent for every pair of points $x_1,x_2$ in $[a,b]$. Then this family of consistent boundary conditions is called a field (of directions) for the system (1).

So a field assigns, to every point $(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ in some region, a specific "recommended slope" $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y)$ — and the defining property is that these recommendations are globally consistent with each other: by the existence-and-uniqueness theorem for systems of differential equations, exactly one integral curve of the resulting first-order system $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ passes through each point of the region, and — crucially — that curve is also automatically a solution of the original second-order system (1). Picture it as a field of little arrows filling up a region, one arrow at each point, such that following the arrows from any starting point traces out a genuine solution curve of (1) — and no two of these traced-out curves ("trajectories," in the book's terminology) ever cross, since exactly one passes through each point.

Theorem

The first-order system $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ is a field for the second-order system (1) if and only if the functions $\psi_i$ satisfy the system of partial differential equations
$$\frac{\partial\psi_i}{\partial x}+\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{\partial\psi_i}{\partial y_k}\psi_k = f_i(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n), \tag{8}$$
called the Hamilton–Jacobi system for (1).

Proof. Differentiate $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ with respect to $x$ along a trajectory, using the chain rule and substituting $dy_k/dx=\psi_k$ (since we're differentiating along a curve that's itself a solution of the first-order system):
$$y_i'' = \frac{\partial\psi_i}{\partial x}+\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{\partial\psi_i}{\partial y_k}\frac{dy_k}{dx} = \frac{\partial\psi_i}{\partial x}+\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{\partial\psi_i}{\partial y_k}\psi_k.$$
So every trajectory of the first-order system automatically satisfies the second-order system (1) exactly when the right-hand side above matches $f_i(x,y,\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n)$ — which is precisely condition (8). $\blacksquare$

A first example: a single linear ODE. Take $y''=p(x)y$. The Hamilton–Jacobi system (8) becomes the single equation $\psi_x+\psi_y\psi=p(x)y$ (dropping the subscript $i$, since $n=1$), i.e. $\psi_x+\frac12(\psi^2)_y = p(x)y$. This has a whole family of solutions (one arbitrary function's worth of freedom), and every one of them gives a valid field — but the simplest are the ones linear in $y$: try $\psi(x,y)=\alpha(x)y$. Substituting, $\alpha'(x)y+\alpha^2(x)y=p(x)y$, so (dividing by $y$) $\alpha$ must satisfy
$$\alpha'(x)+\alpha^2(x)=p(x), \tag{12}$$
a Riccati equation — the same species of nonlinear first-order ODE that showed up in Chapter 5's Legendre-condition discussion, and it's no coincidence: both arise from exactly the same "try to linearize a second-order equation via a first-order substitution" strategy. Any solution $\alpha(x)$ of (12) produces the field $y'=\alpha(x)y$ for the original equation.

A second example: linear systems, and the matrix Riccati equation. The same idea, run with matrices instead of scalars, handles $Y''=P(x)Y$ for a vector $Y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)$ and matrix $P(x)$: trying a solution $\Psi=A(x)Y$ (linear in $Y$, with $A(x)$ a matrix of coefficients to be determined) turns the corresponding Hamilton–Jacobi system into the matrix Riccati equation
$$\frac{d}{dx}A(x)+A^2(x) = P(x),$$
exactly the matrix Riccati equation from Chapter 5, Section 29's proof of the multidimensional conjugate-point theorem — a satisfying confirmation that these two chapters' machinery is, underneath, the same machinery.

A glimpse ahead: fields and boundary value problems (the "sweep method"). It's worth knowing — even without needing the details later — that fields are intimately tied to solving boundary value problems, via a technique called the sweep method. For a single equation $y''=p(x)y+f(x)$ with boundary conditions $y'(a)=c_0y(a)+d_0$ and $y'(b)=c_1y(b)+d_1$: build the first-order equation $y'=\alpha(x)y+\beta(x)$ whose solutions all satisfy the boundary condition at $a$ (forcing $\alpha(a)=c_0,\beta(a)=d_0$) and which is itself consistent with the original second-order equation (forcing $\alpha,\beta$ to solve a companion first-order system derived exactly the way (12) was derived above). Solving that companion system lets you "sweep" the boundary condition originally imposed at $a$ forward to any point in $[a,b]$ — in particular to $b$, where it combines with the boundary condition already given there to pin down $y(b)$ and $y'(b)$ uniquely (the forward sweep); then integrating the first-order equation backward from $b$ recovers $y(x)$ everywhere (the backward sweep). This has real practical value — it turns a two-point boundary value problem into two separate initial-value problems, which are dramatically easier to solve numerically — and the whole method is, quietly, nothing but the construction of two fields (one built from each end) whose common trajectory solves the original problem.

32The Field of a Functional

32.1 Self-adjoint boundary conditions

Now bring this machinery back to the calculus of variations. The Euler equations of $\int_a^bF(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n')\,dx$ are a second-order system, so Section 31's apparatus applies directly — but variational problems come with a piece of extra structure that not every second-order system has: the equations arise from a functional, and boundary conditions for such problems are frequently themselves generated by variation, taking a specific and constrained form.

Consider the variable-endpoint functional $\int_a^bF\,dx+g^{(1)}(a,y_1,\dots,y_n)+g^{(2)}(b,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ from Chapter 3's playbook. Computing its variation and setting it to zero (exactly as in Chapter 3, Section 14) forces, at the left endpoint,
$$\bigl(F_{y_i'}-g^{(1)}_{y_i}\bigr)\Big|_{x=a}=0 \qquad (i=1,\dots,n),$$
i.e., writing $p_i=F_{y_i'}$ as always,
$$p_i(x,y,y')\big|_{x=a} = g^{(1)}_{y_i}(x,y)\big|_{x=a}.$$
Since the matrix $\|F_{y_i'y_k'}\|$ is (generically) nonzero, this relation can be solved for $y_1'(a),\dots,y_n'(a)$ as functions of $y_1(a),\dots,y_n(a)$ — producing a boundary condition of the specific form $y_i'(a)=\psi_i(y(a))$.

Definition 1

Boundary conditions $y_i'=\psi_i(y)$ (with momenta $p_i=F_{y_i'}$) prescribed at $x=a$ are self-adjoint if there's a function $g(x,y)$ such that $p_i[x,y,\psi(y)]\big|_{x=a}\equiv g_{y_i}(x,y)\big|_{x=a}$.

In other words: self-adjointness says the boundary condition is exactly the kind that could have arisen from varying some endpoint-function $g$, as above — even if you weren't handed $g$ up front. This turns out to have a purely algebraic test:

Theorem 1

The boundary conditions $y_i'(a)=\psi_i(y(a))$ are self-adjoint if and only if
$$\frac{\partial p_i[x,y,\psi(y)]}{\partial y_k}\bigg|_{x=a} = \frac{\partial p_k[x,y,\psi(y)]}{\partial y_i}\bigg|_{x=a} \qquad (i,k=1,\dots,n). \tag{32}$$

This should look familiar: (32) is exactly the condition for a vector field $(p_1,\dots,p_n)$ (here, thought of as a function of $y_1,\dots,y_n$ alone, at the fixed value $x=a$) to be the gradient of some scalar potential $g$ — precisely the "mixed partials must match" test for a vector field to be conservative, the same criterion used to check whether a line integral is path-independent in a multivariable calculus course. (For $n=1$, note that (32) is vacuous — there's no second index to compare against — so every boundary condition is automatically self-adjoint when there's only one unknown function. Self-adjointness is a distinctly multi-function phenomenon.)

32.2 The field of a functional

Definition 2

Given a functional $\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$ with Euler equations $F_{y_i}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'}=0$, boundary conditions $y_i'=\psi_i^{(1)}(y)$ at $x=x_1$ and $y_i'=\psi_i^{(2)}(y)$ at $x=x_2$ are (mutually) consistent with respect to the functional if every extremal satisfying the first set at $x_1$ also satisfies the second set at $x_2$, and conversely.

Definition 3

A family of boundary conditions $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y)$ prescribed for every $x\in[a,b]$ is a field of the functional if (1) the conditions are self-adjoint at every $x$, and (2) the conditions are consistent for every pair of points $x_1,x_2$ in $[a,b]$.

So a field of a functional is exactly a field (in Section 31's general sense) for its Euler equations — with the extra structural requirement that self-adjointness hold everywhere. Its trajectories — the solutions of $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y)$ — form an $n$-parameter family of extremals, one through each point of the region.

Theorem 2

gives the effective test, folding self-adjointness (32) and consistency into a single package: the family $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y)$ is a field of the functional if and only if, at every point,
$$\frac{\partial p_i}{\partial y_k}=\frac{\partial p_k}{\partial y_i} \qquad \text{(self-adjointness)}, \qquad \frac{\partial p_i}{\partial x}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i} \qquad \text{(consistency)}, \tag{38,39}$$
where $p_i=F_{y_i'}(x,y,\psi(x,y))$ and $H$ is the Hamiltonian $-F+\sum_ip_iy_i'$ evaluated along the field. (The proof is a direct, if somewhat involved, chain-rule computation checking that these two conditions on $\psi_i$ exactly reproduce the Hamilton–Jacobi system (8) of Section 31, specialized to the Euler-equation system — worth knowing exists, though we won't reproduce every line of it here.) Theorem 3 records a useful byproduct: the quantity $\partial p_i/\partial y_k - \partial p_k/\partial y_i$ stays constant along each trajectory of any consistent field — so if self-adjointness (32) holds at even one point of a trajectory, it holds along the whole trajectory automatically. This is a real time-saver: to verify that a consistent family of boundary conditions defines a field of the functional, you only need to check self-adjointness at a single point per trajectory, not everywhere.

The Hamilton–Jacobi equation returns, in a new role. Here's where the payoff of self-adjointness becomes clear. Since a self-adjoint boundary condition $p_i[x,y,\psi(x,y)]=g_{y_i}(x,y)$ at every point is, by definition, generated from a single potential function $g(x,y)$ — rather than $n$ separate functions $\psi_i$ chosen independently — the question "when does the consistency condition (39) hold?" can be asked directly in terms of $g$ instead of $\psi$:

Theorem 4

The boundary conditions defined via $p_i[x,y,\psi(x,y)]=g_{y_i}(x,y)$ are consistent — i.e., define a field of the functional — if and only if $g(x,y)$ satisfies
$$\frac{\partial g}{\partial x}+H\left(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,\frac{\partial g}{\partial y_1},\dots,\frac{\partial g}{\partial y_n}\right) = 0. \tag{51}$$

This is exactly the Hamilton–Jacobi equation from Chapter 4, Section 23 — the same PDE that, there, governed the geodetic distance function $S$. What's happened is a satisfying closing of a loop: Chapter 4 introduced this equation as the governing PDE for a specific function ($S$, built by integrating $F$ along extremals from a fixed point); this chapter arrives at the same equation from a completely different direction, as the condition for a whole family of boundary directions $\psi_i$ (built from a single potential $g$, exactly the way $p_i=\partial S/\partial y_i$ packaged the momenta in Section 23) to knit together into a field. A field of a functional is, in this precise sense, the same object as a solution of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation — just viewed from the "family of directions" side rather than the "single scalar function" side.

Central fields. One especially clean way fields arise in practice: Theorem 5 shows that if every extremal of the functional through a fixed point $c$ (lying just outside the region of interest) sweeps out, as its initial direction varies, an entire region $R$ exactly once — a central field — then the resulting family of directions automatically satisfies both consistency and self-adjointness, i.e., automatically is a field of the functional. (The proof identifies the relevant potential $g(x,y)$ directly: it's the value of $J$ integrated along the extremal from $c$ to $(x,y)$ — literally the geodetic-distance function $S$ from Chapter 4 all over again, now playing the role of the field's generating potential.) And Theorem 6 gives the payoff that makes this all worth having: if $\det\|F_{y_i'y_k'}\|$ is nonvanishing along an extremal $\gamma$, and $\gamma$ contains no points conjugate to its starting point, then $\gamma$ can be embedded in a field — i.e., surrounded by a whole family of nearby, non-crossing extremals covering a neighborhood of $\gamma$, with $\gamma$ itself as one of the trajectories. This is precisely why Chapter 5's Jacobi condition earns a second life here: "no conjugate points" isn't just a technical ingredient in the weak-extremum sufficient conditions — it is exactly the condition that lets an extremal be surrounded by a field, which is the raw material every construction in the rest of this chapter needs.

33Hilbert's Invariant Integral
Background primer

A primer: path-independent line integrals

If a vector field $(P,Q)$ in the plane satisfies $\partial P/\partial y=\partial Q/\partial x$ everywhere in a simply connected region, then $P\,dx+Q\,dy$ is an exact differential — meaning there's a scalar function $g(x,y)$ with $g_x=P,\ g_y=Q$ — and the line integral $\int_\Gamma(P\,dx+Q\,dy)$, taken along any curve $\Gamma$ from a fixed point $M_0$ to a variable point $M$, depends only on the two endpoints, not on the path connecting them (this is the direct two-dimensional generalization of the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, and it's exactly the fact self-adjointness was engineered to trigger). The value of the integral is simply $g(M)-g(M_0)$.

Now put this to work. Let $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y)$ be a field of the functional $\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$, satisfying, by Theorem 2 of Section 32, both the self-adjointness conditions $\partial p_i/\partial y_k=\partial p_k/\partial y_i$ and the consistency conditions $\partial p_i/\partial x = -\partial H/\partial y_i$. Consider the differential expression (in the $(n+1)$-dimensional space of points $(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$)
$$-H\,dx+\sum_{i=1}^np_i\,dy_i,$$
where $p_i=p_i[x,y,\psi(x,y)]$ and $H=H[x,y,\psi(x,y)]$ are both now genuine functions of $x,y_1,\dots,y_n$ alone (the field has been substituted in, eliminating the $y_i'$ dependence). Checking that this is an exact differential means checking that the coefficient of $dy_k$, differentiated with respect to $y_i$, matches the coefficient of $dy_i$, differentiated with respect to $y_k$ (that's self-adjointness, directly), and that the coefficient of $dy_i$, differentiated with respect to $x$, matches minus the coefficient of $dx$, differentiated with respect to $y_i$ (that's exactly consistency). Both hold, by hypothesis — so this expression is an exact differential, and its line integral is path-independent:
$$g(x,y) = \int_\Gamma\left(-H\,dx+\sum_{i=1}^np_i\,dy_i\right), \tag{61}$$
evaluated along any curve $\Gamma$ from a fixed base point $M_0=(x_0,y(x_0))$ to a variable point $M=(x,y)$, depends only on $M_0$ and $M$. The right-hand side of (61) is called Hilbert's invariant integral.

Rewriting it with $F$ explicit. Substituting the definitions $p_i=F_{y_i'}[x,y,\psi(x,y)]$ and $H=-F+\sum_ip_i\psi_i$ (both evaluated along the field) directly into (61) gives the more explicit, and more useful, form
$$\int_\Gamma\left(\Bigl\{F[x,y,\psi(x,y)]-\sum_{i=1}^n\psi_i(x,y)F_{y_i'}[x,y,\psi(x,y)]\Bigr\}\,dx + \sum_{i=1}^nF_{y_i'}[x,y,\psi(x,y)]\,dy_i\right). \tag{62}$$

The crucial special case: $\Gamma$ is a trajectory of the field. If the curve $\Gamma$ happens to be one of the field's own trajectories, then along it $dy_i=\psi_i\,dx$ by definition — and substituting this in, the bracketed term and the $dy_i$ term recombine: $\bigl(F-\sum\psi_iF_{y_i'}\bigr)dx+\sum F_{y_i'}\psi_i\,dx = F\,dx$, so (62) collapses all the way down to
$$\int_\Gamma F(x,y,y')\,dx$$
— literally the original functional itself, evaluated along that trajectory.

Put these two facts together, and here is the payoff the whole chapter has been building toward: since Hilbert's invariant integral (62) is path-independent, and it equals the functional $J$ itself when evaluated along a trajectory of the field, its value must be exactly the same whether you compute it along a trajectory (giving $J$) or along any other curve with the same endpoints (giving (62)'s value some other way). This lets you rewrite the value of $J$ along one particular extremal as an integral computed along an entirely different curve — the single fact that makes comparing $J[\gamma]$ against $J[\gamma^*]$, for a wildly different nearby curve $\gamma^*$, tractable at all.

34The Weierstrass E-Function. Sufficient Conditions for a Strong Extremum
Definition

For the functional $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$ with $y(a)=A,\ y(b)=B$, the Weierstrass E-function is the function of $3n+1$ variables
$$E(x,y,z,w) = F(x,y,w)-F(x,y,z)-\sum_{i=1}^n(w_i-z_i)F_{y_i'}(x,y,z). \tag{64}$$

Think of $z$ as "the slope the field recommends here" and $w$ as "the slope some rival curve actually has here" — $E$ measures the gap between them. Concretely, $E(x,y,z,w)$ is exactly the difference between $F$'s actual value at the point $w$ and the value predicted by the first-order Taylor approximation to $F$ (as a function of its last $n$ arguments) built at the point $z$: it's the Taylor remainder, made explicit. Equivalently, by Taylor's theorem with the Lagrange remainder form,
$$E(x,y,z,w) = \frac12\sum_{i,k=1}^n(w_i-z_i)(w_k-z_k)F_{y_i'y_k'}[x,y,z+\theta(w-z)] \qquad (0<\theta<1). \tag{\text{Taylor form}}$$

A geometric picture, for $n=1$. If you plot $F(x,y,z)$ as a function of $z$ alone (holding $x,y$ fixed) — call this curve $\Gamma$ — then $F(x,y,w)-F(x,y,z)-(w-z)F_{y'}(x,y,z)$ is exactly the vertical gap between $\Gamma$ itself, evaluated at $w$, and the tangent line to $\Gamma$ at $z$, extended out to $w$. So $E\ge0$ everywhere is exactly the geometric statement that $\Gamma$ lies above every one of its own tangent lines — which you may recognize as precisely the definition of a convex function (compare this directly to the convexity discussion that opened the Legendre transformation in Chapter 4, Section 18 — the E-function is, at bottom, the same "graph lies above its tangent lines" idea, now packaged as a sufficient condition rather than a duality tool).

Setting up the comparison. Recall the three conditions already known (Chapter 5, Sections 28–29) to be sufficient for a weak minimum at an extremal $\gamma$: (1) $\gamma$ is an extremal; (2) $\|F_{y_i'y_k'}\|$ is positive definite along $\gamma$; (3) $(a,b)$ has no points conjugate to $a$. Since every strong extremum is automatically a weak one (though never the reverse — a fact worth remembering, since it's easy to think a wrong direction into this), it's natural to assume these three hold from the outset, and ask what more is needed for a strong extremum.

Conditions (2) and (3) are exactly Theorem 6 of Section 32's hypotheses — so $\gamma$ can be embedded in a field $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y)$. Let $\gamma^*$ be any admissible curve with the same endpoints as $\gamma$, lying in the region covered by the field (no restriction whatsoever on $\gamma^*$'s slope — this is exactly the strong-extremum setting). Using Hilbert's invariant integral's path-independence (Section 33) to rewrite $J[\gamma]$ as the value of (62) evaluated along $\gamma^*$ instead of along $\gamma$, and then subtracting, the increment collapses — after the algebra the book carries out explicitly — into a strikingly clean form:
$$\Delta J = J[\gamma^*]-J[\gamma] = \int_{\gamma^*}E(x,y,\psi,y')\,dx. \tag{67}$$
Every bit of the messy Taylor-expansion bookkeeping from Chapters 1 and 5 has been absorbed into this one integral of the E-function, evaluated with $z=\psi(x,y)$ (the field's recommended slope at each point of $\gamma^*$) and $w=y'$ (the actual slope of $\gamma^*$ there).

Theorem 1

Let $\gamma$ be an extremal embeddable in a field $y_i'=\psi_i(x,y)$ covering a region containing $\gamma$. If $E(x,y,\psi,w)\ge0$ for every point $(x,y)$ in that region and every finite vector $w$, then $J[y]$ has a strong minimum at $\gamma$.

Proof. Immediate from (67): if the integrand is nonnegative pointwise along $\gamma^*$, the integral $\Delta J$ is nonnegative — and this holds for any admissible $\gamma^*$ sufficiently close to $\gamma$ in the $\mathscr{C}$ norm (close enough to stay inside the field's region), with no constraint on $\gamma^*$'s slope whatsoever. That absence of any slope restriction is exactly what makes this a strong, rather than merely weak, minimum. $\blacksquare$

This is worth pausing on, because it's the entire payoff of the chapter: Chapters 1 through 5 could only ever compare $\gamma$ to curves with nearby slopes, because every argument leaned on Taylor-expanding in $h'$ as well as $h$, and small $\|h\|_1$ was needed to keep $h'$ under control. Hilbert's invariant integral sidesteps that constraint entirely, by rewriting the comparison as an integral along $\gamma^*$ itself — using only $\gamma^*$'s own slope at each point, never comparing it to a "nearby" slope on $\gamma$ — which is exactly why $\gamma^*$'s slope is now free to be anything at all.

Necessity, too. The condition isn't just sufficient — it's necessary as well:

Theorem 2 (Weierstrass's necessary condition)

If $J[y]$ has a strong minimum at $\gamma$, then $E(x,y,y',w)\ge0$ along $\gamma$, for every finite $w$.

The idea of the proof: if $E$ were negative at some point $\xi$ on $\gamma$ for some particular direction $q$, you could build an admissible curve $\gamma^*$ that mostly hugs $\gamma$ but detours briefly near $\xi$ to travel in direction $q$ for a short stretch (a sharp "corner," made possible precisely because a strong extremum permits comparison curves with arbitrary, non-nearby slopes) — and a careful estimate (worked out in the practice problems below) shows this detour makes $\Delta J<0$, contradicting the assumed minimum. Since $E\ge0$ is necessary along the actual extremal, while Theorem 1 needs it along the whole field surrounding the extremal, there's a small gap between the necessary and sufficient versions here — a gap of the same general character as Legendre's ordinary versus strengthened condition back in Chapter 5, and one that's unavoidable without further hypotheses.

Chapter recap

This chapter closed the gap Chapter 5 deliberately left open: turning necessary conditions for a weak extremum into sufficient conditions for a strong one. The route ran entirely through the idea of a field — a family of extremals, one through each point of a region, that never cross each other. Section 31 defined a field for a general second-order system, purely in ODE terms, via mutually consistent boundary conditions, and showed a field is characterized by the Hamilton–Jacobi system for that system. Section 32 specialized this to variational problems, introducing self-adjoint boundary conditions (the calculus-of-variations incarnation of "conservative vector field"), and showed — satisfyingly — that a field of a functional is governed by exactly the Hamilton–Jacobi equation from Chapter 4, now reappearing as the consistency condition for a whole family of directions built from one potential function; Theorem 6 then delivered the crucial existence result, that any extremal free of conjugate points (Chapter 5's Jacobi condition, again) can always be embedded in such a field. Section 33 built Hilbert's invariant integral: because a field's momenta and Hamiltonian package into an exact differential, the corresponding line integral is path-independent, and it reduces to the original functional itself along any trajectory of the field. Section 34 used that path-independence to rewrite the increment $\Delta J$ between an extremal and an arbitrary, arbitrarily-sloped nearby curve as a single integral of the Weierstrass E-function — the exact Taylor remainder of $F$ as a function of slope alone — and showed $E\ge0$ throughout the field is sufficient (and, along the extremal itself, necessary) for a genuine strong extremum. Together, Chapters 5 and 6 now give the complete classical answer to "is this extremal actually a minimum?": Euler's equation, the strengthened Legendre and Jacobi conditions for a weak minimum, and — one field-construction and one E-function sign check further — the same conditions promoted all the way to a strong one. The next chapter, outside the scope of this set of lessons, extends the whole theory from curves to surfaces: variational problems for multiple integrals.

Practice problems
Problem 1

1. (A strong minimum via direct computation of $\Delta J$.) Find the curve joining $(-1,-1)$ and $(1,1)$ that minimizes $J[y]=\int_{-1}^1(x^2y'^2+12y^2)\,dx$, and determine the nature of the minimum.

Hint: First find the extremal by solving the Euler equation (the book's hint reveals the answer is $y=x^3$ — verify this satisfies $F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ for this $F$ directly). Rather than building a field and computing an E-function, this particular functional yields to a shortcut: compute $\Delta J=J[y+h]-J[y]$ directly and show it simplifies (using $y=x^3$ explicitly and the boundary conditions on $h$) to $\Delta J=\int_{-1}^1(x^2h'^2+12h^2)\,dx>0$ for every nonzero admissible $h$ — a manifestly nonnegative integrand, valid for any admissible $h$ regardless of size, which is exactly a direct, from-scratch proof of a strong minimum without invoking fields or the E-function machinery at all. (This works here because the functional happens to be exactly quadratic in $y,y'$ — for such functionals, $\Delta J$ equals $\delta^2J$ exactly, with no higher-order remainder to control.)

Problem 2

2. (Same technique, a different functional.) Find the curve joining $(1,3)$ and $(2,5)$ minimizing $J[y]=\int_1^2y'(1+x^2y')\,dx$, and determine the nature of the minimum.

Hint: Again compute $\Delta J$ directly rather than reaching for the E-function — this functional is also quadratic in $y'$ (with no $y$-dependence at all), so the same "$\Delta J$ collapses to an explicitly nonnegative integral" trick applies. Find the extremal first (Euler's equation for an $F$ with no $y$-dependence just says $F_{y'}=$ const, from Chapter 1's Case 3), then substitute $y=\hat y+h$ into $J$ and simplify $\Delta J$ by hand.

Problem 3

3. (Weak but not strong.) Prove that the segment of the $x$-axis from $x=0$ to $x=\pi$ gives a weak, but not a strong, minimum of $J[y]=\int_0^\pi y^2(1-y'^2)\,dx$, with $y(0)=y(\pi)=0$.

Hint: Along $y\equiv0$, direct substitution shows $F=F_y=F_{y'}=0$, so the Euler equation and both Chapter 5 sufficient conditions (check $F_{y'y'}=-2y^2$ along $y\equiv0$, which is identically $0$ — technically only the non-strengthened Legendre condition, but the weak-minimum argument survives with $y\equiv0$ trivially) can be checked to hold, giving a weak minimum. To break strength, try the specific family of rapidly-oscillating comparison curves $y=\frac{1}{\sqrt n}\sin nx$ (small in the $\mathscr{C}$-norm as $n\to\infty$, since the amplitude $1/\sqrt n\to0$, but with slope $\sqrt n\cos nx$ that does not shrink — exactly a "small in position, wild in slope" curve, the signature move for defeating a would-be strong minimum) and show $J$ evaluated on this family goes negative for large $n$, directly contradicting strong-minimality no matter how small a $\mathscr{C}$-neighborhood you were hoping would save it.

Problem 4

4. (Sign condition guarantees strong minima automatically.) Prove that the extrema of $\int_a^bn(x,y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$ are always strong minima whenever $n(x,y)>0$ for all $x,y$.

Hint: Compute the E-function directly from definition (64), using $F(x,y,z)=n(x,y)\sqrt{1+z^2}$: you'll need $F_{y'}=nz/\sqrt{1+z^2}$, and after substituting into (64) and simplifying (using the algebraic identity relating $\sqrt{1+w^2},\sqrt{1+z^2}$, and $wz$ that falls out of the Cauchy–Schwarz-type inequality $\sqrt{1+w^2}\sqrt{1+z^2}\ge 1+wz$), you should find $E(x,y,z,w)=n(x,y)\bigl[\sqrt{1+w^2}\sqrt{1+z^2}-1-wz\bigr]/\sqrt{1+z^2}$, which is manifestly $\ge0$ (given $n>0$) for every $z,w$ — not just $z,w$ close together — since the bracketed factor is a rearrangement of exactly that Cauchy–Schwarz-type inequality. Since $E\ge0$ holds unconditionally, for every $z,w$ everywhere (not merely along one particular field), Theorem 1 applies directly with no need to check the conjugate-point condition at all.

Problem 5

5. (A batch of concrete extremum classifications.) Investigate the extrema of: (a) $J[y]=\int_{-1}^2y'(1+x^2y')\,dx$, $y(-1)=1,y(2)=1$; (b) $J[y]=\int_0^{\pi/4}(4y^2-y'^2+8y)\,dx$, $y(0)=-1,y(\pi/4)=0$; (c) $J[y]=\int_1^2(x^2y'^2+12y^2)\,dx$, $y(1)=1,y(2)=8$; (d) $J[y]=\int_0^1(y'^2+y^2+2ye^{2x})\,dx$, $y(0)=\frac13,y(1)=\frac13e^2$.

Hint: The book's answers: (b) has a strong maximum at $y=\sin2x-1$; (d) has a strong minimum at $y=\frac13e^{2x}$. For each part, first solve the Euler equation to find the candidate extremal, then check Legendre's condition (the sign of $F_{y'y'}$ tells you immediately whether to expect a max or a min), then either compute $\Delta J$ directly (parts (a) and (c) are, like Problems 1–2, exactly quadratic, so this direct route works cleanly) or verify the E-function's sign is unconditional the way Problem 4 did (worth checking whether (b) and (d)'s integrands have the same convenient unconditional-sign structure).

Problem 6

6. (Weak minimum that is not strong, this time by direct E-function inspection.) Prove that $y=bx/a$ gives a weak, but not strong, minimum of $J[y]=\int_0^ay'^3\,dx$, with $y(0)=0,y(a)=b$ ($a,b>0$), by examining the corresponding Weierstrass E-function.

Hint: With $F=z^3$ (writing $z$ for $y'$ along the extremal, which here is a constant, $z=b/a$), compute $E(x,y,z,w)=w^3-z^3-(w-z)\cdot3z^2 = w^3-3z^2w+2z^3$. Factor this cubic in $w$ (it has a known root at $w=z$, by construction, so divide it out): you should find $E=(w-z)^2(w+2z)$. Since $(w-z)^2\ge0$ always, the sign of $E$ is entirely controlled by the sign of $(w+2z)$ — which is positive for $w$ near $z=b/a>0$ (confirming the weak minimum, where only nearby slopes $w$ are ever compared), but goes negative once $w<-2z$, i.e. for slopes far enough in the negative direction — exactly the kind of wild, far-away slope a strong-extremum comparison curve is allowed to have but a weak one is not. This single factored formula is a clean, self-contained illustration of exactly how a weak minimum can fail to be strong: the E-function's sign holds in a neighborhood of the extremal's own slope but not globally.
