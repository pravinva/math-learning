00Roadmap: a variational problem in engineer's clothing

This final appendix sketches, without most of the proofs, a theory developed by L. S. Pontryagin and his students in the 1950s: optimal control theory, the mathematics behind questions like "what's the fastest way to bring a rocket to a stop?" or "what steering strategy uses the least fuel?" At first glance the setup looks like a different subject entirely — differential equations with a "steering" input, a "control region" the steering is confined to, and a maximum principle rather than an Euler equation. The point of this appendix is to show that it is not a different subject: with the right dictionary, optimal control is a variant of the calculus of variations, and its central result — the maximum principle — specializes exactly to the classical Euler equations (and to the Weierstrass E-function) once the control problem is narrowed back down to the classical setting.

  • Section 1 sets up the vocabulary of control theory: phase space, control functions, trajectories, and the optimal control problem itself.
  • Section 2 translates this vocabulary back into the calculus of variations: a control problem is a minimum problem subject to nonholonomic constraints, and the classical variational problem is recovered as a special case.
  • Section 3 builds the control-theoretic analogue of canonical variables — a "conjugate" system $\psi$ and a function $\Pi$ playing the role $F$ and $H$ played before.
  • Section 4 states the maximum principle itself, the necessary condition that replaces Euler's equation here, and shows it reduces to the ordinary Euler equations in the classical case.
  • Section 5 shows the maximum principle also reduces to the Weierstrass necessary condition from Chapter 6 — so the two most powerful necessary conditions in the whole book turn out to be a single theorem, viewed from two different levels of generality.
Background primer

A primer: control systems and phase space (what you need, briefly)

Picture a physical system — a rocket, a chemical reactor, an economy — whose instantaneous state is captured by $n$ numbers $x^1,\dots,x^n$ (position and velocity, concentrations, whatever the relevant quantities are), collected into a vector $x=(x^1,\dots,x^n)$ living in an $n$-dimensional phase space $\mathscr X$ (the word "phase" here is used exactly as in Chapter 4's phase space of positions-and-momenta, though the coordinates here need not be positions and momenta specifically — just "whatever numbers describe the current state completely"). The system evolves according to a system of ODEs
$$\frac{dx^i}{dt} = f^i(x^1,\dots,x^n,u^1,\dots,u^k) \qquad (i=1,\dots,n), \tag{1}$$
but with a twist: the right-hand side depends not just on the state $x$ but also on $k$ extra numbers $u=(u^1,\dots,u^k)$, the control — quantities you (the engineer, or the pilot) get to choose freely at every instant, within some fixed control region $\Omega\subset\mathbb R^k$ (a thrust that can't exceed some maximum, a steering angle confined to some range, and so on). Once you specify a control function $u(t)$ (its values confined to $\Omega$), the system (1) becomes an ordinary system of ODEs, and — given an initial state $x_0$ — has a definite solution, the trajectory. The whole package — a control function $u(t)$, a time interval $[t_0,t_1]$, and an initial state $x_0$ — is called a control process, denoted $U=\{u(t),t_0,t_1,x_0\}$.

01Statement of the Problem

Given the setup above, attach to each control process $U$ a number measuring its "cost" (or, for a maximization problem, its "payoff"): fix a function $f^0(x,u)$ (continuously differentiable, together with its $x$-partials) and define
$$J[U] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}f^0(x,u)\,dt, \tag{4}$$
a genuine functional, now defined on the set of all control processes rather than on a space of curves directly. A control process $U=\{u(t),t_0,t_1,x_0\}$ is called optimal if $J[U]\le J[U^*]$ for every other admissible control process $U^*$ carrying the same starting point $x_0$ to the same destination $x_1$ — i.e., whose trajectory $x^*(t)$ satisfies $x^*(t_1^*)=x_1$ (matching endpoints, exactly as in the classical fixed-endpoint problem, but now the "curve" being compared is a trajectory generated by an admissible control, not an arbitrary admissible function). The admissible control processes are those whose control functions take values in $\Omega$ and are bounded and piecewise continuous (with one-sided limits at every discontinuity — allowing sudden, discontinuous jumps in the control, which is physically completely reasonable: a steering wheel can be snapped from one position to another, even though the resulting trajectory $x(t)$ stays continuous throughout, since it's built by integrating an ODE).

A special case worth keeping in mind throughout: the time-optimal problem. When $f^0\equiv1$, functional (4) collapses to $\int_{t_0}^{t_1}dt = t_1-t_0$, the elapsed time — so "optimal" simply means "fastest": find the control steering the system from $x_0$ to $x_1$ in the least possible time. This special case — sometimes called the fastest-motion or time-optimal problem — is simple enough to work by hand, and every worked example and practice problem below specializes to it.

02Relation to the Calculus of Variations

The connection promised in the roadmap is now straightforward to make precise. The integral $\int_{t_0}^{t_1}f^0(x,u)\,dt$ defining $J[U]$ can be viewed as an ordinary functional depending on $n+k$ functions $x^1,\dots,x^n,u^1,\dots,u^k$ of $t$ — i.e., a functional on curves in $(n+k+1)$-dimensional space (the extra "$+1$" for the variable $t$ itself). The system (1) relating these $n+k$ functions to each other is then exactly a system of nonholonomic constraints, in precisely Chapter 2, Section 12.2's sense: an algebraic-looking relation involving the derivatives $dx^i/dt$, imposed at every instant, rather than one single global (isoperimetric-style) number. And the boundary conditions "start at $x_0$, end at $x_1$" become the requirement that the endpoints of the admissible curve, in this $(n+k+1)$-dimensional space, lie on two specific hyperplanes (one at $t=t_0$ fixing $x^1,\dots,x^n$ to the values $x_0^1,\dots,x_0^n$, one at $t=t_1$ fixing them to $x_1^1,\dots,x_1^n$ — with the control coordinates $u^1,\dots,u^k$ left completely free at both ends, since nothing constrains the control's own value at the endpoints).

So: an optimal control problem is a minimum problem subject to nonholonomic constraints — the same species of problem Chapter 2, Section 12.2 already handled, in a more elaborate setting. The one distinctly new feature, not present in Chapter 2's treatment, is that the control functions $u^1(t),\dots,u^k(t)$ are required to take values in a fixed region $\Omega$ (and are allowed to be discontinuous), whereas Chapter 2's admissible functions were always continuously differentiable with no such range restriction — this is the single feature of the theory that forces a wholly new necessary condition (the maximum principle) rather than letting the classical Euler-Lagrange machinery apply unchanged.

Recovering the classical problem as a special case. To see the fit precisely, take the simplest classical $n$-dimensional variational problem: among curves joining two fixed points $(x_0^1,\dots,x_0^n)$ and $(x_1^1,\dots,x_1^n)$, minimize
$$\int_{t_0}^{t_1}f^0\left(x^1,\dots,x^n,\frac{dx^1}{dt},\dots,\frac{dx^n}{dt}\right)dt \tag{5}$$
(an integrand with no explicit $t$-dependence — no real loss of generality, since any functional can be brought to this form by passing to the parametric form of the problem, exactly Chapter 2, Section 10's device). This is exactly an optimal control problem: take $u^i=dx^i/dt$ as the controls (with $\Omega$ all of $k$-dimensional space, $k=n$, since the classical problem places no bound on the curve's slope), and the system (1) simply becomes $dx^i/dt=u^i$ — the trivial ODE relating each "position" coordinate to its own "control." Every classical fixed-endpoint problem, in other words, sits inside optimal control theory as the special case where the control region is unrestricted and the dynamics (1) merely define the controls as the state's own derivatives.

03Necessary Conditions for Optimality

To build the control-theoretic analogue of canonical variables, first fold the cost integrand into the dynamics itself. Introduce one extra state variable $x^0$, governed by $dx^0/dt=f^0(x,u)$ with initial condition $x^0(t_0)=0$; then, trivially, $x^0(t_1)=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}f^0(x,u)\,dt=J[U]$ — the cost of the whole control process has been repackaged as the final value of one extra coordinate. Writing $\mathbf x(t)=(x^0(t),x(t))=(x^0,x^1,\dots,x^n)$ for this augmented $(n+1)$-dimensional state, the optimal control problem becomes: find the admissible control minimizing the final value $x^0(t_1)$ of the solution of the augmented system
$$\frac{dx^i}{dt} = f^i(x,u) \qquad (i=0,1,\dots,n) \tag{8}$$
(with $f^0$ playing double duty as both the zeroth dynamical equation and the running cost) subject to the given initial conditions on $x^0,\dots,x^n$.

The conjugate system. Now introduce $n+1$ new "adjoint" variables $\psi_0,\psi_1,\dots,\psi_n$ — the control-theoretic stand-ins for the momenta $p_i$ of Chapter 4, Section 16 — governed by
$$\frac{d\psi_i}{dt} = -\sum_{\alpha=0}^n\frac{\partial f^\alpha(x,u)}{\partial x^i}\,\psi_\alpha \qquad (i=0,1,\dots,n), \tag{9}$$
called the system conjugate to (8) — a linear ODE system in the $\psi_i$'s, with coefficients built from the $x$-partial derivatives of $f^\alpha$ evaluated along a given trajectory. (There's a clean geometric meaning behind the name: viewing $(\psi_0,\dots,\psi_n)$ as a covariant vector conjugate to $(x^0,\dots,x^n)$ in exactly Appendix I, Section 3's dual-space sense, equation (9) describes exactly how a fixed hyperplane $\sum_\alpha\psi_\alpha^0x^\alpha=c$ through the initial point "transports" itself along solutions of (8), continuing to satisfy $\sum_\alpha\psi_\alpha(t)x^\alpha(t)=c$ at every later time — the adjoint system is, in this precise sense, just bookkeeping for how a linear relationship among the state coordinates propagates forward in time.)

The function $\Pi$. Package $\boldsymbol\psi(t)=(\psi_0(t),\dots,\psi_n(t))$ and define
$$\Pi(\boldsymbol\psi,x,u) = \sum_{\alpha=0}^n\psi_\alpha f^\alpha(x,u). \tag{10}$$
In terms of $\Pi$, both (8) and (9) take the strikingly Hamiltonian-looking form
$$\frac{dx^i}{dt}=\frac{\partial\Pi}{\partial\psi_i}, \qquad \frac{d\psi_i}{dt}=-\frac{\partial\Pi}{\partial x^i}. \tag{11}$$

Why this is not yet the canonical system — and the fix. Equations (11) look exactly like Chapter 4, Section 16's canonical equations, but there's a real difference in meaning worth being precise about: the canonical system there was closed — the number of equations exactly matched the number of unknown functions ($x_i,p_i$), so the system could in principle be solved on its own. Here, (11) involves not only $\mathbf x$ and $\boldsymbol\psi$ but also the control $u$, an extra unknown function not governed by (11) at all — so (11) only becomes a closed, solvable system once $u$ has somehow already been specified. To build a genuine analogue of the canonical equations — one that is closed — the control has to be eliminated from $\Pi$ somehow, and the natural way to eliminate a free variable via optimization is to maximize it out:
$$\mathscr H(\boldsymbol\psi,x) = \sup_{u\in\Omega}\Pi(\boldsymbol\psi,x,u), \tag{12}$$
a genuine function of $\boldsymbol\psi,x$ alone, with no leftover $u$-dependence — the direct control-theoretic analogue of the Legendre transformation from Chapter 4, Section 18 (the transition from $\Pi$ to $\mathscr H$ here mirrors, term for term, the transition from $F$ to $H$ there — a supremum eliminating one variable in favor of a new one, exactly the Legendre-transform recipe, here applied to $u$ rather than to $y'$).

04The Maximum Principle
Theorem (the maximum principle; stated without proof)

Let $U=\{u(t),t_0,t_1,x_0\}$ be an admissible control process, with corresponding trajectory $\mathbf x(t)$ passing through $(0,x_0^1,\dots,x_0^n)$ at $t=0$ and satisfying $x^i(t_1)=x_1^i$ ($i=1,\dots,n$). If $U$ is optimal, then there exists a continuous vector function $\boldsymbol\psi(t)$ such that:

1. $\boldsymbol\psi(t)$ satisfies the conjugate system (9), evaluated along $x=x(t),u=u(t)$;

2. for every $t\in[t_0,t_1]$, the function $\Pi$ achieves its maximum over $u\in\Omega$ at the actual control value $u(t)$ in use:
$$\Pi[\boldsymbol\psi(t),x(t),u(t)] = \mathscr H[\boldsymbol\psi(t),x(t)]; \tag{13}$$

3. at $t=t_1$, $\psi_0(t_1)\le0$ and $\mathscr H[\boldsymbol\psi(t_1),x(t_1)]=0$ — and, in fact, both $\psi_0(t)$ and $\mathscr H[\boldsymbol\psi(t),\mathbf x(t)]$ turn out to be constant along the whole trajectory, so (14) can equivalently be checked at any $t\in[t_0,t_1]$, not just at $t_1$.

The name "maximum principle" names exactly condition 2: at every instant, whatever control is actually in use must be the one, among all controls available in $\Omega$ at that instant, that maximizes $\Pi$ — a strikingly concrete, pointwise criterion (in contrast to the Euler equation, which is a differential equation satisfied all at once along the whole curve). This is where the restriction of $u$ to a fixed region $\Omega$ really earns its keep: for problems where $\Omega$ has an interior (rather than being all of $k$-dimensional space), the maximizing $u$ frequently sits right on the boundary of $\Omega$ — the physical content behind the well-known engineering strategy of bang-bang control, where the optimal strategy is to run a control at its extreme allowed value, switching abruptly between extremes rather than settling at some interior value, exactly the kind of behavior an ordinary calculus stationarity condition ($\partial\Pi/\partial u=0$) could never produce on its own, since a maximum on the boundary of a region need not be a stationary point of the function at all.

A prescription for finding the optimal trajectory. In practice, the maximum principle is used exactly the way Euler's equation is used classically: for each fixed $\boldsymbol\psi,x$, solve for the $u\in\Omega$ maximizing $\Pi$ — if this determines $u$ as a genuine function $u=u(\boldsymbol\psi,x)$ (as it typically does), substituting back into (8)–(9) produces a closed system of $2(n+1)$ equations in $2(n+1)$ unknown functions — the control-theoretic analogue of Chapter 4, Section 16's canonical system, now actually solvable in the same sense that system was.

Recovering the classical Euler equations. Specialize to the classical case from Section 2 ($u^i=dx^i/dt$, $\Omega$ unrestricted): here $\Pi(\boldsymbol\psi,x,u)=\psi_0f^0(x,u)+\sum_{i=1}^n\psi_iu^i$, and (11) reads $dx^0/dt=f^0(x,u)$, $dx^i/dt=u^i$, $d\psi_0/dt=0$, $d\psi_i/dt=-\psi_0\,\partial f^0/\partial x^i$. Since $\Omega$ is unrestricted, maximizing $\Pi$ over $u$ is a plain unconstrained calculus problem, so the maximum occurs at an interior stationary point: $\partial\Pi/\partial u^i=\psi_0\,\partial f^0/\partial u^i+\psi_i=0$, i.e. $\psi_i=-\psi_0\,\partial f^0/\partial u^i$. Since $\psi_0$ is constant ($d\psi_0/dt=0$), differentiating this relation and combining with the $\psi_i$-equation above gives directly
$$\frac{d}{dt}\left[\frac{\partial f^0(x,u)}{\partial u^i}\right] = \frac{\partial f^0(x,u)}{\partial x^i}, \qquad \frac{dx^i}{dt}=u^i \qquad (i=1,\dots,n)$$
— exactly the system of Euler equations for functional (5), reduced to first-order form by introducing $u^i=dx^i/dt$ as auxiliary functions, precisely Chapter 1, Section 5's own device for turning a second-order Euler equation into a first-order system. So the maximum principle, specialized to the case with no control restriction, reduces exactly to the classical Euler equations — confirming that Section 4's theorem really is the direct generalization it was advertised to be, not merely an analogy.

Two ways to describe the same propagation, once more. This is a good moment to recall Appendix I's closing remark: every propagation process (there, of a physical disturbance; here, of an optimally-controlled trajectory) admits two dual descriptions — one in terms of the trajectories themselves (leading to ordinary differential equations: the canonical system, or here its control-theoretic analogue), and one in terms of the evolving "wave front" (leading to a partial differential equation: the Hamilton–Jacobi equation there). The maximum principle, studying trajectories directly, is the direct analogue of the first approach; a genuine "wave front" approach to optimal control also exists (developed by R. Bellman, under the name dynamic programming), though it goes beyond what's covered here.

05Relation to Weierstrass' Necessary Condition

The final connection this appendix draws ties the maximum principle back to Chapter 6's Weierstrass E-function. Stay with the classical special case (16)–(17) from Section 4, and recall from (20) the E-function
$$E(x,x',z) = f^0(x,z)-f^0(x,x')-\sum_{i=1}^n(z_i-x_i')f^0_{x_i'}(x,x'). \tag{20}$$
A direct computation — substituting the formula for $\Pi$ from Section 4 and simplifying using the interior-maximum relation $\psi_i=-\psi_0f^0_{u^i}$ derived there — shows
$$\Pi(\boldsymbol\psi,x,z) - \Pi(\boldsymbol\psi,x,x') - \sum_{i=1}^n(z_i-x_i')\frac{\partial}{\partial u^i}\Pi(\boldsymbol\psi,x,x') = \psi_0\,E(x,x',z). \tag{21}$$
If $\Pi$ achieves its maximum at the interior point $u=x'$ (exactly the classical, unrestricted-$\Omega$ setting), the last term on the left vanishes (a stationary interior maximum has zero gradient in every direction), and — since $\psi_0\le0$ by part 3 of the maximum principle — condition (13) (that $u=x'$ actually achieves the maximum, i.e. the left side of (21) is $\le0$) becomes exactly
$$E(x,x',z) \ge 0, \tag{22}$$
Weierstrass' necessary condition itself, in precisely the form met in Chapter 6, Section 34. So the maximum principle and Weierstrass' condition are, in the classical unrestricted-control setting, literally the same statement — the maximum principle is what Weierstrass' condition becomes once "nearby slope $z$" is replaced by "any control value in $\Omega$," and once $\Omega$ actually restricts the available controls (rather than being all of space), the maximum principle remains valid and meaningful even though (22) itself, tied as it is to an interior maximum, no longer directly applies at a control value sitting on the boundary of $\Omega$.

This is a fitting note to end the whole book's lesson series on: two necessary conditions that looked, on first encounter back in Chapter 6, like a sophisticated but essentially self-contained piece of classical analysis — Hilbert's invariant integral, a cleverly chosen exact differential, a Taylor remainder turned into a sign condition — turn out, from the vantage point of optimal control theory, to be a special case of a single, more general principle: at an optimum, whatever choice is actually made must be the best one available, at every instant, among every alternative actually on offer. Restricting "every alternative on offer" to "slopes near the extremal's own slope" recovers the classical Legendre and Jacobi theory of Chapter 5; widening it to "slopes anywhere at all" recovers Weierstrass and Chapter 6; and widening it once more, to "any value in an arbitrary control region, possibly overriding the dynamics entirely," recovers Pontryagin's maximum principle — three ever-more-demanding notions of "the best," radiating outward from the same single idea that opened this whole book: $\delta J=0$.

Chapter recap

This appendix translated Pontryagin's theory of optimal control into the language built over the rest of the book, and found the translation to be remarkably direct. Section 1 set up the control-theoretic vocabulary — phase space, control functions confined to a region $\Omega$, and the optimal control problem. Section 2 identified this as a minimum problem subject to nonholonomic constraints (Chapter 2, Section 12.2's machinery, now applied with the extra twist of a restricted control region), and showed the ordinary classical variational problem sits inside it as the special case with no restriction on the control at all. Section 3 built the control-theoretic analogue of canonical variables — the conjugate system $\psi$ and the function $\Pi$ — and showed that closing this almost-Hamiltonian system requires maximizing $\Pi$ over the control, producing $\mathscr H$ via a Legendre-transform-flavored construction. Section 4 stated the maximum principle itself and confirmed it specializes exactly to the classical Euler equations once the control restriction is lifted — with the restricted case producing the distinctly new phenomenon of bang-bang control, controls that hug the boundary of $\Omega$ rather than sitting at an interior stationary point. Section 5 closed the loop by showing the maximum principle specializes, in the classical interior-maximum case, to exactly the Weierstrass necessary condition of Chapter 6. Across this whole set of lessons — from Euler's equation for a single function of a single variable, all the way to Pontryagin's maximum principle for a controlled dynamical system — the same idea has recurred in ever more general clothing: compare the actual choice to every alternative actually available, and demand that no alternative do better. That one idea, chased across eight chapters and two appendices, is what the calculus of variations turns out to be.

Practice problems
Problem 1

1. (The maximum principle for the time-optimal problem.) State the maximum principle for the "fastest motion" problem, where the functional (4) reduces simply to $J[U]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}dt$.

Hint: With $f^0\equiv0$ (no running cost beyond elapsed time itself — check that $J[U]=x^0(t_1)$ with $dx^0/dt=1$ reduces exactly to $t_1-t_0$), the sum defining $\Pi$ in (10) loses its $\alpha=0$ term entirely, becoming $P(\psi,x,u)=\sum_{\alpha=1}^n\psi_\alpha f^\alpha(x,u)$, and correspondingly $\mathscr H(\psi,x)=\sup_{u\in\Omega}P(\psi,x,u)=\mathscr H(\psi,x)-\psi_0$ (the book's own stated answer) — with condition 3 of the maximum principle becoming $H[\psi(t_1),x(t_1)]=-\psi_0\ge0$, holding at every $t\in[t_0,t_1]$ by the same constancy fact noted in the theorem.

Problem 2

2. (A first bang-bang control computation.) For $d^2x/dt^2=u$ with $|u|\le1$, writing $x^1=x,x^2=dx/dt$ so that $dx^1/dt=x^2,\ dx^2/dt=u$, find the trajectory giving the fastest motion from a given initial point $x_0$ to the origin $(0,0)$.

Hint: Following the book's hint, the adjoint equations reduce to $d\psi_1/dt=0,\ d\psi_2/dt=-\psi_1$, so $\psi_1=$ const and $\psi_2(t)=c_2-c_1t$ is linear. The maximum principle forces $u(t)=\text{sgn}\,\psi_2(t)$ (maximizing $\psi_2u$ over $|u|\le1$ pointwise picks $u=+1$ where $\psi_2>0$ and $u=-1$ where $\psi_2<0$ — the sign function $\text{sgn}(x)=x/|x|$ simply reads off which extreme of $[-1,1]$ maximizes a linear function of $u$), and since $\psi_2$ is linear in $t$ it can change sign at most once. So the optimal control is "bang-bang": constant at $+1$ or $-1$, with at most one switch. Integrating $\ddot x=\pm1$ gives parabolic arcs in the $(x^1,x^2)$-plane; sketch the family of parabolas through the origin for each sign of $u$, and determine, for a given starting point $x_0$, which parabola (and which possible switch) reaches the origin fastest.

Problem 3

3. (Bang-bang for a different equation.) Study the same time-optimal problem for $d^2x/dt^2+x=u$, $|u|\le1$, with system $dx^1/dt=x^2,\ dx^2/dt=-x^1+u$.

Hint: Same overall strategy as Problem 2 — derive the adjoint system for this modified dynamics, show the maximum principle again forces $u=\text{sgn}(\psi_2)$, and work out how $\psi_2(t)$'s sign changes now that the underlying dynamics has an oscillatory term (the homogeneous part of the equation is simple harmonic motion, rather than Problem 2's straight-line uniform acceleration) — expect the switching structure to look different because the "coasting" trajectories between switches are now arcs related to circles/ellipses in phase space rather than parabolas.

Problem 4

4. (Two independent controls.) Study the same time-optimal problem for the system $dx^1/dt=x^2+u^1,\ dx^2/dt=-x^1+u^2$, with $|u^1|\le1,|u^2|\le1$ independently.

Hint: With two independent controls, each confined to its own interval, the maximum principle's pointwise maximization separates into two independent one-variable problems (maximize $\psi_1u^1$ over $|u^1|\le1$, and separately maximize $\psi_2u^2$ over $|u^2|\le1$) — so each $u^i(t)=\text{sgn}\,\psi_i(t)$ independently, and the interesting new content is working out the adjoint system's behavior for this coupled dynamics to determine each $\psi_i$'s sign pattern.

Problem 5

5. (Verifying the maximum-principle relations for the classical problem, via Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions.) Verify the relations (14) — $\psi_0(t_1)\le0,\ \mathscr H[\psi(t_1),x(t_1)]=0$ — for the simple variational problem (16) discussed in Remark 2 of Section 4.

Hint: Use Euler's theorem on positive-homogeneous functions (already met in Chapter 2's problem set, in the context of the parametric-form integrand's homogeneity from Section 10): for a function $g(u)$ positive-homogeneous of degree $1$, $\sum_iu^ig_{u^i}(u)=g(u)$. Apply this to $\Pi(\psi,x,u)$ as a function of $u$ alone (holding $\psi,x$ fixed) at the maximizing point $u=x'$, using the interior-stationarity relations $\psi_i=-\psi_0f^0_{u^i}$ derived in Section 4, to show $\mathscr H=\Pi[\psi,x,x']$ collapses to exactly $0$ along the optimal trajectory — the same style of homogeneity argument that made the parametric-form Euler equations work out cleanly all the way back in Chapter 2.
