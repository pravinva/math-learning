00Roadmap: what's new here

Look back at every variational problem in Chapters 1 and 2: no matter how many unknown functions there were, or whether the functional depended on higher derivatives, or whether there was a subsidiary constraint, the endpoints of the curve were always either completely fixed, or (in one single instance — Chapter 1, Section 6) allowed to slide along a fixed vertical line. Chapter 3 removes this restriction entirely. Now:

  • The two endpoints can move to any new location in the plane — not just up and down a fixed vertical line, but anywhere at all (Section 13).
  • More specifically, the endpoints might be required to lie on two given curves (or surfaces, for several unknown functions) — think of the shortest-distance problem between two curves, rather than between two fixed points (Section 14).
  • The curve itself might not even be smooth everywhere — it might have a corner, a "kink," at some interior point, and we need to figure out what conditions hold right at that kink (Section 15).

The good news: there is no new method here. Every result in this chapter comes from applying the exact same three-step machine from Chapter 1 (Taylor-expand the increment, integrate by parts, invoke a fundamental-lemma-type argument) — the only new bookkeeping is being careful about the boundary terms that Chapter 1 mostly discarded (because they vanished automatically, thanks to fixed endpoints) or partially kept (in Section 6, for the one-directional sliding case). This chapter is the full, general treatment of those boundary terms.

There is also a nice conceptual bonus at the end of Section 13: a reformulation using "canonical variables" that turns out to be a preview of the powerful Hamiltonian framework fully developed in Chapter 4 (outside our scope here, but worth knowing this is where the idea is first planted).

13Derivation of the Basic Formula

13.1 Setting up: what does "distance between two curves with different endpoints" even mean?

Here's an immediate technical snag. In every previous chapter, when we compared a curve $y(x)$ to a nearby perturbed curve $y(x)+h(x)$, both curves were defined on the same interval $[x_0,x_1]$, and comparing them (e.g. via the norm $\|h\|_1 = \max|h|+\max|h'|$) was completely routine. But now we want to compare $y=y(x)$, defined on some interval, to a different curve $y=y^*(x)$ whose endpoints have shifted to new locations — meaning $y^*$ might naturally be defined on a slightly different interval $[x_0+\delta x_0,\ x_1+\delta x_1]$. How do we even measure the "distance" between two curves with different domains?

The book's fix is simple and practical: extend each curve linearly (along its tangent line) a little past its actual endpoint, just far enough that both curves are now defined on a common interval large enough to contain both original intervals. This is purely a bookkeeping device — draw the tangent line at each endpoint and extend the curve along it for a tiny distance — and it lets us define the distance between the two curves as
$$\rho(y,y^*) = \max|y-y^*| + \max|y'-y^*{}'| + \rho(P_0,P_0^*) + \rho(P_1,P_1^*),$$
where $P_0,P_1$ and $P_0^*,P_1^*$ are the (left- and right-hand) endpoints of the two curves, and $\rho(P,P^*)$ is just the ordinary Euclidean distance between two points in the plane. In words: two curves count as "close" if they're close in the usual $\mathscr{D}_1$ sense over their common domain, and their endpoints are close together as points in the plane.

13.2 Notation for the general perturbation

Let $y=y(x)$ have endpoints $P_0=(x_0,y_0)$ and $P_1=(x_1,y_1)$. Let the nearby curve be $y^*(x) = y(x)+h(x)$, with endpoints shifted to
$$P_0^* = (x_0+\delta x_0,\ y_0+\delta y_0), \qquad P_1^* = (x_1+\delta x_1,\ y_1+\delta y_1).$$
So now we have six small quantities describing the perturbation: the function $h(x)$ itself (and implicitly $h'$), plus the four numbers $\delta x_0,\delta y_0,\delta x_1,\delta y_1$ describing how far each endpoint has moved, in each coordinate direction. The general variation $\delta J$ of $J[y]=\int F(x,y,y')\,dx$ is defined as the part of the increment $\Delta J = J[y^*]-J[y]$ that is linear in all six of these quantities ($h,h',\delta x_0,\delta y_0,\delta x_1,\delta y_1$), differing from $\Delta J$ itself by something of higher order.

13.3 Deriving the formula

Write the increment as a difference of two integrals over slightly different intervals:
$$\Delta J = \int_{x_0+\delta x_0}^{x_1+\delta x_1} F(x,y+h,y'+h')\,dx - \int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y,y')\,dx.$$
Split this cleverly into two pieces: (i) the difference of the two integrands over the common core interval $[x_0,x_1]$ (this is exactly the same computation as Chapter 1's derivation!), plus (ii) two small "sliver" corrections accounting for the slightly different intervals at each end:
$$\Delta J = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}\bigl[F(x,y+h,y'+h')-F(x,y,y')\bigr]dx + \int_{x_1}^{x_1+\delta x_1}F(x,y+h,y'+h')\,dx - \int_{x_0}^{x_0+\delta x_0}F(x,y+h,y'+h')\,dx.$$
For the core piece, exactly as in Chapter 1's Section 4.1, Taylor expansion plus integration by parts gives (using the symbol $\sim$ to mean "equal up to terms of higher order than linear," exactly as the book does):
$$\int_{x_0}^{x_1}[F_yh+F_{y'}h']\,dx = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}\left(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\right)h\,dx + \bigl[F_{y'}h\bigr]_{x_0}^{x_1}.$$
For the two sliver pieces, since $\delta x_0,\delta x_1$ are themselves small, each sliver integral is well-approximated (to leading, linear order) by simply "value of the integrand at the endpoint, times the width of the sliver":
$$\int_{x_1}^{x_1+\delta x_1}F\,dx \sim F(x,y,y')\big|_{x=x_1}\,\delta x_1, \qquad \int_{x_0}^{x_0+\delta x_0}F\,dx \sim F(x,y,y')\big|_{x=x_0}\,\delta x_0.$$
Putting the pieces together:
$$\Delta J \sim \int_{x_0}^{x_1}\left(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\right)h\,dx + \Bigl[F_{y'}h\Bigr]_{x=x_1} - \Bigl[F_{y'}h\Bigr]_{x=x_0} + F\big|_{x_1}\delta x_1 - F\big|_{x_0}\delta x_0.$$

The one new geometric substitution. We want the final formula expressed in terms of the endpoint-motion quantities $\delta y_0,\delta y_1,\delta x_0,\delta x_1$ — not $h(x_0)$ and $h(x_1)$ directly, since $h$ was only defined (strictly) on the original curve's domain, and the whole point of the tangent-line-extension trick was to relate the two. Picture the endpoint region: the curve $y(x)$ has some value and slope at $x_0$; as we move a small horizontal distance $\delta x_0$ further (using the tangent-line extension), the curve's height changes by approximately $y'(x_0)\,\delta x_0$; and separately, the new curve's endpoint has been displaced vertically by $\delta y_0$ relative to the old curve's point directly below/above it. Comparing these two effects (see the picture in the book — a small right triangle at each endpoint, with $h(x_0)$ as the leg connecting them) gives the key relations
$$h(x_0) \sim \delta y_0 - y'(x_0)\,\delta x_0, \qquad h(x_1) \sim \delta y_1 - y'(x_1)\,\delta x_1.$$
(In words: the vertical gap between the two curves right at the original endpoint, $h(x_0)$, equals the actual endpoint displacement $\delta y_0$, minus however much the original curve itself would have risen or fallen over that same horizontal displacement $\delta x_0$ — that "would have risen" amount is subtracted off because it's already accounted for by staying on the same curve, not by the two curves actually differing.)

Substituting these into the boundary terms above and collecting everything gives the basic formula for the general variation:
$$\boxed{\delta J = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}\left(F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\right)h(x)\,dx + F_{y'}\,\delta y\Big|_{x=x_0}^{x=x_1} + \bigl(F-y'F_{y'}\bigr)\,\delta x\Big|_{x=x_0}^{x=x_1},} \tag{5}$$
where we abbreviate $\delta y|_{x=x_i}:=\delta y_i$ and $\delta x|_{x=x_i}:=\delta x_i$ for $i=0,1$.

Sanity check against Chapter 1. If both endpoints are completely fixed ($\delta x_0=\delta x_1=\delta y_0=\delta y_1=0$), formula (5) collapses to just the integral term — recovering exactly the fixed-endpoint variation from Chapter 1, Section 4.1. If the endpoints are constrained to slide along the vertical lines $x=x_0,x=x_1$ (so $\delta x_0=\delta x_1=0$, but $\delta y_0,\delta y_1$ free), formula (5) collapses to the integral term plus $F_{y'}\delta y|_{x_0}^{x_1}$ — exactly reproducing the variable-endpoint formula from Chapter 1, Section 6! So this new formula is the common generalization of everything seen so far — a good consistency check to run yourself, rather than just taking the book's word for it.

13.4 Generalizing to $n$ functions

Everything above goes through with no new ideas for $J[y_1,\dots,y_n]=\int F(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n')\,dx$, where now each $y_i$ has its own endpoint values that can shift by $\delta y_i^0,\delta y_i^1$, while the shared $x$-endpoints shift by $\delta x_0,\delta x_1$. The resulting formula is a direct sum over $i$:
$$\delta J = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(F_{y_i}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'}\right)h_i(x)\,dx + \sum_{i=1}^n F_{y_i'}\,\delta y_i\Big|_{x_0}^{x_1} + \left(F-\sum_{i=1}^n y_i'F_{y_i'}\right)\delta x\Big|_{x_0}^{x_1}. \tag{7}$$

13.5 Canonical variables and the Hamiltonian: a preview of Chapter 4

This subsection introduces some notation that looks like a detour, but it's worth working through carefully, since it will make Chapter 4 (if you go on to study it) feel far less mysterious.

Look at formula (7): the quantity $F_{y_i'}$ shows up as the natural "coefficient" attached to $\delta y_i$ in the boundary term. Define
$$p_i := F_{y_i'} \qquad (i=1,\dots,n). \tag{8}$$
These are called momenta (the name comes from mechanics: if $F$ is a mechanical Lagrangian and $y_i'$ are velocities, $F_{y_i'}$ is literally momentum — but the definition here is purely formal and makes sense for any $F$). Assuming the Jacobian $\det\|F_{y_i'y_k'}\|$ is nonzero (a technical condition guaranteeing this substitution is invertible — directly analogous to the nonzero-Jacobian condition for changing coordinates in Chapter 1, Section 8), equations (8) can, in principle, be solved for the velocities $y_1',\dots,y_n'$ in terms of $x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n$ — i.e., we can trade the "velocity-like" description of the curve's local behavior for a "momentum-like" description. This trade is called a Legendre transform, and it shows up all over physics (it's exactly how you pass from the Lagrangian to the Hamiltonian formulation of mechanics).

Having made this substitution, define the Hamiltonian
$$H(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n) := -F + \sum_{i=1}^n y_i'p_i,$$
where every occurrence of $y_i'$ on the right is now understood to have been rewritten in terms of $p_1,\dots,p_n$ using the (assumed invertible) relations (8). The new variables $x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n$ (position and momentum, instead of position and velocity) are called canonical variables.

The payoff: formula (7) can be rewritten, purely as bookkeeping, in the tidy canonical form
$$\delta J = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}\sum_{i=1}^n\left(F_{y_i}-\frac{dp_i}{dx}\right)h_i(x)\,dx + \left(\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\,\delta y_i - H\,\delta x\right)\Big|_{x=x_0}^{x=x_1}. \tag{12}$$
(You can check this is just (7) rewritten using the definitions of $p_i$ and $H$ — a completely mechanical substitution, nothing conceptually new, but notice how much cleaner it looks: the boundary term is now "$\sum p_i\,\delta y_i - H\,\delta x$," a elegant, symmetric-looking expression. This elegance is not an accident — it's the beginning of the whole Hamiltonian machinery, which Chapter 4 develops far beyond what we need here.)

13.6 A key structural remark: extremals first, boundary conditions second

Here is the single most important takeaway from this section, worth stating in its own right because it governs the logic of every remaining section in the chapter:

Remark

Suppose $J[y_1,\dots,y_n]$ has an extremum, among some class of admissible curves with movable endpoints $P_0,P_1$, at a particular curve joining particular points $P_0,P_1$. Since this curve, in particular, beats every other curve joining that same specific pair of points $P_0,P_1$ (a strictly smaller comparison class than "every admissible curve with movable endpoints"), it must in particular be a solution of the ordinary fixed-endpoint Euler equations — because being optimal against a larger field of competitors (those with movable endpoints) automatically implies being optimal against the smaller field of competitors sharing your exact endpoints. Therefore, in formula (7), the integral term (which is exactly the Euler-equation combination) vanishes identically, and all that's left is the leftover boundary term:
$$\delta J = \left(\sum_{i=1}^n F_{y_i'}\,\delta y_i + \Bigl(F-\sum y_i'F_{y_i'}\Bigr)\delta x\right)\Bigg|_{x=x_0}^{x=x_1} = 0. \tag{11}$$
This is exactly the two-stage recipe first seen in Chapter 1, Section 6, now stated in full generality: whatever kind of endpoint freedom your problem has, the recipe is always (1) solve the ordinary Euler equations first, ignoring the endpoint freedom entirely, and (2) use the specific kind of endpoint freedom you have to decide which combination of $\delta x_0,\delta y_0,\delta x_1,\delta y_1$ (or their several-function analogues) is actually free to vary, then set the corresponding coefficients in (11) to zero. Sections 14 and 15 are simply two different instances of "what kind of endpoint freedom do we have, and what does (11) become as a result."

14End Points Lying on Two Given Curves or Surfaces

14.1 Setting up: transversality

The problem. Among smooth curves whose endpoints $P_0,P_1$ lie on two given fixed curves $y=\varphi(x)$ and $y=\psi(x)$ (not fixed points, and not restricted to a fixed vertical line as in Chapter 1, Section 6 — arbitrary curves), find the one making $J[y]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y,y')\,dx$ extremal. (A natural example: the shortest-distance problem between two curves in the plane is exactly this, with $F=\sqrt{1+y'^2}$.)

Step 1 (per the Remark above): the curve must be an extremal. By Section 13.6, we know immediately that the desired curve, whatever it is, must satisfy Euler's equation $F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$. So the boundary-term condition (11) — specialized to $n=1$ — is all that's left:
$$\delta J = F_{y'}\big|_{x_1}\delta y_1 + \bigl(F-y'F_{y'}\bigr)\big|_{x_1}\delta x_1 - F_{y'}\big|_{x_0}\delta y_0 - \bigl(F-y'F_{y'}\bigr)\big|_{x_0}\delta x_0 = 0.$$

Step 2 (the new geometric substitution): endpoints confined to curves. Since the left endpoint must stay on the curve $y=\varphi(x)$ as $x_0$ shifts by $\delta x_0$, its height must shift by exactly $\varphi'(x_0)\,\delta x_0$ (to first order — this is nothing but the statement "the point $(x_0+\delta x_0,\ \varphi(x_0+\delta x_0))$ has height $\approx \varphi(x_0)+\varphi'(x_0)\delta x_0$," a direct application of the ordinary single-variable linear approximation from calc 1). So:
$$\delta y_0 \sim \varphi'(x_0)\,\delta x_0, \qquad \delta y_1 \sim \psi'(x_1)\,\delta x_1.$$
Substituting these into the boundary condition above turns it into an expression purely in terms of the two now fully free, independent quantities $\delta x_0$ and $\delta x_1$:
$$\Bigl[F_{y'}\psi'+F-y'F_{y'}\Bigr]_{x=x_1}\delta x_1 - \Bigl[F_{y'}\varphi'+F-y'F_{y'}\Bigr]_{x=x_0}\delta x_0 = 0.$$
Since $\delta x_0$ and $\delta x_1$ can be chosen completely independently (they describe two unrelated, unconstrained endpoints), each bracket must separately vanish — giving the transversality conditions:
$$\boxed{\bigl[F+(\varphi'-y')F_{y'}\bigr]\Big|_{x=x_0}=0, \qquad \bigl[F+(\psi'-y')F_{y'}\bigr]\Big|_{x=x_1}=0.} \tag{13}$$

A curve satisfying these conditions relative to the two given curves $\varphi,\psi$ is called a transversal of $\varphi$ and $\psi$. To fully solve such a problem: (1) write down Euler's equation and find its general solution (two arbitrary constants); (2) use the two transversality conditions in (13) — instead of ordinary fixed-value boundary conditions — to solve for those two constants.

14.2 The special (and very common) case of arc-length-type functionals

For functionals of the form $F(x,y,y')=f(x,y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ — a weight function $f$ times the arc-length element, exactly the pattern from Chapter 1's Case 4, and the pattern underlying every "shortest distance"-flavored problem — the transversality condition simplifies dramatically. Compute $F_{y'} = f\cdot\dfrac{y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}$, so:
$$F+(\varphi'-y')F_{y'} = f\sqrt{1+y'^2} + (\varphi'-y')\cdot\frac{fy'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = \frac{f\bigl[(1+y'^2)+(\varphi'-y')y'\bigr]}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = \frac{f(1+y'\varphi')}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}$$
(the numerator simplifies: $1+y'^2+\varphi'y'-y'^2 = 1+y'\varphi'$). Since $f$ and the denominator are (generically) nonzero, the transversality condition $F+(\varphi'-y')F_{y'}=0$ reduces to just $1+y'\varphi'=0$, i.e.
$$y' = -\frac{1}{\varphi'} \quad \text{at the left endpoint (and similarly } y'=-1/\psi' \text{ at the right)}.$$
This is exactly the condition for two curves to meet at a right angle (recall from calc 1/2: two lines with slopes $m_1,m_2$ are perpendicular iff $m_1m_2=-1$ — here that's $y'\cdot\varphi' = -1$). So for arc-length-type functionals, "transversality" reduces to plain old orthogonality: the shortest-distance curve between two given curves must meet each of them at a right angle. This matches a fact you may already believe geometrically (the shortest segment from a point to a line is the perpendicular one — this generalizes that fact to curves at both ends), and it's a satisfying moment where the abstract transversality machinery reproduces something concretely visualizable.

14.3 Generalizing to two unknown functions (endpoints on surfaces)

If instead we have a curve $y=y(x),z=z(x)$ in space, with endpoints required to lie on two given surfaces $x=\varphi(y,z)$ and $x=\psi(y,z)$ (parametrized this way — solving for $x$ in terms of $y,z$ — purely for notational convenience matching the book), the identical style of argument (apply formula (7) with $n=2$, note the curve must be an extremal by Section 13.6, then substitute the appropriate "must stay on the surface" relations for how $\delta x_0$ relates to $\delta y_0,\delta z_0$, this time using two partial-derivative relations since the surface has two independent directions) produces four transversality conditions (two at each end, one per unknown function):
$$\Bigl[F_{y'}+\varphi_y(F-y'F_{y'}-z'F_{z'})\Bigr]_{x_0}=0,\qquad \Bigl[F_{z'}+\varphi_z(F-y'F_{y'}-z'F_{z'})\Bigr]_{x_0}=0,$$
and the analogous pair at $x_1$ using $\psi_y,\psi_z$. You aren't expected to re-derive these from scratch on a first pass — the important takeaway is the pattern: one transversality condition per unknown function per endpoint, each condition obtained by the identical "the endpoint must stay on the constraint surface" substitution used in the one-function case, just with the extra bookkeeping of two partial derivatives ($\varphi_y,\varphi_z$) instead of one ordinary derivative ($\varphi'$).

15Broken Extremals. The Weierstrass–Erdmann Conditions

15.1 Motivation: some variational problems have no smooth solution

Consider the functional $J[y]=\int_{-1}^1 y^2(1-y')^2\,dx$, with $y(-1)=0,y(1)=1$. Since the integrand is a square, $J[y]\ge0$ always, so the greatest lower bound of $J[y]$ over all admissible curves is (at least) $0$. In fact this lower bound is exactly $0$ — but here is the surprising part: no smooth curve satisfying the boundary conditions actually achieves $J[y]=0$. (To make the integrand vanish everywhere you'd need $y\equiv0$ or $y'\equiv1$ at every point, but neither can hold on the whole interval while also satisfying $y(-1)=0,y(1)=1$ — a smooth curve is forced to have some stretch where the integrand is strictly positive.) However, the piecewise smooth curve
$$y(x) = \begin{cases}0 & -1\le x\le 0\\ x & 0 achieves $J[y]=0$ exactly — it's identically $y=0$ (so the first factor $y^2$ vanishes) on the left piece, and identically has slope $y'=1$ (so the second factor $(1-y')^2$ vanishes) on the right piece. But this curve has a corner at $x=0$ — its derivative jumps from $0$ to $1$, so it's continuous but not differentiable there.

This is not an isolated curiosity: recall the catenoid/minimal-surface-of-revolution problem from Chapter 1, Section 4.3 (Worked Example 2) — when the two given points are close enough together relative to their distance from the axis, no smooth catenary achieves the minimum surface area; instead, the true minimizer is a broken curve made of three straight segments (vertical, horizontal, vertical), corresponding to a degenerate surface (two flat end-disks joined along the axis). Both examples make the same point: restricting to smooth curves can make certain variational problems literally unsolvable, even though a "reasonable" answer clearly exists once you allow curves with a finite number of corners.

15.2 Setting up the broken-extremal problem

We therefore enlarge the admissible class: consider functions $y(x)$ that are continuously differentiable on $[a,b]$ except possibly at one interior point $c$ (where $y$ is still required to be continuous — no actual jump in height, just a kink in the slope), satisfying $y(a)=A,y(b)=B$, and seek the curve minimizing (or maximizing) $J[y]=\int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,dx$ in this enlarged class, allowing the location $c$ of the corner to be anything (it's not prescribed in advance — part of what we're solving for is where the corner sits, if there is one).

15.3 Deriving the corner conditions

On each smooth piece, Euler's equation must hold. This is immediate: fixing the corner point at some particular location $(c,y(c))$ and looking only at what happens on $[a,c]$, this restricted problem is just an ordinary fixed-endpoint problem (endpoints $a$ and $c$, both momentarily held fixed) — so, by Chapter 1, the piece on $[a,c]$ must satisfy the ordinary Euler equation there; symmetrically for $[c,b]$.

Now let the corner itself move, and see what extra condition falls out. Split the functional as a sum of two pieces, $J[y]=J_1[y]+J_2[y] = \int_a^c F\,dx + \int_c^b F\,dx$, and think of the shared point $(c,y(c))$ as a movable right endpoint for $J_1$ and, simultaneously, a movable left endpoint for $J_2$ — exactly the setup Section 13 was built for! Apply formula (5) to each piece separately (with $x_0=a,x_1=c$ fixed-at-$a$/movable-at-$c$ for $J_1$; and symmetrically for $J_2$), remembering that on each piece the integral term vanishes (Euler's equation already holds there), leaving pure boundary terms:
$$\delta J_1 = F_{y'}\big|_{x=c^-}\,\delta y_1 + (F-y'F_{y'})\big|_{x=c^-}\,\delta x_1, \qquad \delta J_2 = -F_{y'}\big|_{x=c^+}\,\delta y_1 - (F-y'F_{y'})\big|_{x=c^+}\,\delta x_1$$
(the signs flip for $J_2$ because $c$ is $J_2$'s left endpoint, and formula (5) has an overall sign difference between its two ends — check this against the general formula (5) itself). Here $\delta y_1,\delta x_1$ denote the shared displacement of the corner point — shared, because we require $y$ to remain continuous at the corner as it moves (the two pieces must always meet up, even as the corner slides around). At an extremum, $\delta J = \delta J_1+\delta J_2 = 0$:
$$\Bigl(F_{y'}\big|_{c^-}-F_{y'}\big|_{c^+}\Bigr)\delta y_1 + \Bigl[(F-y'F_{y'})\big|_{c^-} - (F-y'F_{y'})\big|_{c^+}\Bigr]\delta x_1 = 0.$$
Since $\delta x_1,\delta y_1$ are now free, independent quantities (nothing pins down exactly where or how the corner moves), each coefficient must vanish separately, giving the Weierstrass–Erdmann corner conditions:
$$\boxed{F_{y'}\big|_{x=c^-} = F_{y'}\big|_{x=c^+}, \qquad (F-y'F_{y'})\big|_{x=c^-} = (F-y'F_{y'})\big|_{x=c^+}.} \tag{18}$$

Why this is exactly what you'd hope for, in hindsight. Recall the canonical variables from Section 13.5: $p=F_{y'}$ (momentum) and $H=-F+y'F_{y'}$ (Hamiltonian, for $n=1$). Conditions (18) say precisely that $p$ and $H$ are continuous across the corner, even though $y'$ itself jumps discontinuously there! This is a elegant fact: the "position and slope" description of the curve is allowed to be kinked, but the "position and momentum/energy" description is not — momentum and (this notion of) energy must be conserved smoothly through the kink, exactly the kind of statement you'd expect from a well-posed physical or geometric optimality principle.

Solving a broken-extremal problem in practice. On each of $[a,c]$ and $[c,b]$, Euler's equation is a second-order ODE, so we get $2+2=4$ arbitrary constants total, plus the unknown corner location $c$ itself — five unknowns. These are pinned down by: the two original boundary conditions $y(a)=A,y(b)=B$; the requirement that the two pieces meet continuously at $c$ (one more equation); and the two corner conditions (18) — that's $2+1+2=5$ equations for the $5$ unknowns, so (generically) the system is exactly determined.

15.4 The geometric picture: the indicatrix and the double tangent line

Here is a beautiful way to visualize conditions (18), which the book presents as a nice geometric aside (and which becomes central machinery in the field theory of Chapter 6, beyond our current scope — but the picture is worth having now). Fix the values of $x$ and $y$ at the corner point, and consider $F(x,y,y')$ purely as a function of the single remaining variable $y'$ — plot this function's graph (with $y'$ along the horizontal axis and the value of $F$ along the vertical axis). This curve is called the indicatrix.

The first Weierstrass–Erdmann condition, $F_{y'}|_{c^-}=F_{y'}|_{c^+}$, says the slopes of the tangent lines to the indicatrix, at the two points $y'(c^-)$ and $y'(c^+)$, are equal — i.e., the two tangent lines are parallel. The second condition can be rewritten (using the first) as $F|_{c^+}-F|_{c^-} = F_{y'}\cdot\bigl[y'(c^+)-y'(c^-)\bigr]$, which is exactly the statement that the chord connecting the two points $(y'(c^-), F|_{c^-})$ and $(y'(c^+),F|_{c^+})$ on the indicatrix has the same slope as those two (already-parallel) tangent lines. Put geometrically: the two tangent lines to the indicatrix, at the two different slope-values $y'(c^-)$ and $y'(c^+)$, are not merely parallel — they are literally the same line, touching the indicatrix curve at two separate points. This "doubly-tangent line" picture is a striking way to encode both corner conditions in a single geometric image, and it's exactly the tool used, in Chapter 6, to decide in advance whether a given variational problem's indicatrix even admits such broken extremals at all (some indicatrices, like those that are strictly convex everywhere, can never have a line tangent to them at two different points — ruling out broken extremals entirely for such problems).

Chapter recap

This chapter answered one question in full generality: what happens to the necessary conditions for an extremum when the endpoints of the curve are allowed to move? The answer, distilled to its essence, is always the same two-stage recipe first glimpsed in Chapter 1's Section 6: solve the ordinary Euler equation(s) first (this part never changes, no matter how exotic the endpoint freedom is — Section 13.6's Remark explains exactly why), and then use whatever specific kind of freedom the endpoints have to decide which combination of boundary quantities is free to vary, setting the corresponding coefficient of the leftover boundary term (formula (11) or its canonical-variable form (12)) to zero. Section 14 specialized this to endpoints confined to given curves or surfaces, producing the transversality conditions — which reduce to simple orthogonality for the common case of arc-length-type functionals. Section 15 applied the very same idea to an interior movable point (a corner), producing the Weierstrass–Erdmann conditions, and revealed the pleasing fact that these conditions are exactly "the canonical variables $p,H$ stay continuous even where $y'$ itself does not" — tying this chapter's boundary-term bookkeeping directly back to the canonical-variable machinery introduced in Section 13.5. Together, Chapters 1 through 3 now give you the complete classical toolkit for finding candidate extremals of essentially any variational problem you're likely to encounter: fixed or moving endpoints, one function or many, first or higher derivatives, parametric or explicit curves, constrained or unconstrained. What remains beyond this point — covered in Chapters 4 through 8 of the full book, outside the scope of this set of lessons — is (in one sentence each) the deeper structural theory of the canonical/Hamiltonian equations and their conservation laws (Chapter 4), genuine sufficient conditions distinguishing true minima from mere candidates (Chapters 5–6), the extension to multiple integrals over regions (Chapter 7), and computational methods that approximate extrema directly, without ever solving a differential equation (Chapter 8).

3. (Broken extremals: when can they NOT occur?) Prove that the functional $J[y]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}(ay'^2+byy'+cy^2)\,dx$, with $a\ne 0$, can have no broken extremals.

Hint: Apply the first Weierstrass–Erdmann condition, $F_{y'}|_{c^-}=F_{y'}|_{c^+}$. Compute $F_{y'}=2ay'+by$ directly from the given $F$ — notice this is a linear, strictly increasing (since $a\ne0$; assume WLOG $a>0$) or strictly decreasing function of $y'$ alone (for the fixed value of $y$ at the corner point, which is the same on both sides since $y$ itself is required to be continuous there).
