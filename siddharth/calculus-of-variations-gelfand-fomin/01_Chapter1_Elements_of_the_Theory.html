00Before we start: what is this subject actually about?

In ordinary calculus, you are handed a function $f(x)$ and you look for the number $x$ that makes $f(x)$ largest or smallest. The whole machinery of "set the derivative to zero" is designed to search through a one-dimensional (or $n$-dimensional) haystack of numbers for the best one.

The calculus of variations asks a more ambitious question: what if the thing you're searching over isn't a number, but an entire curve, or an entire function? Here are three questions of exactly this flavor:

  • Among all curves joining two points $A$ and $B$, which one is shortest? (You already know the answer — a straight line — but you've probably never seen a proof that starts from "define length as a functional and minimize it.")
  • Among all curves joining two points $A$ and $B$ in a vertical plane, along which one does a bead sliding under gravity (with no friction) get from $A$ to $B$ in the least time? This is the famous brachistochrone problem, posed by Johann Bernoulli in 1696. The answer is not a straight line — it's an arc of a cycloid.
  • Among all closed curves of a fixed perimeter $l$, which one encloses the largest area? (Answer: a circle. This is the isoperimetric problem.)

In each case, the "input" to the problem is a whole function (a curve), and the "output" is a single number (a length, a time, an area). A rule that eats a function and spits out a number is called a functional. The calculus of variations is, in large part, the theory of finding maxima and minima of functionals — and Chapter 1 builds the entire foundation for doing that, culminating in the single most important tool in the subject: Euler's equation.

Here is the roadmap for this chapter, in plain language:

  1. Section 1 pins down exactly what a functional is, and shows you the zoo of classical problems that motivate the whole subject.
  2. Section 2 asks: "close" in what sense? Before we can talk about maxima and minima of functionals, we need a notion of two functions being "near" each other — exactly as we needed a notion of two points in $\mathbb{R}^n$ being near each other in ordinary calculus. This section builds that notion (normed function spaces).
  3. Section 3 transplants the idea of "differential" from ordinary calculus to functionals (this is called the variation), and proves the fundamental necessary condition for an extremum: the variation must vanish.
  4. Section 4 is the heart of the chapter. It converts "the variation vanishes" into a genuine differential equation you can actually solve — Euler's equation.
  5. Section 5 repeats the whole argument one dimension up, for functionals defined by double integrals over a surface (this needs a short primer on partial derivatives, double integrals, and Green's theorem, which we'll supply).
  6. Section 6 loosens the requirement that both endpoints of the curve are pinned down, and shows what extra conditions appear when an endpoint is allowed to slide.
  7. Section 7 repackages Euler's equation as a "derivative with respect to a function," called the variational derivative — a beautiful piece of bookkeeping that makes the analogy with ordinary calculus completely precise.
  8. Section 8 shows that whether or not a curve is an extremal doesn't depend on which coordinate system you happen to be using — a fact that lets you switch to convenient coordinates (like polar coordinates) before deriving Euler's equation, saving a lot of algebra.

Take your time with Sections 2 and 3. They can feel abstract on a first pass, but everything from Section 4 onward is just turning the crank on the machinery built there.

01Functionals. Some Simple Variational Problems

1.1 The definition

A functional is a rule $J$ that assigns a single real number $J[y]$ to each function $y$ (or each curve) belonging to some class of functions. The square-bracket notation $J[y]$ (instead of $J(y)$) is a typographical convention to remind you that the "input" is a whole function, not a number.

Compare:

  • A function $f$ takes a number $x$ and returns a number $f(x)$.
  • A functional $J$ takes a function $y(x)$ and returns a number $J[y]$.

Here are the book's five introductory examples, unpacked:

  1. Length of a curve. For each rectifiable plane curve, associate its length. Different curves give different numbers — that's a functional.
  2. Center of mass. Imagine each curve is a wire made of some material of uniform density. The $y$-coordinate of its center of mass is a number depending on the whole shape of the wire — another functional.
  3. Travel time along a path. Fix two points $A, B$ in the plane, and suppose a particle moving through the plane has a prescribed speed $v(x,y)$ at each point $(x,y)$ (the speed depends on where you are, not on which path you take to get there). For each path joining $A$ to $B$, the time taken to traverse it is a number — a functional of the path.
  4. A simple integral functional. If $y(x)$ is any continuously differentiable function on $[a,b]$, then
    $$J[y] = \int_a^b y'(x)^2\, dx$$
    is a number depending on $y$. This is one of the simplest possible functionals built from an integral.
  5. The general pattern. Let $F(x,y,z)$ be some fixed continuous function of three ordinary variables. Then
    $$J[y] = \int_a^b F\bigl(x,\, y(x),\, y'(x)\bigr)\, dx \tag{1}$$
    is a functional: plug in a candidate curve $y(x)$, and out comes a number. Notice that examples 1 and 4 are special cases of this pattern: taking $F(x,y,z) = \sqrt{1+z^2}$ recovers arc length, and taking $F(x,y,z) = z^2$ recovers example 4.

Almost the entire book — certainly all of Chapters 1–3 — is about functionals of the form (1), or natural generalizations of it (several unknown functions, higher derivatives, functionals of surfaces instead of curves). So it's worth staring at (1) until it feels completely natural: you feed in a curve, the machine reads off $x$, $y(x)$, and $y'(x)$ at every point along the curve, feeds those three numbers into the fixed function $F$, and integrates the result.

1.2 Three classical variational problems

A variational problem asks you to find the function that makes some functional as large or as small as possible.

Problem 1 (shortest path). Find the curve $y=y(x)$ joining two points $A$ and $B$ that minimizes
$$\int_a^b \sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx.$$
The answer, unsurprisingly, is the straight line segment from $A$ to $B$ — but by the end of Section 4 you will be able to derive this rather than just assert it.

Problem 2 (the brachistochrone). A particle slides, frictionlessly, under gravity alone, along some curve joining two fixed points $A$ and $B$ (with $A$ higher than $B$, and not directly above it). Different curves take different amounts of time to traverse. The curve of least time is called the brachistochrone (Greek for "shortest time"). Posed by Johann Bernoulli in 1696, this problem was solved by Johann Bernoulli, Jakob Bernoulli, Newton, and L'Hospital, and its solution helped launch the calculus of variations as a subject. The answer turns out to be an arc of a cycloid (the curve traced by a point on the rim of a rolling wheel) — we will derive this in Section 6.

Problem 3 (the isoperimetric problem). Among all closed curves of a given perimeter $l$, find the one enclosing the largest area. Solved by Euler, the answer is a circle. We will solve a close variant of this in Section 12 of Chapter 2 (out of scope for this particular lesson, but Chapter 1 lays every brick needed to get there).

1.3 The "localization" property, and a functional that lacks it

All three problems above, and indeed every functional of the form
$$\int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,dx,$$
share a nice feature called localization: if you chop the curve $y=y(x)$, $a \le x \le b$, into pieces (say at some point $c$ between $a$ and $b$), then
$$J[y]\big|_{[a,b]} = J[y]\big|_{[a,c]} + J[y]\big|_{[c,b]}.$$
This is just the additivity of the integral over adjacent intervals — nothing deep, but it is a structural feature worth naming, because not every functional has it. For instance,
$$\frac{\displaystyle\int_a^b x\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx}{\displaystyle\int_a^b \sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx}$$
(the $x$-coordinate of the center of mass of a wire shaped like $y=y(x)$) is a ratio of two integrals, and there is no way to compute this ratio for the whole curve just by knowing its value on two sub-pieces separately. This functional is called nonlocal. The calculus of variations, as classically developed (and as covered in this book), focuses almost entirely on local functionals of the integral form (1), because they are the ones amenable to the "differentiate and set to zero" strategy we're about to build.

1.4 Euler's original idea: turn a functional into an ordinary function of many variables

Here is a beautiful piece of historical insight, due to Euler, that motivates everything that follows. Consider the functional
$$J[y] = \int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,dx, \qquad y(a) = A,\ \ y(b) = B.$$
Chop $[a,b]$ into $n+1$ equal pieces using points $a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n < x_{n+1} = b$, each of width $h = x_i - x_{i-1}$. Now approximate the smooth curve $y(x)$ by the polygonal line through the points $(x_0, A), (x_1,y_1), \dots, (x_n,y_n), (x_{n+1}, B)$ — i.e., replace the curve by a connect-the-dots approximation using only finitely many heights $y_1,\dots,y_n$ (the endpoints $y_0 = A$ and $y_{n+1}=B$ are fixed by the boundary conditions). On each little segment, the polygonal line has a well-defined constant slope $\frac{y_i - y_{i-1}}{h}$, so we can approximate the integral $J[y]$ by the Riemann-sum-like expression
$$J(y_1,\dots,y_n) = \sum_{i=1}^{n+1} F\left(x_i,\, y_i,\, \frac{y_i-y_{i-1}}{h}\right) h. \tag{2}$$
The right-hand side of (2) is an ordinary function of the $n$ real numbers $y_1,\dots,y_n$ — exactly the kind of object calculus already knows how to optimize! Euler's method of finite differences was to find the extremum of (2) using ordinary multivariable calculus (set every partial derivative $\partial J/\partial y_k$ to zero), and then take the limit $n \to \infty$ to recover the exact answer for the original curve-optimization problem.

We won't carry out this finite-difference program in full generality (it reappears properly in Chapter 8), but keep this picture in your head throughout the chapter: a functional can be thought of as "a function of infinitely many variables" — namely the values $y(x)$ at every point $x$ — and the calculus of variations is the calculus of such functions. This is precisely why, in Section 7, "Euler's equation" will turn out to be nothing but the statement that infinitely many partial derivatives (one for every point $x$) simultaneously vanish.

02Function Spaces

2.1 Why we need a notion of "closeness" between functions

To talk about a minimum of an ordinary function $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, you need to be able to say what it means for two points $(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ to be "close" — that's built into the usual Euclidean distance, and it's what lets you say things like "$f$ has a local min at $\hat x$ if $f(x) \ge f(\hat x)$ for all $x$ close enough to $\hat x$." To do the same thing for functionals, we need a notion of "closeness" between two functions $y(x)$ and $z(x)$. There is no single "obviously correct" way to measure the distance between two functions — it depends on what you're doing — so this section builds several useful notions side by side, using the general machinery of normed linear spaces.

2.2 Linear spaces (a five-second refresher, since you already know this from linear algebra)

A linear space (or vector space) $\mathscr{R}$ is a set of objects $x,y,z,\dots$ that can be added together and multiplied by real numbers $\alpha,\beta,\dots$, obeying the familiar eight axioms: commutativity and associativity of addition, existence of a zero element, existence of additive inverses, and the usual distributive/associative laws for scalar multiplication. You've seen this in linear algebra for $\mathbb{R}^n$. The point of this section is that sets of functions also form linear spaces under the ordinary pointwise operations $(y+z)(x) := y(x)+z(x)$ and $(\alpha y)(x) := \alpha\, y(x)$. Nothing new is required to check the eight axioms — they follow immediately from the corresponding facts about real numbers, applied pointwise. So: functions are vectors, in exactly the linear-algebra sense you already know. The only new ingredient is what comes next.

2.3 Norms: measuring the "size" of a function

A linear space $\mathscr{R}$ is called normed if every element $x \in \mathscr{R}$ is assigned a nonnegative number $\|x\|$ (its norm, generalizing "length of a vector") satisfying:

  1. $\|x\| = 0$ if and only if $x = 0$;
  2. $\|\alpha x\| = |\alpha|\, \|x\|$;
  3. $\|x+y\| \le \|x\| + \|y\|$ (the triangle inequality).

Once you have a norm, you automatically get a notion of distance: the distance between $x$ and $y$ is $\|x-y\|$. This is exactly how ordinary Euclidean distance in $\mathbb{R}^n$ arises from the norm $\|x\| = \sqrt{x_1^2+\cdots+x_n^2}$ — nothing mysterious, just the same idea one level of abstraction up.

Now here is the crucial new idea for this subject: the elements of a normed linear space can be functions, and different choices of norm encode different notions of what it means for two functions to be "close." The book introduces three such spaces, each of which will matter later:

The space $\mathscr{C} = \mathscr{C}(a,b)$. Elements: all continuous functions $y(x)$ on $[a,b]$. Norm:
$$\|y\|_0 = \max_{a\le x\le b} |y(x)|.$$
Two functions are close in this norm exactly when their graphs stay within a thin horizontal strip of each other everywhere on $[a,b]$ — picture a function $y^*(x)$ squeezed inside a band of height $2\varepsilon$ around $y(x)$. Crucially, this norm says nothing at all about how steep or wiggly the functions are — only about their heights.

The space $\mathscr{D}_1 = \mathscr{D}_1(a,b)$. Elements: continuously differentiable functions on $[a,b]$. Norm:
$$\|y\|_1 = \max_{a\le x\le b}|y(x)| + \max_{a\le x \le b}|y'(x)|.$$
Now two functions count as close only if both their heights and their slopes stay close everywhere. This is strictly more demanding than $\mathscr{C}$-closeness: a function can wiggle rapidly with tiny amplitude (so it's $\mathscr{C}$-close to a flat line) while having wildly different slope at every point (so it's not $\mathscr{D}_1$-close to that flat line).

The space $\mathscr{D}_n = \mathscr{D}_n(a,b)$. Elements: functions with $n$ continuous derivatives. Norm:
$$\|y\|_n = \sum_{i=0}^n \max_{a \le x \le b} |y^{(i)}(x)|,$$
i.e., we demand that the function and all its derivatives up to order $n$ stay uniformly close.

Why does this matter? Because whether a given functional is continuous depends on which of these norms you use! Consider the arc-length functional $J[y] = \int_a^b \sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$. This functional is continuous if we measure closeness using $\|\cdot\|_1$ (the $\mathscr{D}_1$ norm) — small changes in both height and slope produce small changes in arc length, which is intuitively obvious. But it is not continuous if we only use $\|\cdot\|_0$ (the $\mathscr{C}$ norm): you can find a curve $y^*(x)$ whose graph stays within an arbitrarily thin strip of $y(x)$ (so $\|y^*-y\|_0$ is tiny) while its arc length is, say, ten times longer, simply by making $y^*$ zigzag rapidly with tiny amplitude but enormous total slope-changes (think of a very finely serrated saw blade squeezed into a thin strip — its total path length can be made huge even though it barely deviates in height). This is exactly the sense in which "close in height" does not imply "close in length."

The moral: since we want to use ordinary calculus-style reasoning ("small changes in input produce small changes in output"), we should choose the function space in which our functional actually is continuous. For functionals of the form $\int F(x,y,y')\,dx$, that space is $\mathscr{D}_1$, not $\mathscr{C}$ — even though $\mathscr{C}$ might seem like the more natural, larger space to work in.

2.4 Continuity of a functional, and a remark on non-linear admissible sets

With a norm in hand, continuity of a functional $J[y]$ at a point $\hat y$ is defined exactly as you'd guess by analogy with ordinary continuity:

$$J[y] \text{ is continuous at } \hat y \iff \text{for every } \varepsilon>0 \text{ there is a } \delta>0 \text{ such that } |J[y]-J[\hat y]| < \varepsilon \text{ whenever } \|y - \hat y\| < \delta.$$

This is word-for-word the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ definition of continuity from calculus 1, with "$|x - \hat x| < \delta$" replaced by "$\|y-\hat y\|<\delta$." That substitution — swapping absolute value of a number-difference for norm of a function-difference — is the single trick that lets almost all of one-variable calculus intuition carry over to functionals.

One last important caveat, which will matter as soon as we impose boundary conditions: the set of admissible curves for a variational problem is usually not itself a linear space. For instance, in the fixed-endpoint problem, admissible curves must satisfy $y(a) = A$, $y(b) = B$; the sum of two such curves satisfies $y(a) = 2A$, not $A$, so it's not admissible. This is the exact analogue of the fact that, in ordinary calculus, an affine constraint set (like a line not through the origin) is not a linear subspace either — yet we still freely talk about distances between its points. The same is true here: even though the admissible curves don't form a vector space, the ambient normed space they sit inside of still gives us a perfectly good notion of distance between them.

03The Variation of a Functional. A Necessary Condition for an Extremum

This is the most abstract section of the chapter, but also the one carrying all the conceptual weight. Push through it once, carefully, and Section 4 will feel almost automatic.

3.1 Linear functionals, and the two lemmas that make everything else possible

Linear functionals. A functional $\varphi[h]$ (defined on a normed linear space) is called a linear functional if:

  1. $\varphi[\alpha h] = \alpha\, \varphi[h]$ for every real $\alpha$;
  2. $\varphi[h_1+h_2] = \varphi[h_1]+\varphi[h_2]$;
  3. $\varphi$ is continuous.

This is exactly the notion of a linear map to $\mathbb{R}$ from linear algebra, specialized to a possibly infinite-dimensional space, with a continuity requirement bolted on (continuity is automatic in finite dimensions, but not in general infinite-dimensional spaces, so it has to be assumed explicitly here). Four examples the book gives, all worth internalizing since they'll recur constantly:

  • $\varphi[h] = h(x_0)$ (evaluate at a fixed point) is linear.
  • $\varphi[h] = \int_a^b h(x)\,dx$ is linear.
  • $\varphi[h] = \int_a^b \alpha(x) h(x)\,dx$, for any fixed continuous weight function $\alpha(x)$, is linear.
  • More generally, $\varphi[h] = \int_a^b \bigl[\alpha_0(x)h(x) + \alpha_1(x)h'(x) + \cdots + \alpha_n(x)h^{(n)}(x)\bigr]dx$ is linear (on $\mathscr{D}_n$).

Why we need lemmas at all. The strategy of this whole chapter is: derive a formula that says "some fixed function $\alpha(x)$, integrated against an arbitrary test function $h(x)$ (subject to some boundary condition), always gives zero" — and then we want to conclude that $\alpha(x)$ itself must be identically zero (or some other simple thing). This step — going from "integrates to zero against every test function" to "must itself be zero" — is not obvious and needs a genuine proof. That proof is supplied by the following lemma, often called the fundamental lemma of the calculus of variations.

Lemma 1

If $\alpha(x)$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, and $\int_a^b \alpha(x)h(x)\,dx = 0$ for every continuous $h(x)$ with $h(a)=h(b)=0$, then $\alpha(x)\equiv 0$ on $[a,b]$.

Proof, unpacked. Suppose, for contradiction, that $\alpha(x_1) \ne 0$ for some point $x_1$ — say $\alpha(x_1) > 0$ (the negative case is identical with signs flipped). Since $\alpha$ is continuous, it stays positive on some small interval $[x_1', x_2']$ around $x_1$ (this is just the "continuous functions don't jump" fact from calc 1: if a continuous function is positive somewhere, it's positive in a whole neighborhood). Now here is the clever move: we get to choose $h$ ourselves, since the hypothesis holds for every admissible $h$. So we manufacture a specific test function that is a nuisance everywhere except exactly where $\alpha$ is known to be positive:
$$h(x) = \begin{cases}(x-x_1')(x_2'-x) & x \in [x_1',x_2'] \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.}\end{cases}$$
Check: this $h$ is continuous, satisfies $h(a)=h(b)=0$ (it's zero outside $[x_1',x_2']$, which is strictly inside $[a,b]$), so it's a legal test function. But then
$$\int_a^b \alpha(x)h(x)\,dx = \int_{x_1'}^{x_2'}\alpha(x)\underbrace{(x-x_1')(x_2'-x)}_{>0 \text{ strictly inside}}\,dx > 0,$$
since the integrand is a product of two positive continuous functions on the open interval (the parabola $(x-x_1')(x_2'-x)$ is positive strictly between its two roots). This contradicts the hypothesis that the integral is always zero. Since assuming $\alpha(x_1)\ne 0$ leads to a contradiction, we must have $\alpha(x)=0$ for all $x$. $\blacksquare$

Notice the shape of this argument, because it recurs constantly in this subject: assume the conclusion fails at some point; use continuity to get a whole neighborhood where it fails; build a bump function supported exactly on that neighborhood; plug it in and derive a nonzero integral, contradicting the hypothesis. This is sometimes summarized as: "the identity holds for arbitrary $h$, so in particular it holds for an $h$ engineered to detect exactly where $\alpha$ misbehaves."

Lemma 2

If $\alpha(x)$ is continuous on $[a,b]$, and $\int_a^b \alpha(x) h'(x)\,dx = 0$ for every $h \in \mathscr{D}_1(a,b)$ with $h(a)=h(b)=0$, then $\alpha(x) = c$ (a constant) on $[a,b]$.

This time the test function multiplies $h'(x)$ instead of $h(x)$, and the conclusion is a constant instead of zero — so we can't reuse Lemma 1's bump-function trick directly. Instead, here's the idea: let $c$ be the average value of $\alpha$ in the specific sense that $\int_a^b[\alpha(x)-c]\,dx = 0$ (this equation determines $c$ uniquely — it's just saying $c$ is the mean of $\alpha$ over $[a,b]$). Now define
$$h(x) = \int_a^x [\alpha(\xi)-c]\,d\xi.$$
By the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus, $h'(x) = \alpha(x)-c$, so $h$ is automatically in $\mathscr{D}_1$; also $h(a)=0$ trivially, and $h(b)=0$ by our choice of $c$. So $h$ is a legal test function. Plugging into the hypothesis:
$$0 = \int_a^b \alpha(x)h'(x)\,dx = \int_a^b [\alpha(x)-c]h'(x)\,dx + c\int_a^b h'(x)\,dx = \int_a^b[\alpha(x)-c]^2\,dx + c\bigl[h(b)-h(a)\bigr].$$
The last bracket is zero, and we're left with $\int_a^b[\alpha(x)-c]^2\,dx = 0$. Since the integrand is a nonnegative continuous function whose integral is zero, the integrand must be identically zero (this is a standard calc-1-level fact: a nonnegative continuous function with zero integral is the zero function), i.e., $\alpha(x) = c$ everywhere. $\blacksquare$

The next one — Lemma 4 (skipping Lemma 3, which is only needed in Chapter 8, out of our current scope) — is used repeatedly starting in Section 4, so let's understand it now.

Lemma 4

If $\alpha(x),\beta(x)$ are continuous on $[a,b]$, and $\int_a^b[\alpha(x)h(x)+\beta(x)h'(x)]\,dx = 0$ for every $h\in\mathscr{D}_1(a,b)$ with $h(a)=h(b)=0$, then $\beta$ is differentiable and $\beta'(x)=\alpha(x)$.

The trick: define $A(x) = \int_a^x \alpha(\xi)\,d\xi$, so $A'(x)=\alpha(x)$. Integrate the first term by parts:
$$\int_a^b \alpha(x)h(x)\,dx = \Bigl[A(x)h(x)\Bigr]_a^b - \int_a^b A(x)h'(x)\,dx = -\int_a^b A(x)h'(x)\,dx$$
(the boundary term vanishes because $h(a)=h(b)=0$). Substituting this into the hypothesis converts it into
$$\int_a^b\bigl[-A(x)+\beta(x)\bigr]h'(x)\,dx = 0 \quad \text{for all admissible } h,$$
which is now exactly the setup of Lemma 2! Applying Lemma 2 tells us $-A(x)+\beta(x) = \text{const}$, i.e., $\beta(x) = A(x) + \text{const}$, and differentiating both sides gives $\beta'(x) = A'(x) = \alpha(x)$, as claimed. $\blacksquare$

The overall lesson from this whole subsection: integration by parts lets you shuttle derivatives off of the test function $h$ and onto the known functions $\alpha,\beta$, converting an "integrates to zero against $h'$" statement into an "integrates to zero against $h$" statement (Lemma 1's territory) or vice versa, at the cost of picking up a derivative or an integral on the known side of the equation. This move is the single most-used algebraic maneuver in the entire book.

3.2 The variation (differential) of a functional

Now we build the functional analogue of the differential $df = f'(x)\,dx$ from ordinary calculus.

Let $J[y]$ be a functional. For a small "perturbation" $h = h(x)$ of $y$, define the increment
$$\Delta J[h] = J[y+h] - J[y].$$
For fixed $y$, this is some functional of $h$ — generally a nonlinear one. Suppose it can be split as
$$\Delta J[h] = \varphi[h] + \varepsilon\|h\|, \qquad \text{where } \varphi[h] \text{ is linear and } \varepsilon \to 0 \text{ as } \|h\|\to 0.$$
Then $J[y]$ is called differentiable, $\varphi[h]$ is called the variation (or differential) of $J[y]$, denoted $\delta J[h]$.

Compare directly to ordinary calculus: if $f(x)$ is differentiable, then $f(x+h)-f(x) = f'(x)h + \varepsilon h$ where $\varepsilon \to 0$ as $h \to 0$. The term $f'(x)h$ — linear in $h$, and dominating the error term — is exactly $df$. The variation $\delta J[h]$ plays the identical role: it is the part of the change in $J$ that depends linearly on the perturbation $h$, and it captures everything about $\Delta J$ up to (and not including) higher-order corrections.

Theorem 1 (uniqueness)

The variation of a differentiable functional is unique — i.e., there's only one linear functional $\varphi$ that can play this role.

Why: Suppose $\varphi[h] \to 0$ as $\|h\|\to 0$ faster than linearly, meaning $\varphi[h]/\|h\| \to 0$. If $\varphi$ is linear but not identically zero, pick $h_0$ with $\varphi[h_0] \ne 0$, and consider the shrinking sequence $h_n = h_0/n$. Then $\|h_n\|\to 0$, but $\varphi[h_n]/\|h_n\| = \varphi[h_0]/\|h_0\|$ is a fixed nonzero constant, not going to zero — contradiction. So any linear functional that is a "higher-order-than-linear" quantity must be identically zero. Applying this to the difference of two candidate variations $\varphi_1 - \varphi_2$ (which is linear, and is higher-order by construction) shows $\varphi_1 \equiv \varphi_2$. $\blacksquare$

3.3 Extrema of functionals; weak vs. strong

Just as $F(\hat x)$ is a (relative) minimum of a multivariable function if $F(x) - F(\hat x) \ge 0$ for all $x$ near $\hat x$, we say $J[\hat y]$ is a (relative) extremum of the functional $J[y]$ if $J[y]-J[\hat y]$ doesn't change sign for all admissible $y$ near $\hat y$.

But "near" in what sense? This is where Section 2's work pays off, and it introduces a new subtlety that has no counterpart in ordinary calculus:

  • $J[\hat y]$ is a weak extremum if the sign condition holds for all admissible $y$ with $\|y-\hat y\|_1 < \varepsilon$ (closeness measured in the $\mathscr{D}_1$ norm — both height and slope must be close).
  • $J[\hat y]$ is a strong extremum if the sign condition holds for all admissible $y$ with $\|y-\hat y\|_0 < \varepsilon$ (closeness measured only in the $\mathscr{C}$ norm — only height must be close; slopes can differ wildly).

Which is the more demanding requirement? At first glance it may look backwards, so slow down here: any curve that is $\mathscr{D}_1$-close to $\hat y$ (close in both height and slope) is automatically $\mathscr{C}$-close to $\hat y$ (close in height alone) — that direction is free, since dropping a requirement only enlarges the set of curves satisfying what's left. So the collection of competitor curves you must "beat" to be a strong extremum (all curves within $\mathscr{C}$-distance $\varepsilon$, which includes wildly-sloped wiggly curves squeezed into a thin horizontal band) is much larger than the collection you must beat to be a weak extremum (only the tamer curves that are close in both height and slope). Beating a bigger field of competitors is a strictly harder task. Hence:

$$\text{strong extremum} \implies \text{weak extremum}, \qquad \text{but not conversely.}$$

A curve can be a weak extremum (unbeatable among gently-varying nearby curves) while some wildly zigzagging curve, that stays visually close in height but has totally different slopes, beats it — in which case it fails to be a strong extremum. Since the functionals we study (of the form $\int F(x,y,y')dx$) are typically continuous in the $\mathscr{D}_1$ norm but not in the $\mathscr{C}$ norm (recall the arc-length discussion in Section 2.3), weak extrema are the natural, easier target, and that's what Chapters 1–5 focus on; strong extrema require the extra machinery of Chapter 6.

3.4 The necessary condition: the variation must vanish

Theorem 2

A necessary condition for a differentiable functional $J[y]$ to have an extremum at $y=\hat y$ is that $\delta J[h] = 0$ for $y = \hat y$ and all admissible $h$.

Proof idea, in words first: This is the exact functional analogue of "if $f$ has a minimum at $\hat x$, then $f'(\hat x)=0$." The one-variable proof works by noting that if $f'(\hat x)\ne 0$, then $f$ increases in one direction away from $\hat x$ and decreases in the other, so $\hat x$ can't be an extremum. The proof here runs the identical argument, with $h$ playing the role of "direction to move," and using the fact that we can flip the sign of $h$ (replacing $h$ by $-h$) and rescale it (replacing $h$ by $\alpha h$ for any small $\alpha$) freely, since both remain valid "admissible perturbations."

Proof in full: Suppose $J$ has a minimum at $\hat y$, so $\Delta J[h] = J[\hat y+h]-J[\hat y] \ge 0$ for all sufficiently small admissible $h$. We know $\Delta J[h] = \delta J[h] + \varepsilon\|h\|$ with $\varepsilon \to 0$. Suppose, for contradiction, that $\delta J[h_0] \ne 0$ for some admissible $h_0$. Since $\delta J$ is linear, $\delta J[-\alpha h_0] = -\delta J[\alpha h_0] = -\alpha\,\delta J[h_0]$ for any $\alpha > 0$. As $\alpha \to 0^+$, the term $\varepsilon\|{\pm}\alpha h_0\|$ becomes negligible compared to $\alpha\,\delta J[h_0]$ (since it's the higher-order piece), so the sign of $\Delta J[\alpha h_0]$ matches the sign of $\delta J[\alpha h_0] = \alpha\,\delta J[h_0]$, and the sign of $\Delta J[-\alpha h_0]$ matches the sign of $-\alpha\,\delta J[h_0]$ — opposite signs, for arbitrarily small perturbations. But this contradicts $\Delta J[h] \ge 0$ for all small admissible $h$ (one of these two perturbations would have to make $\Delta J$ negative). This contradiction forces $\delta J[h] = 0$ for every admissible $h$. $\blacksquare$

This theorem is the engine of the entire subject: it converts the search for an extremum into the search for a curve where a certain linear functional identically vanishes — and Section 4 shows that "the variation vanishes" is exactly equivalent to a differential equation.

(One remark worth flagging for later: in ordinary calculus, a vanishing first derivative is necessary but not sufficient for a minimum — you also need the second derivative to be nonnegative. The exact same caveat applies here, and the functional analogue of the second-derivative test is developed in Chapter 5, using the "second variation." For now, just as in a first calculus course, we'll use vanishing of the first variation as our main tool, and rely on the physical or geometric context to tell us we've actually found a genuine extremum.)

04The Simplest Variational Problem. Euler's Equation
Background primer

A short primer on multivariable Taylor expansion (what you need, no more)

Before diving in, one calc-3-flavored tool needs to be introduced carefully, since Chapter 1 leans on it right away: partial derivatives, and the two-variable version of Taylor's theorem.

If $F(x,y,z)$ is a function of three ordinary variables, the partial derivative $F_y$ (also written $\partial F/\partial y$) means: differentiate $F$ with respect to $y$ while treating $x$ and $z$ as fixed constants. Likewise $F_z$ differentiates with respect to $z$ holding $x,y$ fixed. For example, if $F(x,y,z) = y^2 + xz^2$, then $F_y = 2y$ and $F_z = 2xz$ — completely mechanical, just ordinary single-variable differentiation applied to whichever letter you're told to differentiate by, freezing the others.

The only other fact we need is the multivariable analogue of "$f(x+h) \approx f(x) + f'(x)h$" (linear approximation / first-order Taylor expansion). For a function of several variables, if you nudge all of the inputs slightly, the total change is (to first order) the sum of the changes due to nudging each input separately:
$$F(x, y+\eta, z+\zeta) - F(x,y,z) \approx F_y(x,y,z)\,\eta + F_z(x,y,z)\,\zeta,$$
with an error that is "higher order" (goes to zero faster than $\eta,\zeta$ themselves as they shrink). This is really just the single-variable Taylor expansion applied one variable at a time and added up — nothing more exotic is required for this chapter.

4.1 Setting up the problem and deriving Euler's equation

The simplest variational problem. Let $F(x,y,z)$ have continuous first and second partial derivatives. Among all continuously differentiable functions $y(x)$ on $[a,b]$ satisfying the fixed boundary conditions
$$y(a) = A, \qquad y(b) = B,$$
find the one that gives a weak extremum of
$$J[y] = \int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,dx.$$

Derivation. Perturb $y(x)$ by $h(x)$, where — in order for $y+h$ to still satisfy the boundary conditions — we must have
$$h(a) = h(b) = 0.$$
(This is the key structural fact: because both endpoints are pinned down, the space of legal perturbations $h$ consists exactly of functions vanishing at both ends. Keep this in mind — it's precisely the hypothesis under which Lemmas 1, 2, and 4 were proved!)

Compute the increment:
$$\Delta J = \int_a^b\Bigl[F(x,y+h,y'+h') - F(x,y,y')\Bigr]dx.$$
Applying the linear (first-order) Taylor expansion described above, with $\eta = h$ and $\zeta = h'$:
$$\Delta J = \int_a^b\bigl[F_y(x,y,y')h + F_{y'}(x,y,y')h'\bigr]dx + (\text{higher-order terms}).$$
The leading integral, being linear in $h$ and $h'$, is the variation:
$$\delta J = \int_a^b\bigl[F_y h + F_{y'} h'\bigr]dx.$$

By Theorem 2, a necessary condition for an extremum is $\delta J = 0$ for every admissible $h$ (i.e., every $h$ with $h(a)=h(b)=0$):
$$\int_a^b\bigl(F_y h + F_{y'}h'\bigr)dx = 0.$$
This is exactly the hypothesis of Lemma 4 (Section 3.1), with $\alpha = F_y$ and $\beta = F_{y'}$! Lemma 4 tells us immediately that $\beta$ is differentiable with $\beta' = \alpha$, i.e.,
$$\boxed{F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} = 0.} \tag{\text{Euler's equation}}$$

This is Euler's equation. Its solutions are called extremals. It is, generically, a second-order ordinary differential equation (differentiating $F_{y'}(x,y,y')$ with respect to $x$ using the chain rule brings in $y''$), so its general solution has two arbitrary constants, which get pinned down by the two boundary conditions $y(a)=A$, $y(b)=B$.

A subtlety worth flagging explicitly, because it's easy to breeze past: notice that we never assumed $y''$ exists in order to derive Euler's equation — the derivation only used $y$ and $y'$ being continuous. The existence of the term $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}$ (and hence of $y''$, generically) is something Lemma 4 proves, not something we had to assume in advance. This is a delicate logical point that the book flags with its own footnote, and it matters: it means Euler's equation makes sense even for curves that turn out not to be twice differentiable everywhere (see Theorem 3 below for exactly when smoothness is guaranteed, and the worked example right after it for a case where it fails).

Solving Euler's equation, by itself, gives you candidates for the extremum — it's a necessary condition, not a sufficient one (exactly parallel to $f'(\hat x)=0$ being necessary but not sufficient for a minimum in ordinary calculus). Often, though, you can tell from the geometry or physics of the problem that an extremum must exist, and if Euler's equation has only one solution meeting the boundary conditions, that solution has to be it.

Existence and uniqueness (Bernstein's theorem). Euler's equation, written as $y'' = \Phi(x,y,y')$ for some function $\Phi$, is a second-order ODE, and there is a classical worry in ODE theory: does a solution meeting given boundary values at both ends of a fixed, possibly long interval actually exist, and is it unique? (This is different from — and harder than — the usual "initial value problem" you may have seen, where you're only given data at one point.) Bernstein's theorem gives sufficient conditions (a growth bound on $F$ and a positivity bound on $F_y$) guaranteeing that exactly one such solution exists connecting any two boundary points with different $x$-coordinates. You don't need to memorize the technical hypotheses — just know that such a guarantee exists, and that it is not automatic in general (unlike initial-value problems, where local existence/uniqueness is essentially always available under mild smoothness).

4.2 When are extremals actually twice differentiable?

Theorem 3

If $y=y(x)$ has a continuous first derivative, satisfies Euler's equation, and $F$ has continuous first and second partial derivatives, then $y(x)$ automatically has a continuous second derivative at every point where $F_{y'y'}[x,y(x),y'(x)] \ne 0$.

The idea of the proof: Euler's equation says $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} = F_y$. Expanding $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}$ via the chain rule as $\Delta x \to 0$ produces three pieces — one involving $\Delta x$ directly, one involving $\Delta y/\Delta x \to y'$, and one involving $\Delta y'/\Delta x$ (which is exactly the difference quotient defining $y''$, if it exists). Since we're told the limit of the whole sum exists (it equals $F_y$, by Euler's equation) and the first two pieces are known to converge (by continuity and existence of $y'$), the third piece must also converge — and dividing by the (nonzero, by hypothesis) coefficient $F_{y'y'}$ isolates $\lim_{\Delta x\to0} \Delta y'/\Delta x = y''(x)$. So the limit defining $y''$ exists after all, purely as a consequence of Euler's equation holding, continuity of $F$'s second derivatives, and $F_{y'y'}\ne0$.

A cautionary example. Consider $J[y] = \int_{-1}^1 y^2(2x-y')^2\,dx$ with $y(-1)=0,y(1)=1$. One can check directly that the piecewise function
$$y(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & -1\le x\le 0\\ x^2 & 0 < x \le 1\end{cases}$$
achieves the minimum value $J[y]=0$, yet $y(x)$ has no second derivative at $x=0$ (the left piece is identically $0$, so its second derivative is $0$; the right piece $x^2$ has second derivative $2$ — they don't match, and in fact the first derivative barely matches: $y'(0^-)=0=y'(0^+)$, so $y$ is $C^1$ but not $C^2$ at $0$). Despite this, direct substitution shows $F_y, F_{y'},$ and $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}$ all vanish identically along this curve, so it satisfies Euler's equation as an identity, second derivative or no. The resolution: at $x=0$ here, $F_{y'y'} = 2y^2$ happens to vanish (since $y(0)=0$), so Theorem 3's hypothesis fails exactly at the one bad point — no contradiction.

4.3 Four special cases where Euler's equation simplifies

These four cases are worth memorizing, because a large fraction of concrete problems fall into one of them, letting you skip straight to a first-order equation or even a closed-form quadrature.

Case 1: $F=F(x,y')$ (no explicit $y$). Euler's equation becomes $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ directly (since $F_y \equiv 0$), giving the first integral
$$F_{y'} = C.$$
This is now only a first-order equation in $y$, which you solve for $y'$ as a function of $x$ (and the constant $C$), then integrate once more (a "quadrature").

Case 2: $F=F(y,y')$ (no explicit $x$). Here's a slick trick: multiply Euler's equation
$$F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} = F_y - F_{y'y}y' - F_{y'y'}y'' = 0$$
by $y'$:
$$F_y y' - F_{y'y}y'^2 - F_{y'y'}y'y'' = 0.$$
Now notice that the left side is exactly $\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(F - y'F_{y'}\bigr)$ — check this by differentiating $F(y,y')$ using the chain rule ($\frac{d}{dx}F = F_y y' + F_{y'}y''$) and $y'F_{y'}$ using the product rule ($\frac{d}{dx}(y'F_{y'}) = y''F_{y'} + y'\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} = y''F_{y'}+y'(F_{y'y}y'+F_{y'y'}y'')$); subtracting gives exactly the left-hand side above. So Euler's equation has the first integral
$$F - y'F_{y'} = C.$$
This is the variational-calculus analogue of energy conservation in mechanics (and indeed, that's literally what it becomes when $F$ is a mechanical Lagrangian not depending explicitly on time — a fact developed fully in Chapter 4).

Case 3: $F=F(x,y)$ (no $y'$ at all). Then Euler's equation collapses to the algebraic (non-differential!) equation $F_y(x,y)=0$, whose solution is one or more explicit curves $y=y(x)$ — no integration needed at all.

Case 4: $F(x,y,y') = f(x,y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ (a weight function times the arc-length element). This pattern shows up constantly (surface area of revolution, optical path length, etc.). Working through the derivative $F_{y'} = f(x,y)\,y'/\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ and substituting into Euler's equation, then multiplying through by $\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ to clear denominators, gives the equation
$$f_y - f_x y' - f\,\frac{y''}{1+y'^2} = 0.$$

Worked Example 1

Solve $J[y] = \int_1^2 \frac{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}{x}\,dx$, with $y(1)=0,\ y(2)=1$.

The integrand doesn't contain $y$ explicitly, so we're in Case 1: $F_{y'} = C$, i.e.
$$\frac{y'}{x\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = C \implies y'^2(1-C^2x^2) = C^2x^2 \implies y' = \frac{Cx}{\sqrt{1-C^2x^2}}.$$
Integrating (substitute $u = 1-C^2x^2$, $du = -2C^2x\,dx$):
$$y = \int\frac{Cx\,dx}{\sqrt{1-C^2x^2}} = -\frac{1}{C}\sqrt{1-C^2x^2} + C_1.$$
Rearranging: $(y-C_1)^2 = \frac1{C^2}(1-C^2x^2) = \frac1{C^2}-x^2$, i.e.
$$(y-C_1)^2 + x^2 = \frac{1}{C^2}$$
— a circle centered on the $y$-axis. Plugging in $y(1)=0$ and $y(2)=1$ and solving the resulting pair of equations for $C, C_1$ gives $C = 1/\sqrt5$, $C_1=2$, so the final answer is
$$(y-2)^2+x^2 = 5.$$

Worked Example 2 (minimal surface of revolution — the catenary)

Among curves joining $(x_0,y_0)$ to $(x_1,y_1)$, find the one whose rotation about the $x$-axis generates the surface of least area. The area of the surface of revolution is $2\pi\int y\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$, so (dropping the constant $2\pi$, which doesn't affect where the extremum is) $F = y\sqrt{1+y'^2}$, with no explicit $x$ — Case 2 applies:
$$F - y'F_{y'} = C \implies y\sqrt{1+y'^2} - y'\cdot\frac{yy'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = C \implies \frac{y}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = C$$
(the two terms combine over a common denominator and the numerator simplifies to just $y$). Solving for $y'$:
$$y' = \frac{\sqrt{y^2-C^2}}{C}.$$
This separates: $\dfrac{C\,dy}{\sqrt{y^2-C^2}} = dx$. Using the standard antiderivative $\int \frac{dy}{\sqrt{y^2-C^2}} = \ln\left(\frac{y+\sqrt{y^2-C^2}}{C}\right)$ (or, equivalently, $\cosh^{-1}(y/C)$), integrating gives
$$x + C_1 = C\ln\frac{y+\sqrt{y^2-C^2}}{C} \iff y = C\cosh\frac{x+C_1}{C}.$$
This curve — a hyperbolic cosine — is called a catenary (it's also, famously, the shape a hanging chain takes under gravity). The surface generated by rotating it is a catenoid. Depending on the specific location of the two boundary points, there may be one such catenary through them, two (in which case only one actually minimizes area — the other is merely a "stationary" extremal, not a true minimum), or none at all — in the last case, the actual area-minimizing surface degenerates into two flat end-disks joined by a segment of the axis (a "broken" or degenerate solution, which is not smooth — we revisit this exact example in Chapter 3's discussion of broken extremals).

Quick Example 3

For $J[y]=\int_a^b (x-y)^2\,dx$, the integrand $F=(x-y)^2$ doesn't involve $y'$ — Case 3 — so Euler's equation is the algebraic equation $F_y = -2(x-y)=0$, i.e., $y=x$, the straight line (and indeed direct substitution shows the integral is exactly $0$ along this line, its minimum possible value since the integrand is a square).

05The Case of Several Variables
Background primer

A primer: partial derivatives, double integrals, and Green's theorem (what you need, briefly)

This section generalizes everything above from curves $y=y(x)$ to surfaces $z=z(x,y)$. Three multivariable ideas are needed, so let's build them from the ground up.

Partial derivatives of a two-variable function. If $z=z(x,y)$, then $z_x$ (or $\partial z/\partial x$) means: differentiate with respect to $x$ holding $y$ fixed; $z_y$ means the reverse. Geometrically, if you slice the surface $z=z(x,y)$ with a plane of constant $y$, you get an ordinary curve in $x$, and $z_x$ is its ordinary slope.

Double integrals. Just as $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$ is (informally) "sum up $f(x)\,dx$ over infinitesimal slices of the interval $[a,b]$," the double integral $\iint_R g(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ is "sum up $g(x,y)$ times the area of an infinitesimal little square $dx\,dy$, over every such square inside the region $R$." You can compute one by integrating over $x$ for each fixed $y$, then integrating the result over $y$ (or the other order) — but for this section, you don't need to compute any double integrals; you just need to know they represent "total amount of $g$ accumulated over a two-dimensional region $R$," directly generalizing what a single integral does over an interval.

Green's theorem. This is the one new tool, and we'll just state it, since it's used as a black box (an off-the-shelf identity, exactly the way you'd use the Fundamental Theorem of Calculus as a black box). For a region $R$ with boundary curve $\Gamma$ (traversed counterclockwise), and functions $P(x,y), Q(x,y)$ with continuous partial derivatives,
$$\iint_R\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy = \oint_\Gamma (P\,dx + Q\,dy).$$
Intuitively: Green's theorem is the two-dimensional cousin of "integration by parts" — it lets you convert an integral of a derivative over the interior of a region into an integral over just the boundary of the region, at the cost of a sign and a swap of which function gets differentiated. That's exactly the role integration by parts played in Lemma 4 above, and it's exactly the role Green's theorem will play in a moment.

5.1 Setting up the two-dimensional problem

Let $F(x,y,z,p,q)$ have continuous first and second partial derivatives, and consider the functional
$$J[z] = \iint_R F(x,y,z,z_x,z_y)\,dx\,dy, \tag{20}$$
where $R$ is some fixed closed region in the plane, and we seek $z=z(x,y)$ (with continuous first and second partials in $R$) taking prescribed values on the boundary curve $\Gamma$ of $R$, such that $J[z]$ has an extremum.

This is the exact two-dimensional analogue of the simplest problem in Section 4: $z(x,y)$ plays the role of $y(x)$, and $z_x, z_y$ play the role of $y'$.

5.2 The two-dimensional fundamental lemma

Lemma

If $\alpha(x,y)$ is continuous on a closed region $R$, and $\iint_R \alpha(x,y)h(x,y)\,dx\,dy = 0$ for every function $h$ with continuous first and second derivatives in $R$ that vanishes on the boundary $\Gamma$, then $\alpha \equiv 0$ in $R$.

The proof is a direct copy of Lemma 1's strategy: if $\alpha(x_0,y_0)>0$ somewhere, it stays positive on a small disk $(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2\le \varepsilon^2$ around that point (continuity again), and the bump function
$$h(x,y) = \bigl[(x-x_0)^2+(y-y_0)^2-\varepsilon^2\bigr]^3 \text{ inside the disk, and } 0 \text{ outside}$$
is a legitimate test function (it and its first derivatives vanish smoothly at the edge of the disk, matching zero outside) that makes the integral strictly positive — a contradiction, exactly as before.

5.3 Deriving Euler's equation for the surface problem

Perturb $z$ by $h(x,y)$, vanishing on $\Gamma$ (so the boundary values stay fixed). Exactly as in Section 4.1 (multivariable Taylor expansion, now with three perturbed inputs $h, h_x, h_y$ instead of two):
$$\Delta J = \iint_R\bigl[F_z h + F_{z_x}h_x + F_{z_y}h_y\bigr]dx\,dy + (\text{higher order}),$$
so the variation is
$$\delta J = \iint_R\bigl(F_z h + F_{z_x}h_x + F_{z_y}h_y\bigr)dx\,dy.$$

Now we need to move the derivatives off of $h_x, h_y$ and onto the known functions $F_{z_x}, F_{z_y}$ — the job Green's theorem was built for. Apply Green's theorem with $P = -F_{z_y}h$ and $Q = F_{z_x}h$:
$$\iint_R\bigl(F_{z_x}h_x + F_{z_y}h_y\bigr)dx\,dy = \iint_R\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(F_{z_x}h)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(F_{z_y}h)\right]dx\,dy - \iint_R\left(\frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{z_x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}F_{z_y}\right)h\,dx\,dy,$$
where the first integral on the right equals a boundary integral over $\Gamma$ (by Green's theorem, since it's the integral of $\partial Q/\partial x - \partial P/\partial y$ for exactly this choice of $P,Q$) that vanishes, because $h=0$ everywhere on $\Gamma$. So:
$$\delta J = \iint_R \left(F_z - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{z_x} - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}F_{z_y}\right)h\,dx\,dy.$$
Setting $\delta J = 0$ for all admissible $h$ and invoking the two-dimensional fundamental lemma above gives the two-dimensional Euler's equation:
$$\boxed{F_z - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{z_x} - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}F_{z_y} = 0.} \tag{24}$$

This is now a second-order partial differential equation for $z(x,y)$, to be solved subject to the prescribed boundary values on $\Gamma$.

A geometric application (minimal surfaces, for interest — not required for mastery of the core method). Taking $F = \sqrt{1+z_x^2+z_y^2}$ (the surface-area functional, generalizing arc length), equation (24) works out to
$$r(1+q^2) - 2spq + t(1+p^2) = 0, \qquad p=z_x,\ q=z_y,\ r=z_{xx},\ s=z_{xy},\ t=z_{yy}.$$
This combination of second partials is, up to a positive factor, exactly the mean curvature of the surface $z=z(x,y)$ (a quantity from differential geometry measuring how the surface curves, averaged over all directions). So Euler's equation for the surface-area functional says: the extremal surfaces are exactly those with zero mean curvature everywhere — these are called minimal surfaces (soap films spanning a wire frame are physical examples). You don't need differential geometry background to take the main point: the abstract machine built in this section (perturb, expand, integrate by parts via Green's theorem, invoke the fundamental lemma) reproduces, as a special case, a genuine geometric fact about soap films.

06A Simple Variable End Point Problem

So far, both endpoints of the curve have been pinned to fixed points. This section studies the first departure from that: what if the endpoints are allowed to slide along two fixed vertical lines $x=a$ and $x=b$ (i.e., the $x$-coordinates of the endpoints stay fixed, but the $y$-values at each end are free)?

6.1 Deriving the boundary conditions

We compute the variation exactly as in Section 4.1 — except that now $h(x)$ need not vanish at $x=a,b$ (since $y$ is free to end anywhere on those two vertical lines). This time, when we integrate the $F_{y'}h'$ term by parts, we must keep the boundary terms:
$$\delta J = \int_a^b F_yh\,dx + \int_a^b F_{y'}h'\,dx = \int_a^b F_y h\,dx + \Bigl[F_{y'}h\Bigr]_a^b - \int_a^b\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\cdot h\,dx,$$
i.e.
$$\delta J = \int_a^b\left(F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}\right)h(x)\,dx + F_{y'}h(x)\Big|_{x=b} - F_{y'}h(x)\Big|_{x=a}. \tag{27}$$

Now the logic proceeds in two stages, and this two-stage pattern — "first restrict to a sub-class of perturbations to get the differential equation, then use the remaining freedom to get boundary conditions" — is the master template for every variable-endpoint problem in the rest of the book (and reappears fully generalized in Chapter 3).

Stage 1. Restrict attention, for a moment, to only those perturbations $h$ that do happen to vanish at both ends ($h(a)=h(b)=0$) — a special case of the freedom we have. For these, the boundary terms in (27) drop out automatically, and $\delta J=0$ forces (exactly as in Section 4) the Euler equation
$$F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} = 0.$$
So: whatever curve solves this variable-endpoint problem must, in particular, be an extremal.

Stage 2. Now that we know $y$ is an extremal, the integral term in (27) vanishes identically (Euler's equation holds pointwise), leaving
$$\delta J = F_{y'}h(x)\big|_{x=b} - F_{y'}h(x)\big|_{x=a}.$$
But now $h(a)$ and $h(b)$ are completely free real numbers (nothing constrains them anymore, since the endpoint $y$-values are free) — so the only way $\delta J=0$ can hold for every choice of $h(a),h(b)$ is if each coefficient vanishes separately:
$$\boxed{F_{y'}\Big|_{x=a} = 0, \qquad F_{y'}\Big|_{x=b}=0.} \tag{29}$$

These are called the natural boundary conditions — "natural" because, unlike the fixed-endpoint conditions $y(a)=A$, they aren't imposed by hand; they emerge automatically from the requirement of being an extremum. To actually solve a variable-endpoint problem: solve Euler's equation to get the general solution (with its two arbitrary constants), then use (29) — instead of prescribed $y$-values — to pin down those two constants.

In a mixed case (one end fixed, e.g. $y(a)=A$, the other free along $x=b$), only the single natural condition $F_{y'}|_{x=b}=0$ survives, paired with the ordinary fixed condition $y(a)=A$.

6.2 Worked example: the brachistochrone to a vertical line

Problem. A bead starts at the origin $P=(0,0)$ and slides frictionlessly under gravity. Find the curve along which it reaches the vertical line $x=b$ (some fixed $b\ne0$) in the shortest possible time — the endpoint's height on that line is unconstrained.

Setting up the functional. If $v = ds/dt$ is the speed at height $y$ below the start, energy conservation for a particle falling under gravity gives $v = \sqrt{2gy}$ (all the potential energy lost, $mgy$, becomes kinetic energy $\frac12 mv^2$). Since $ds = \sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$, we get $dt = \frac{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}{\sqrt{2gy}}dx$, so the total transit time is
$$T[y] = \int_0^b \frac{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}{\sqrt{2gy}}\,dx.$$

Solving Euler's equation. Here $F = \sqrt{1+y'^2}/\sqrt{2gy}$ has no explicit $x$ — Case 2 from Section 4.3. Working through $F-y'F_{y'}=C$ for this $F$ (the algebra is a standard but slightly involved computation; the book simply states the result, which is a classical fact about the brachistochrone) leads to the parametrized family of cycloids
$$x = r(\theta - \sin\theta) + c, \qquad y = r(1-\cos\theta),$$
for constants $r, c$ (a cycloid is the path traced by a fixed point on the rim of a circle of radius $r$ as it rolls along a line — here rolling along the $x$-axis, generating the curve "upside down" relative to the usual picture, since $y$ measures depth here).

Applying the boundary conditions. Since the curve must pass through the origin, $c=0$. The natural boundary condition at the free end, $x=b$, is $F_{y'}=0$ there, i.e.
$$\frac{y'}{\sqrt{2gy}\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = 0 \quad \text{at } x=b \iff y'=0 \text{ at } x=b,$$
meaning the tangent to the optimal curve must be horizontal exactly at the free endpoint. Working out where the cycloid parametrization has a horizontal tangent (this happens at $\theta=\pi$, where $dx/d\theta = r(1-\cos\theta)=2r\ne0$ but $dy/d\theta = r\sin\theta = 0$) and matching $x(\pi) = r\pi = b$ pins down $r = b/\pi$. The final answer:
$$x = \frac{b}{\pi}(\theta - \sin\theta), \qquad y = \frac{b}{\pi}(1-\cos\theta).$$

Notice how cleanly the two-stage method worked: Euler's equation (an ODE, no boundary data needed yet) told us the curve had to be some cycloid; the natural boundary condition — a fact we would never have guessed from physical intuition alone — told us which cycloid, by translating "no constraint on the ending height" into the precise geometric fact "the curve must arrive horizontally."

07The Variational Derivative

7.1 Motivation

In ordinary multivariable calculus, a necessary condition for an extremum of $F(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is that every partial derivative $\partial F/\partial x_k$ vanishes. Section 3 showed the functional analogue is "the variation vanishes," and Section 4 converted that into Euler's equation. This section closes the loop by showing that Euler's equation is, quite literally, the statement "a certain derivative, called the variational derivative $\delta J/\delta y$, vanishes at every point $x$" — making the analogy with ordinary partial derivatives completely precise, not just suggestive.

7.2 Building the variational derivative via finite differences

Return to Euler's finite-difference approximation from Section 1.4: chop $[a,b]$ into $n+1$ equal pieces of width $\Delta x$, and approximate $J[y]$ by the ordinary function
$$J(y_1,\dots,y_n) = \sum_{i=0}^n F\left(x_i, y_i, \frac{y_{i+1}-y_i}{\Delta x}\right)\Delta x.$$
Since $y_k$ appears in exactly two terms of this sum (the $i=k$ term, where it's the "current" height, and the $i=k-1$ term, where it's the "previous" height feeding into that segment's slope), the ordinary partial derivative works out to
$$\frac{\partial J}{\partial y_k} = F_y\left(x_k,y_k,\frac{y_{k+1}-y_k}{\Delta x}\right)\Delta x - \left[F_{y'}\left(x_k,y_k,\frac{y_{k+1}-y_k}{\Delta x}\right) - F_{y'}\left(x_{k-1},y_{k-1},\frac{y_k-y_{k-1}}{\Delta x}\right)\right].$$
As $\Delta x \to 0$, this whole expression $\to 0$ (it's manifestly of order $\Delta x$) — not very informative by itself. But look at the denominator hiding in this picture: the quantity $\Delta y_k \cdot \Delta x$ (the change in $y_k$ needed to bump the polygonal curve at $x_k$, times the width of the interval there) has a direct geometric meaning — it's approximately the area swept out between the original polygonal curve and the bumped one. Dividing $\partial J/\partial y_k$ by $\Delta x$ (equivalently, by this area, per unit bump height) and taking $\Delta x \to 0$ produces a genuine, nonzero limit:
$$\frac{\delta J}{\delta y} := \lim_{\Delta x \to 0}\frac{1}{\Delta x}\frac{\partial J}{\partial y_k} = F_y(x,y,y') - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}(x,y,y').$$

This limit is called the variational derivative of $J[y]$. Notice: it is exactly the left-hand side of Euler's equation. So Euler's equation
$$F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} = 0$$
can be restated as simply
$$\frac{\delta J}{\delta y} = 0 \quad \text{at every point } x,$$
which is now a word-for-word copy of "every partial derivative vanishes at a critical point" — except here there are infinitely many "partial derivatives," one for every point $x \in [a,b]$, and each one is a number, $\delta J/\delta y$ evaluated at that particular $x$.

7.3 The general definition

More generally (without appealing to the finite-difference limit), one can define the variational derivative directly: perturb $y(x)$ by a bump $h(x)$ that's nonzero only in a small neighborhood of a point $x_0$, let $\Delta\sigma$ denote the (small) area between the bump curve $y=h(x)$ and the $x$-axis, and define
$$\frac{\delta J}{\delta y}\bigg|_{x=x_0} := \lim_{\Delta\sigma \to 0} \frac{J[y+h]-J[y]}{\Delta\sigma}$$
(the limit taken as both the height and the width of the bump shrink to zero together). One can show — though we won't belabor the proof — that the variational derivative obeys all the same formal rules as an ordinary derivative (sum rule, product rule, chain rule, etc.), which is part of why it's such a satisfying piece of notation: it really does behave like "differentiating $J$ with respect to the value of $y$ at the single point $x_0$," treating $J$ as if it were an ordinary (if infinite-dimensional) function.

08Invariance of Euler's Equation

8.1 The question

Suppose you switch from the usual rectangular coordinates $(x,y)$ to some new curvilinear coordinates $(u,v)$, via
$$x=x(u,v), \qquad y=y(u,v), \qquad \text{with the Jacobian } \begin{vmatrix}x_u & x_v\\ y_u& y_v\end{vmatrix}\ne 0$$
(the nonzero-Jacobian condition just says the change of coordinates doesn't collapse areas to zero — it's invertible near each point, exactly as in a change-of-variables you'd do in an ordinary multivariable integral). A curve $y=y(x)$ becomes some curve $v=v(u)$ in the new coordinates, and the functional $J[y]=\int F(x,y,y')dx$ becomes some new functional $J_1[v] = \int F_1(u,v,v')\,du$ after substitution. The question: if $y(x)$ satisfies the original Euler equation, does $v(u)$ automatically satisfy the Euler equation for the new functional $J_1$?

This matters practically: if the answer is yes, you're free to switch to whatever coordinate system makes the algebra easiest (e.g. polar coordinates for a problem with circular symmetry) before grinding through the Euler-equation derivation, rather than deriving Euler's equation in the original coordinates and then painfully transforming the resulting differential equation.

8.2 Why invariance holds

The cleanest argument uses the variational derivative from Section 7, together with its defining property: $\delta J/\delta y$ at a point is the limit of $\Delta J/\Delta\sigma$, where $\Delta\sigma$ is the area between the original curve and a perturbed one. Under the change of coordinates, this same little sliver of area transforms according to the standard area-transformation rule from multivariable calculus — the new area $\Delta\sigma_1$ relates to the old one via the Jacobian:
$$\frac{\Delta\sigma}{\Delta\sigma_1} \to \begin{vmatrix}x_u&x_v\\y_u&y_v\end{vmatrix} \ne 0 \quad \text{(a nonzero constant, in the limit).}$$
Since $\Delta J$ (the actual numerical change in the functional's value) doesn't care which coordinate system you used to describe the perturbation — it's the same number either way — we get
$$\lim_{\Delta\sigma\to0}\frac{\Delta J}{\Delta\sigma} = 0 \quad \iff \quad \lim_{\Delta\sigma_1\to0}\frac{\Delta J}{\Delta\sigma_1}=0,$$
because the two limits differ only by multiplication by the nonzero Jacobian factor above. In other words: the variational derivative vanishing is a coordinate-independent statement, precisely because the ratio of "area elements" in the two coordinate systems is a nonzero constant, not zero or infinite. Since Euler's equation is equivalent to "the variational derivative vanishes," being an extremal is a geometric property of the curve, not an artifact of how you chose to write down coordinates.

8.3 Worked example: switching to polar coordinates

Problem. Find the extremals of $J[r] = \int_{\varphi_0}^{\varphi_1}\sqrt{r^2+r'^2}\,d\varphi$, where $r=r(\varphi)$ (this is arc length in polar coordinates, $ds^2 = dr^2+r^2d\varphi^2$, so $ds/d\varphi = \sqrt{r^2+r'^2}$ — this functional literally computes the length of a curve given in polar form).

Grinding out Euler's equation directly in these variables would be a chore. Instead, invariance tells us we may switch to Cartesian coordinates $x=r\cos\varphi, y=r\sin\varphi$ before differentiating: since $\varphi$ plays the role of "$x$" and $r$ the role of "the curve," but the underlying geometric functional is literally arc length in the plane, in Cartesian coordinates it becomes just
$$\int_{x_0}^{x_1}\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx,$$
whose Euler equation is simply $y''=0$ (Case 1 from Section 4.3, with $F_{y'}=y'/\sqrt{1+y'^2}=C \implies y'=$ const), so the general solution is a straight line:
$$y = \alpha x + \beta.$$
Translating this straight line back into the original polar variables ($x=r\cos\varphi$, $y=r\sin\varphi$) gives the answer to the original problem:
$$r\sin\varphi = \alpha\, r\cos\varphi + \beta.$$
(Sanity check on why this is right without any calculation: the extremals of the arc-length functional, in any coordinate system, have to be straight lines, since arc length is minimized by straight lines in the plane, full stop. This example is really a demonstration that invariance correctly reproduces a fact we could already sanity-check by other means — which is exactly the kind of check you should always run when a new piece of theoretical machinery gives you an answer.)

Chapter recap

Let's take stock of the whole toolkit built in this chapter, since Chapters 2 and 3 use every piece of it constantly.

You now know precisely what a functional is, and you've met the three motivating classical problems (shortest path, brachistochrone, isoperimetric problem) that make the subject worth caring about. You've built the notion of a normed function space, and — crucially — you understand why the choice of norm matters: it determines both what "close" means and, consequently, whether a given functional is even continuous, which in turn is why "weak extremum" (measured in the $\mathscr{D}_1$ norm) is the natural target rather than "strong extremum." You've defined the variation $\delta J[h]$ of a functional as the exact analogue of the ordinary differential $df$, proved it's unique, and proved the master necessary condition: at an extremum, $\delta J[h]=0$ for every admissible perturbation $h$. You then turned that abstract vanishing condition into a real, solvable differential equation — Euler's equation $F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ — using the two workhorse lemmas (integrate by parts, then invoke the fundamental lemma), and you've collected four special cases (Cases 1–4) that let you shortcut straight to a first integral whenever $F$ is missing one of its arguments. You've seen the same machine reproduce a genuine partial differential equation (and a real geometric fact about minimal surfaces) when the domain is upgraded from a curve to a surface. You've learned the two-stage recipe — solve Euler's equation first, then use leftover freedom in the boundary term to pin down natural boundary conditions — for problems where an endpoint is allowed to move. And finally, you've seen that Euler's equation can be repackaged as "$\delta J/\delta y = 0$ pointwise," which both explains its name and its analogy with ordinary calculus, and explains why it doesn't matter which coordinate system you solve it in.

Chapter 2 takes this exact toolkit and stretches it in four independent directions (many unknown functions, parametric curves, higher derivatives, and constrained problems), and Chapter 3 goes back and generalizes Section 6's variable-endpoint idea to its fullest extent. Nothing new needs to be invented — it's the same two moves (Taylor-expand the increment; integrate by parts and invoke a fundamental lemma) applied over and over in new settings.

Practice problems

These are drawn from the book's own end-of-chapter problem set (renumbered here to match), chosen to reinforce the core skills of this chapter. Try each one before reading the hint.

Problem 1

1. (Euler's equation, direct application.) Find the extremals of:

  (a) $\int_0^1 y'\,dx$, with $y(0)=0,\ y(1)=1$.
  (b) $\int_0^1 yy'\,dx$, with $y(0)=0,\ y(1)=1$.
  (c) $\int_0^1 xyy'\,dx$, with $y(0)=0,\ y(1)=1$.

Hint: In each case, check whether the integrand is actually of the form $\frac{d}{dx}\Phi(x,y)$ for some $\Phi$ (recall Remark 1/2 after Theorem in Section 9's discussion of "equivalent functionals" — or just try to spot it directly here). If so, the value of the functional is the same for every admissible curve, so every curve is trivially an "extremal" (the problem is degenerate) — verify this is exactly what happens in each part.

Problem 2

2. (Cases 1–4 practice.) Find the extremals of $\int_a^b(y^2+y'^2-2y\sin x)\,dx$.

Hint: This does not fall into Cases 1–3 (it depends on all of $x,y,y'$), so write out the full Euler equation $F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ directly: $F_y = 2y-2\sin x$, $F_{y'}=2y'$, so the equation is $y''-y = -\sin x$, a linear ODE you can solve by undetermined coefficients (the book's answer: $y=C_1e^x+C_2e^{-x}+\tfrac12\sin x$ — verify this by direct substitution).

Problem 3

3. (First integral, Case 2.) Find the extremals of $\int_a^b \frac{y'^2}{x^3}\,dx$.

Hint: No explicit $y$ — Case 1: $F_{y'}=C$ directly gives a separable first-order equation for $y'$ in terms of $x$; integrate once more. (Book's stated answer for a closely related problem: $y=C_1x^4+C_2$ — make sure your own algebra lines up with a check by direct substitution into Euler's equation.)

Problem 4

4. (Quadrature, Case 4 family.) Find the general solution of Euler's equation for $J[y]=\int_a^b f(x)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$, and work out the special cases $f(x)=\sqrt x$ and $f(x) = x$.

Hint: This is Case 1 in disguise (no explicit $y$), so start from $F_{y'}=C$: $\dfrac{f(x)y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = C$. Solve for $y'$ in terms of $f(x)$ and $C$, then integrate — the answer will be an explicit quadrature $y = \int \frac{C\,dx}{\sqrt{f(x)^2-C^2}} + C_1$ that you can evaluate in closed form for the two specific choices of $f$.

Problem 5

5. (Variable endpoint, natural boundary condition.) Which curve minimizes $\int_0^1\left(\tfrac12y'^2+yy'+y'+y\right)dx$, if the values of $y$ at the endpoints are not prescribed?

Hint: Both endpoints are free, so you get two natural boundary conditions $F_{y'}|_{x=0}=0$ and $F_{y'}|_{x=1}=0$, in addition to Euler's equation. Compute $F_y = y'+1$ and $F_{y'}=y'+y+1$; Euler's equation becomes $y'+1 - \frac{d}{dx}(y'+y+1) = 0$, i.e. $y'+1-y''-y'=0$, i.e. $y''=1$ — integrate twice, then use both natural conditions to pin down the two constants. (Book's answer: $y=\tfrac12(x^2-3x+1)$.)

Problem 6

6. (Variational derivative, direct computation.) Compute the variational derivative $\delta J/\delta y$ at the point $x_0$ of the quadratic functional $J[y] = \int_a^b\int_a^b K(s,t)y(s)y(t)\,ds\,dt$.

Hint: This functional is not of the "simplest" form $\int F(x,y,y')dx$ — it's built from a double integral over $s,t$ with $y$ appearing twice. Perturb $y \to y+h$ where $h$ is a small bump concentrated near $x_0$, expand $J[y+h]$, keep only the terms linear in $h$, and match against the general definition of the variational derivative from Section 7.3. (This is a good test of whether you've understood the definition of the variational derivative, rather than just Euler's-equation shortcut for the one specific functional type Section 7 focused on.)

Problem 7

7. (Invariance / change of variables.) Find the extremals of $J[r]=\int\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$ by switching to polar coordinates.

Hint: Exactly parallel to the worked Example in Section 8.3 — recognize $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}=r$ and rewrite the whole functional in terms of $r,\varphi$ using $x=r\cos\varphi,y=r\sin\varphi, dx = ...$; alternatively, since this functional is $\int r\,ds$ in disguise, try setting it up directly in polar form and using Case-type simplifications. (Book's answer, for reference: $x^2\cos\alpha+2xy\sin\alpha-y^2\cos\alpha=\beta$, a family of conics — a good target to check your algebra against.)
