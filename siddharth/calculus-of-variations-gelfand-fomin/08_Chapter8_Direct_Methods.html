00Roadmap: solving problems without solving equations

Every technique in Chapters 1 through 7 followed the same two-step plan: derive the Euler equation (or Euler system, for multiple functions or multiple integrals), then solve that differential equation to find the extremal. This works beautifully when the Euler equation happens to be solvable — but partial differential equations, in particular, are frequently not solvable in closed form at all. This chapter introduces an entirely different strategy, one that never writes down the Euler equation:

  • Section 39 sets up the general idea of a minimizing sequence — a sequence of admissible functions whose values of $J$ approach the infimum — and asks exactly when the limit of such a sequence (if it has one) actually achieves that infimum.
  • Section 40 describes two concrete ways to build a minimizing sequence without ever touching the Euler equation: the Ritz method (approximate the infinite-dimensional problem by a sequence of finite-dimensional ones) and the method of finite differences (approximate the continuous problem by a discrete one).
  • Section 41 turns the whole idea around and uses it as a tool for solving a famous differential equation, the Sturm–Liouville equation, entirely through the calculus of variations — recovering, as a byproduct, one of the cleanest existence proofs available for its eigenvalues and eigenfunctions.

The name "direct methods" is a bit of physics-style irony: they are direct in the sense of attacking $J$ head-on, but they are anything but a shortcut — what they buy you is a way to approximate solutions (and even to prove existence of solutions) for problems that a differential-equations approach can't crack at all.

39Minimizing Sequences
Background primer

A short primer on infimum and why it might not be attained

For a set of real numbers, the infimum ($\inf$) is the greatest number that's still $\le$ every number in the set — the natural generalization of "minimum" to sets that might not actually contain their own smallest element. For instance, $\inf\{1/n : n=1,2,3,\dots\}=0$, even though $0$ itself is never one of the numbers $1/n$: you can get arbitrarily close to $0$, but you never land on it. The analogous notion for a functional $J$ ranging over a space of admissible functions $\mathscr M$ is $\mu=\inf_yJ[y]$ — the greatest lower bound of all the values $J[y]$ actually takes — and the central question of this section is exactly the functional-space version of the question above: does some admissible $\hat y$ actually achieve $J[\hat y]=\mu$, or does $J$ only get arbitrarily close to $\mu$ without ever reaching it?

The general strategy

Suppose you want to minimize a functional $J[y]$ over a space $\mathscr M$ of admissible functions, and suppose (as is needed for the problem to make sense at all) that $J$ is bounded below: $\mu=\inf_yJ[y]>-\infty$. By the very definition of an infimum, there must exist a sequence of admissible functions $\{y_n\}=y_1,y_2,\dots$ — called a minimizing sequence — with
$$\lim_{n\to\infty}J[y_n] = \mu.$$
(Such a sequence always exists whenever $\mu$ is finite: just pick $y_n$ so that $J[y_n]<\mu+\tfrac1n$, which is possible because no number bigger than $\mu$ can be a lower bound for all the values of $J$.) If this sequence happens to have a limit function $\hat y$ — meaning $y_n\to\hat y$ in whatever norm the space $\mathscr M$ carries — and if it's legitimate to pass the limit inside $J$, i.e., if
$$J[\hat y] = \lim_{n\to\infty}J[y_n], \tag{2}$$
then combining this with the definition of $\mu$ gives $J[\hat y]=\mu$ — meaning $\hat y$ actually solves the minimization problem. So the whole direct-methods program reduces to three separate tasks, worth stating explicitly since each one is a distinctly separate thing that can go wrong: (1) construct a minimizing sequence; (2) prove it has a limit function $\hat y$; (3) prove that passing the limit inside $J$, as in (2), is legitimate. Even when step (3) fails, or step (2)'s limit fails to exist, the individual functions $y_n$ of the minimizing sequence are still useful in their own right, as approximate solutions of the original problem — this is exactly the sense in which "direct methods" double as approximation methods, independent of whether a rigorous existence proof goes all the way through.

Why step (2) can fail. It's worth seeing a minimizing sequence with no limit at all, so the difficulty feels concrete rather than hypothetical. Take $J[y]=\int_{-1}^1x^2y'^2\,dx$ with $y(-1)=-1,y(1)=1$. Clearly $J[y]\ge0$ always, and $\inf_yJ[y]=0$ (though no admissible $y$ achieves $J[y]=0$ exactly, since that would force $y'\equiv0$ away from $x=0$, which is incompatible with the boundary conditions). The sequence $y_n(x)=\tan^{-1}(nx)/\tan^{-1}n$ satisfies the boundary conditions for every $n$, and a direct computation of $J[y_n]$ (using the substitution and bound worked out below) shows $J[y_n]\to0$ as $n\to\infty$ — so $\{y_n\}$ is a minimizing sequence. But $y_n(x)\to\text{sgn}(x)$ pointwise (the sign function, which jumps from $-1$ to $+1$ right at $x=0$) — a discontinuous function, not a legitimate member of the space of continuous admissible functions at all. The minimizing sequence, in other words, is trying to converge to something outside the space you started in — a failure mode worth watching for, since it shows up constantly once you start hunting for minimizing sequences in practice.

When step (3) is safe: lower semicontinuity

Even granting that a minimizing sequence converges to some legitimate $\hat y$ (in the $\mathscr C$-norm, say — plain uniform convergence, with no control at all over how the derivatives $y_n'$ behave), passing the limit inside $J$ is still not automatic, because most functionals studied in this book (anything built from an integral of $y'$) are simply not continuous in the $\mathscr C$-norm: $y_n$ can converge uniformly to $\hat y$ while $y_n'$ fails to converge to $\hat y'$ at all, so there's no reason to expect $J[y_n]\to J[\hat y]$. What comes to the rescue is a weaker property than continuity:

Definition

$J[y]$ is lower semicontinuous at $\hat y$ if, for every sequence $y_n\to\hat y$ (in the norm of $\mathscr M$),
$$J[\hat y] \le \varliminf_{n\to\infty}J[y_n]$$
(where $\varliminf$, the limit inferior, is the greatest number that $J[y_n]$ stays eventually above — the right notion of "limit" to use for a sequence that might oscillate rather than converge outright).

Lower semicontinuity says the functional can only ever undershoot in the limit, never overshoot — exactly the one-directional inequality actually needed to make the minimizing-sequence argument work, and no more.

Theorem

If $\{y_n\}$ is a minimizing sequence for $J[y]$, with limit function $\hat y$, and $J[y]$ is lower semicontinuous at $\hat y$, then $J[\hat y]=\lim_{n\to\infty}J[y_n]$.

Proof. On one hand, lower semicontinuity gives directly
$$J[\hat y] \le \varliminf_{n\to\infty}J[y_n] = \lim_{n\to\infty}J[y_n] = \inf_yJ[y] = \mu \tag{5}$$
(the middle equality holds because $\{y_n\}$ is a minimizing sequence, so $J[y_n]$ actually converges, and its limit equals its $\varliminf$). On the other hand, since $\hat y$ is itself an admissible function, $J[\hat y]\ge\mu$ by the very definition of $\mu$ as the greatest lower bound. Given any $\varepsilon>0$, for $n$ large enough $J[y_n]<\mu+\varepsilon\le J[\hat y]+\varepsilon$, i.e., $J[y_n]-J[\hat y]>-\varepsilon$; letting $n\to\infty$ gives $\mu-J[\hat y]\ge-\varepsilon$, and since $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $J[\hat y]\le\mu$. Combined with $J[\hat y]\ge\mu$ from before, $J[\hat y]=\mu=\lim_nJ[y_n]$. $\blacksquare$

This is the entire logical backbone of the chapter: lower semicontinuity is a strictly weaker, and hence much easier to verify, requirement than continuity — and it's exactly what's needed to make step (3) go through.

40The Ritz Method and the Method of Finite Differences

Section 39 explained what a minimizing sequence needs to do; this section gives two concrete recipes for actually building one.

40.1 The Ritz method

Suppose $J[y]$ is defined on a normed linear space $\mathscr M$ of admissible functions. Pick an infinite sequence of functions $\varphi_1,\varphi_2,\dots$ in $\mathscr M$ (think of them as a proposed "basis," in the same spirit as a basis of a finite-dimensional vector space from linear algebra, except here the space is infinite-dimensional and the "basis" is really just a spanning sequence), and let $\mathscr M_n$ be the $n$-dimensional subspace of all linear combinations
$$\alpha_1\varphi_1+\cdots+\alpha_n\varphi_n. \tag{9}$$
Restricting $J$ to $\mathscr M_n$ turns it into an ordinary function of $n$ real variables, $J_n(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)=J[\alpha_1\varphi_1+\cdots+\alpha_n\varphi_n]$ — a plain calculus problem (minimize a function of finitely many variables), solvable in principle by ordinary multivariable-calculus techniques (setting partial derivatives to zero, exactly the finite-dimensional case that the whole calculus of variations has been generalizing from since Chapter 1). Let $\mu_n$ be this minimum and $y_n\in\mathscr M_n$ the minimizer. Since every linear combination of $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_n$ is automatically also a linear combination of $\varphi_1,\dots,\varphi_n,\varphi_{n+1}$, the subspaces are nested, $\mathscr M_1\subset\mathscr M_2\subset\cdots$, and minimizing over a bigger space can only ever do at least as well:
$$\mu_1\ge\mu_2\ge\cdots.$$

For $\{y_n\}$ to be a genuine minimizing sequence for the original problem (not just for each finite-dimensional slice), the spanning sequence $\{\varphi_n\}$ needs one more property:

Definition

The sequence $\{\varphi_n\}$ is complete in $\mathscr M$ if, for every $y\in\mathscr M$ and every $\varepsilon>0$, some linear combination $\eta_n$ of the form (9) satisfies $\|\eta_n-y\|<\varepsilon$.

Completeness says exactly: every admissible function can be approximated arbitrarily well by some finite linear combination of the $\varphi_i$'s — the direct functional-space analogue of a spanning set in linear algebra, except now "spanning" means "dense," since an infinite-dimensional space generally can't be spanned by finitely many, or even by a merely countable list of, vectors in the exact algebraic sense.

Theorem

If $J[y]$ is continuous (in the norm of $\mathscr M$) and $\{\varphi_n\}$ is complete, then $\mu_n\to\mu=\inf_yJ[y]$.

Proof. Given $\varepsilon>0$, pick $y^*$ with $J[y^*]<\mu+\varepsilon$ (possible since $\mu$ is the infimum). By continuity of $J$, there's a $\delta>0$ such that $\|y-y^*\|<\delta$ forces $|J[y]-J[y^*]|<\varepsilon$. By completeness, some $\eta_n$ of the form (9) satisfies $\|\eta_n-y^*\|<\delta$, hence $J[\eta_n]<J[y^*]+\varepsilon<\mu+2\varepsilon$. Since $y_n$ is the actual minimizer over the same subspace, $J[y_n]\le J[\eta_n]<\mu+2\varepsilon$. On the other hand, $\mu\le\mu_n=J[y_n]$, so $\mu\le\mu_n<\mu+2\varepsilon$. As $\varepsilon$ was arbitrary, $J[y_n]\to\mu$. $\blacksquare$

The geometric content of this proof is worth restating in words, since it's the entire intuition behind the method: because $\{\varphi_n\}$ is complete, the finite-dimensional subspaces $\mathscr M_n$ eventually approximate any point of the infinite-dimensional space $\mathscr M$ arbitrarily well — informally, $\lim_n\mathscr M_n=\mathscr M$ — so minimizing over $\mathscr M_n$ for large $n$ is nearly as good as minimizing over $\mathscr M$ itself. In practice, remarkably few terms of (9) are often needed for a strikingly good approximation — this is what makes the Ritz method a serious computational tool, not merely a theoretical existence device.

40.2 The method of finite differences

The second recipe discretizes the domain rather than approximating in a function space. Recall from Chapter 1, Section 7 that the derivation of Euler's equation itself proceeded by first approximating $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$ by a function of finitely many variables: divide $[a,b]$ into $n+1$ equal subintervals via points $x_0=a,x_1,\dots,x_n,x_{n+1}=b$, replace $y$ by the polygonal line through $(x_0,y_0),\dots,(x_{n+1},y_{n+1})$, and approximate $J$ by the sum
$$J(y_1,\dots,y_n) = \sum_{i=0}^n F\left[x_i,y_i,\frac{y_{i+1}-y_i}{\Delta x}\right]\Delta x, \tag{13}$$
a function of the $n$ free heights $y_1,\dots,y_n$ (with $y_0=A,y_{n+1}=B$ fixed by the boundary conditions). Minimizing this ordinary function of $n$ variables, for each $n$, and taking the resulting polygonal lines as the sequence $\{y_n\}$, gives another concrete route to a minimizing sequence — one that discretizes the interval itself rather than expanding in a fixed family of basis functions. The two methods are complementary tools for the same job: Ritz approximates in "function space," finite differences approximates in "physical space," and both ultimately reduce an infinite-dimensional minimization to a sequence of finite-dimensional ones, exactly the strategy Section 39 laid out in the abstract.

41The Sturm-Liouville Problem

This section runs the whole program in reverse: instead of using a differential equation to solve a variational problem (Chapters 1–7's approach throughout), it uses a variational problem, attacked by direct methods, to prove the existence of solutions to a differential equation — and to compute them, to any desired accuracy, along the way.

41.1 Setting up the variational problem

Let $P=P(x)>0$ and $Q=Q(x)$ be given functions on $[a,b]$, with $P$ continuously differentiable. The Sturm–Liouville problem asks for the nontrivial solutions (eigenfunctions) and corresponding values of the parameter $\lambda$ (eigenvalues) of the boundary value problem
$$-(Py')'+Qy=\lambda y, \qquad y(a)=y(b)=0. \tag{14, 15}$$
(Equation (14) is the Sturm–Liouville equation; note that $y\equiv0$ trivially satisfies (14)–(15) for every $\lambda$, so "eigenfunction" always means a nontrivial solution.) This single equation governs an enormous range of physical problems (vibrating strings and membranes with variable density or tension, quantum-mechanical bound states, heat conduction in a rod with variable material properties), which is part of why an existence proof for its solutions is worth having in complete generality, not just for special choices of $P,Q$.

The connection to the calculus of variations: equation (14) is exactly the Euler equation (from the standard $F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ recipe of Chapter 1, applied here with an extra Lagrange-multiplier term, exactly Chapter 2, Section 10's machinery for a subsidiary integral constraint) of the problem of extremizing the quadratic functional
$$J[y]=\int_a^b(Py'^2+Qy^2)\,dx \tag{16}$$
subject to the boundary conditions (15) and the normalization condition
$$\int_a^by^2\,dx=1. \tag{17}$$
So: if $y(x)$ extremizes (16) subject to (15) and (17), then $y$ automatically solves (14)–(15) as well, for $\lambda$ equal to the Lagrange multiplier attached to constraint (17) — and $y\not\equiv0$ is guaranteed by (17) itself (an identically-zero function can't have $\int y^2=1$). This is worth pausing on: it converts "find an eigenfunction" into "find an extremal of a quadratic functional subject to a normalization constraint," a problem direct methods are perfectly suited to attack, since (16) is bounded below whenever $Q$ is (a short computation, using $P>0$ and $\int_a^by^2\,dx=1$ from (17), shows $J[y]\ge M\int_a^by^2\,dx=M$ where $M=\min_{[a,b]}Q(x)$ — so $\mu=\inf J[y]>-\infty$, exactly the hypothesis Section 39 needed from the very start).

Background primer

A primer: orthogonal functions and Fourier sine series (what you need, briefly)

Two vectors in ordinary linear algebra are orthogonal if their dot product is zero; the natural analogue for functions on $[a,b]$ replaces the dot product $\sum_iv_iw_i$ by an integral, $\langle f,g\rangle=\int_a^bf(x)g(x)\,dx$, and calls $f,g$ orthogonal when $\langle f,g\rangle=0$. The functions $\sin x,\sin2x,\sin3x,\dots$ are mutually orthogonal on $[0,\pi]$ in exactly this sense: $\int_0^\pi\sin kx\sin lx\,dx=0$ whenever $k\ne l$ (a standard product-to-sum computation) — the direct function-space echo of an orthogonal basis of vectors from linear algebra, and it's why they make such a convenient choice of the spanning sequence $\{\varphi_n\}$ for the Ritz method: expanding a function in this basis, $y(x)\approx\sum_k\alpha_k\sin kx$, is exactly a Fourier sine series, and orthogonality is what makes the coefficients $\alpha_k$ easy to compute and the resulting quadratic form easy to diagonalize (no cross terms between different $\varphi_k,\varphi_l$ survive integration). This sequence is also complete on $[0,\pi]$ for functions vanishing at both endpoints — a fact from Fourier analysis taken as given here — which is exactly the property Section 40.1 needed for the Ritz method to actually converge.

Background primer

A primer: uniform convergence, equicontinuity, and the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem

A sequence of functions $\{f_n\}$ converges to $f$ uniformly on $[a,b]$ if the worst-case gap $\max_x|f_n(x)-f(x)|$ shrinks to zero — a strictly stronger requirement than merely $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ at each individual $x$ separately, and exactly the notion of convergence carried by the $\mathscr{C}$-norm from Chapter 1. A family of functions is uniformly bounded if a single constant $M$ bounds every function in the family everywhere, and equicontinuous if a single $\delta$ works, for every function in the family simultaneously, to guarantee $|\psi(x_2)-\psi(x_1)|<\varepsilon$ whenever $|x_2-x_1|<\delta$ — informally, no function in the family is allowed to have an arbitrarily steep wiggle, and the same "steepness budget" has to work for the whole family at once, not just for each function individually.

Arzelà's theorem (also called the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem) says: every uniformly bounded, equicontinuous sequence of functions on $[a,b]$ has a uniformly convergent subsequence. This is the real-analysis engine behind everything in the rest of this section — it's the tool that turns "these functions don't blow up and don't wiggle too fast" into "some subsequence of them actually converges," which is exactly what's needed to extract a genuine limit function out of a Ritz-method sequence $\{y_n\}$ before you know anything else about it.

41.2 Proving the first eigenvalue exists

Take $a=0,b=\pi$ for concreteness, and choose $\{\sin nx\}$ as the Ritz method's spanning sequence — complete (by the primer above) and orthogonal, so a linear combination $\sum_{k=1}^n\alpha_k\sin kx$ satisfies the normalization condition (17) exactly when $\frac\pi2\sum_k\alpha_k^2=1$, i.e., $(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ lies on a sphere $\sigma_n$ of a specific radius in $n$-dimensional space (using the orthogonality of the $\sin kx$'s to collapse the integral of the square into a plain sum of squares — exactly the Pythagorean-theorem-style computation orthogonality is built to make possible). On this compact set $\sigma_n$, the continuous function $J_n(\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ (the quadratic form (16) restricted to this finite-dimensional family) attains an actual minimum $\lambda_n^{(1)}$ at some point of the sphere — compactness (a closed, bounded set in a finite-dimensional space) is exactly what guarantees a continuous function attains its minimum there, the finite-dimensional fact from real analysis that makes this whole first step legitimate. As in Section 40.1's general argument, restricting to a smaller subspace can only hurt, so $\lambda_{n+1}^{(1)}\le\lambda_n^{(1)}$, and since $J[y]$ is bounded below, this decreasing sequence of numbers converges: $\lambda^{(1)}=\lim_n\lambda_n^{(1)}$ exists.

The harder work is showing the corresponding minimizing functions $y_n^{(1)}(x)=\sum_k\alpha_k^{(1)}\sin kx$ converge too, and to a genuine solution of the Sturm–Liouville equation. This happens in three stages, each resolving a specific gap in the argument:

Stage 1 (a convergent subsequence exists). Since $\{\lambda_n^{(1)}\}$ converges, it's bounded, which (unwinding (16) and using $P>0$) bounds $\int_0^\pi y_n'^2\,dx$ uniformly in $n$. Combined with $y_n(0)=0$ and the Cauchy–Schwarz inequality, this bounds $y_n(x)$ itself uniformly (uniform boundedness) and controls $|y_n(x_2)-y_n(x_1)|$ by $|x_2-x_1|$ uniformly in $n$ (equicontinuity) — exactly the two hypotheses of Arzelà's theorem, which then hands over a uniformly convergent subsequence $y_{n_m}(x)\to y^{(1)}(x)$.

Stage 2 (the limit function solves the equation — a delicate integration-by-parts argument). Knowing $y_{n_m}\to y^{(1)}$ uniformly is not yet enough to say the derivatives converge, so the limit can't simply be substituted into the Euler equation directly the way you might hope. The book's route around this: reformulate "$y$ solves the Sturm–Liouville equation" as an integral identity ($y$ solving (14) is equivalent to $\int_0^\pi[-(Ph')'+Q_1h]y\,dx=0$ for every sufficiently smooth test function $h$ vanishing at the endpoints along with its derivative — a weak formulation, verified by integrating by parts twice and applying the fundamental lemma once more), and check that this integral identity survives passing to the limit under only uniform convergence, using the finite-dimensional Euler-equation analogue satisfied by each $y_{n_m}$ along with a Fourier-coefficient convergence argument (Problems 8–9 below fill in the remaining estimates). The payoff is that $y^{(1)}$ does in fact satisfy the Sturm–Liouville equation (14) with $\lambda=\lambda^{(1)}$, even though it was only ever known to be a uniform limit.

Stage 3 (the whole sequence converges, not just a subsequence). A final uniqueness argument — using the fact that a solution of (37) with given boundary and normalization conditions is unique up to an overall sign — upgrades "some subsequence converges to a solution" to "the whole sequence $y_n^{(1)}$ converges to the solution $y^{(1)}$," provided the sign of each $y_n^{(1)}$ is chosen consistently.

The result of all three stages: $\lambda^{(1)}$ is a genuine eigenvalue of the Sturm–Liouville problem, with genuine eigenfunction $y^{(1)}$ — the first, in the sense of being the smallest, since $\lambda^{(1)}=\inf_yJ[y]$ subject only to (15) and (17).

41.3 Finding the rest of the eigenvalues

Having found the first eigenpair $(\lambda^{(1)},y^{(1)})$, the next one is found by minimizing the same functional (16) subject to (15) and (17) as before, plus one more constraint: orthogonality to $y^{(1)}$,
$$\int_a^by^{(1)}(x)y(x)\,dx=0. \tag{41}$$
Geometrically, in the Ritz picture, this cuts the sphere $\sigma_n$ down by one dimension — intersecting it with a hyperplane through the origin, giving an $(n-1)$-dimensional sphere — and the entire argument of Section 41.2 repeats essentially unchanged on this smaller set, producing a second eigenvalue $\lambda^{(2)}\ge\lambda^{(1)}$ (in fact strictly greater, since eigenfunctions for different eigenvalues are automatically orthogonal, and only one eigenfunction — up to a constant multiple — exists per eigenvalue, so $\lambda^{(1)}=\lambda^{(2)}$ would force $y^{(1)}$ and $y^{(2)}$ to be the same function up to scale, contradicting the orthogonality condition (41) that was just imposed between them) and eigenfunction $y^{(2)}$. Repeating this — minimize subject to normalization and orthogonality to every eigenfunction found so far — produces the entire sequence $\lambda^{(1)}<\lambda^{(2)}<\lambda^{(3)}<\cdots$ and their eigenfunctions, one step of mathematical induction at a time, each step a verbatim repeat of Section 41.2's three-stage argument on a successively smaller sphere.

This is the theorem promised at the top of the section: the Sturm–Liouville problem has an infinite sequence of eigenvalues, each with an eigenfunction unique up to a constant multiple — and the proof, remarkably, never once had to write down or solve the differential equation (14) directly. It was extracted entirely from the existence of minimizing sequences for a quadratic functional, exactly the machinery Sections 39–40 built for an entirely different-looking purpose.

Chapter recap

This chapter stepped back from finding extremals via the Euler equation and asked instead when a minimizing sequence — any sequence of admissible functions whose $J$-values approach the infimum — actually converges to a genuine solution. Section 39 isolated the two places this program can fail (the sequence might have no limit at all, or the limit might not achieve the infimum) and identified lower semicontinuity as the exact property needed to rule out the second failure. Section 40 gave two concrete recipes for building minimizing sequences without ever touching a differential equation: the Ritz method, which approximates the infinite-dimensional problem by minimizing over successively larger finite-dimensional subspaces spanned by a complete sequence of functions, and the method of finite differences, which discretizes the domain itself. Section 41 spent this machinery on the Sturm–Liouville problem, recasting "find an eigenfunction" as "extremize a quadratic functional subject to a normalization constraint," and using the Ritz method (built on the orthogonal, complete sequence $\{\sin nx\}$) together with the Arzelà–Ascoli theorem and a weak-formulation argument to prove — constructively, with an explicit approximation scheme built in for free — that an infinite sequence of eigenvalues and eigenfunctions must exist. The throughline across this whole book, now complete: Chapters 1 through 6 built the classical machinery for one independent variable, Chapter 7 extended it to several, and this chapter shows that even when none of that machinery produces a solvable equation, the calculus of variations still has something to offer — a direct, constructive path to existence and approximation, built entirely out of minimizing sequences.

Problem 5

5. (The two-dimensional Ritz method, solving a PDE.) Use the Ritz method to find an approximate solution of Poisson's equation $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=-1$ inside the square $R:-a\le x\le a,\ -a\le y\le a$, with $u=0$ on the boundary of $R$.

Hint: This is exactly Problem 1 of Chapter 7 (Poisson's equation) turned into a Ritz computation: minimize $J[u]=\iint_R[(u_x)^2+(u_y)^2-2u]\,dx\,dy$ (its Euler equation, by the multivariable machinery of Chapter 7, Section 35, is exactly $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=-1$), using the two-dimensional spanning sequence $(x^2-a^2)(y^2-b^2),\ (x^2+y^2)(x^2-a^2)(y^2-b^2),\dots$ — each term already built to vanish on the boundary of the square, again baking the boundary condition directly into the choice of $\varphi_n$.

Problem 6

6. (Sturm–Liouville by hand, for the simplest possible $P,Q$.) Write the Sturm–Liouville equation for $J[y]=\int_a^b(c_1y'^2+cy^2)\,dx$ ($c,c_1>0$ constants), $y(a)=y(b)=0$, and find its eigenvalues and eigenfunctions explicitly.

Hint: Here (14) becomes the constant-coefficient equation $-c_1y''+cy=\lambda y$, i.e. $y''=\frac{c-\lambda}{c_1}y$ — solvable directly (Chapter 1 methods), with nontrivial solutions vanishing at both $a$ and $b$ existing only for specific values of $\lambda$ forcing $\sqrt{(\lambda-c)/c_1}=n\pi/(b-a)$ for a positive integer $n$ — an explicit, closed-form instance of the abstract existence theorem Section 41 proved in general.

Problem 7

7. (Changing the boundary conditions, changing the variational problem.) Formulate a variational problem leading to the Sturm–Liouville equation (14), but with the boundary conditions $y'(a)=y'(b)=0$ instead of $y(a)=y(b)=0$.

Hint: Recall the natural (free) boundary conditions from Chapter 1, Section 6 (also revisited in Chapter 7, Section 36.1's free-end discussion): if no boundary condition is imposed by hand on $y$ in the functional $J[y]=\int_a^b(Py'^2+Qy^2)\,dx$, the vanishing of the boundary terms in $\delta J$ forces $Py'=0$ at each end, i.e. $y'(a)=y'(b)=0$ automatically, since $P>0$. So simply drop conditions (15) from the variational problem's hypotheses and let it emerge as a natural boundary condition instead.

Problem 8

8. (An approximation lemma needed in Stage 2 of Section 41.2.) Prove that any function $h(x)\in\mathscr D_2(0,\pi)$ satisfying $h(0)=h(\pi)=0$, $h'(0)=h'(\pi)=0$ can be approximated in the mean by a linear combination $h_n(x)=\sum_{r=1}^nC_r^{(n)}\sin rx$ such that, simultaneously, $h_n'(x)$ approximates $h'(x)$ and $h_n''(x)$ approximates $h''(x)$ in the mean too — and that the coefficients $C_r^{(n)}$ can be taken independent of $n$.

Hint: Form the Fourier sine series of $h''(x)$ itself, then integrate it term by term twice, using the boundary conditions on $h,h'$ to fix the constants of integration at each stage — this produces a single sequence of coefficients $C_r$ that simultaneously work for approximating $h,h',h''$, which is exactly the extra mileage needed beyond an ordinary Fourier convergence theorem.

Problem 9

9. (The convergence lemma needed in Stage 2 of Section 41.2.) Show that if $f_n(x)\to f(x)$ in the mean and $g_n(x)\to g(x)$ uniformly on $[a,b]$, then $\int_a^bf_n(x)g_n(x)\,dx\to\int_a^bf(x)g(x)\,dx$.

Hint: Split the difference $\int f_ng_n-\int fg$ into $\int f_n(g_n-g)+\int(f_n-f)g$, bound the first piece using $\|g_n-g\|_\infty\to0$ together with a uniform bound on $\int|f_n|$ (itself derivable from $f_n\to f$ in the mean), and bound the second piece using Schwarz's inequality together with $\|g\|_\infty$ bounded and $\int(f_n-f)^2\to0$.
