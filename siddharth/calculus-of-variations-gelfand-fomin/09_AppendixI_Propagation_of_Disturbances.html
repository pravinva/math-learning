00Roadmap: rebuilding the theory from a wave, instead of from a curve

Every other chapter in this book starts from a functional $J[y]$ and works forward toward Euler's equation, canonical variables, and the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. This appendix runs the entire story in reverse and from a completely different starting point: a physical picture of a disturbance spreading through a medium, spreading fastest in some directions and slowest in others, the way a ripple on the surface of a lake spreads through water, or (the appendix's own guiding image) the way light spreads through a medium whose optical density varies from place to place and direction to direction. Starting purely from geometric reasoning about how such a disturbance propagates — with no functional, no variation, no Euler equation in sight at the outset — the appendix rebuilds, one step at a time, three of the book's central objects: the Hamiltonian function $H$, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, and the canonical (Hamiltonian) equations of motion.

  • Section 1 sets up the geometric picture: a medium in which the time it takes a disturbance to travel depends on both position and direction, and observes that disturbances always take the fastest available path — a direct instance of Fermat's principle.
  • Section 2 turns the "time to travel in a given direction" function into a genuine norm, using exactly the norm axioms from Chapter 1's normed-space primer.
  • Section 3 introduces the conjugate (dual) space of directions and shows that the natural function $H(x,p)$ living there is nothing but the Legendre transformation of Chapter 4, Section 18, appearing again in a new guise.
  • Section 4 describes how the wave front — the boundary of the region reached by the disturbance so far — evolves in time, via a geometric construction called an envelope.
  • Section 5 turns that geometric construction into a differential equation for the wave front — and the equation that comes out is, verbatim, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation from Chapter 4, Section 23.
  • Section 6 goes one step further and derives the canonical equations governing the trajectories along which the disturbance actually propagates.

By the end, you'll have seen the same three objects — $H$, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, and the canonical equations — derived twice, by two utterly different routes: once from a functional and its variation (Chapters 4 and 6), and once from nothing but a wave spreading through space. That two such different starting points land on exactly the same equations is itself worth sitting with — it's a strong hint that these objects are not artifacts of the calculus-of-variations method, but something more fundamental about how "shortest/fastest path" problems work in general.

01Statement of the Problem

Picture a medium filling an $n$-dimensional space $\mathscr X$, with points $x=(x^1,\dots,x^n)$ (using superscripts as coordinate labels, matching Chapter 7's index-notation convention — a stylistic choice that will pay off once the Legendre-transform bookkeeping shows up in Section 3). At each point $x_0$, consider all smooth curves $x=x(s)$ passing through $x_0$; the set of tangent vectors $x'=dx/ds$ to these curves at $x_0$ forms an $n$-dimensional space called the tangent space at $x_0$, denoted $\mathscr T(x_0)$. In the simple Euclidean setting used throughout this appendix, $\mathscr T(x_0)$ is really just another copy of ordinary $n$-dimensional space, sitting "at" the point $x_0$ — the distinction is bookkeeping, useful because it keeps straight which vectors describe directions available at $x_0$ as opposed to positions in $\mathscr X$ itself, and it's exactly analogous to the everyday habit of drawing a velocity vector as an arrow attached to a specific point on a trajectory, rather than as a free-floating arrow with no particular location.

The medium is inhomogeneous (propagation speed depends on position) and anisotropic (propagation speed depends on direction too), so the reciprocal of the propagation velocity is a genuine function of both $x$ and the direction $x'$, written $f(x,x')$. If a disturbance travels along a curve $x=x(s)$, the time to go from $x(s)$ to a neighboring point $x(s+ds)$ is $dt=f(x,dx/ds)\,ds$, and the total time to traverse the curve from $x_0=x(s_0)$ to $x_1=x(s_1)$ is
$$\int_{s_0}^{s_1}f\left(x,\frac{dx}{ds}\right)ds. \tag{2}$$

The "on-or-off" assumption, and Fermat's principle. The appendix makes two simplifying physical assumptions: each point of the medium is either "excited" or "at rest" (no notion of the intensity of a disturbance, only whether one has arrived yet), and once a disturbance reaches a point, that point immediately becomes a source of further disturbances propagating outward from it. Under these assumptions, if $x_0$ is excited at time $0$, a disturbance reaches $x_1$ via whichever path gets there fastest — any disturbance arriving via a slower path simply arrives at a point already excited, and has no further effect. So the actual time of first arrival is
$$\tau = \min\int_{s_0}^{s_1}f\left(x,\frac{dx}{ds}\right)ds,$$
the minimum taken over all curves joining $x_0,x_1$ — an instance of Fermat's principle (already met, for the special case of light in an optical medium, back on p. 34 of the book, in Chapter 1): among all paths joining two points, a disturbance always propagates along the one taking the least time. The actual minimizing paths are called the trajectories of the disturbance — and notice this is already, quietly, a full-fledged parametric variational problem of exactly the kind Chapter 2, Section 10 studied, with (2) playing the role of the functional $J$.

A physically reasonable list of properties for $f$. Four properties of $f(x,x')$ are assumed from the physics, each with a one-line justification: (1) $f(x,x')>0$ for $x'\ne0$ (travel takes positive time); (2) $f$ is positive-homogeneous of degree 1 in $x'$, i.e. $f(x,\lambda x')=\lambda f(x,x')$ for $\lambda>0$ (traveling twice as far along the same direction, at the same point, takes twice as long — the same homogeneity property Chapter 2, Section 10 required of every parametric-form integrand, since (2) must not depend on how a curve happens to be parameterized); (3) $f(x,-x')=f(x,x')$ (traversing a path takes the same time in either direction); and (4), for a homogeneous medium where $f$ depends only on direction, a convexity condition $f(x'+\bar x')\le f(x')+f(\bar x')$ — a disturbance can never reach a destination faster by detouring through an intermediate point than by going straight there, since a straight path is by definition the most direct route. The appendix's fifth and final assumption strengthens this to a strict convexity condition — equality in (4) only when $\bar x'$ is a positive multiple of $x'$ — a technical strengthening whose payoff shows up concretely in Section 4's construction.

02Introduction of a Norm in $\mathscr T(x)$

Properties (1)–(4) above are, item for item, exactly the axioms of a norm from Chapter 1's normed-space primer (positivity, homogeneity, and the triangle inequality — property (3), symmetry under reversal, together with homogeneity, is what lets the ordinary triangle inequality $\|x'+\bar x'\|\le\|x'\|+\|\bar x'\|$ be stated using $+$ rather than needing a separate reversed version). So it's natural, and turns out to be extremely productive, to simply define a norm on the tangent space $\mathscr T(x)$ directly from the physical function $f$:
$$\|x'\| = f(x,x') \tag{8}$$
for $x'\ne0$ (and $\|0\|=0$). The set of vectors with $f(x,x')=\|x'\|=\alpha$ is called a sphere of radius $\alpha$ in $\mathscr T(x)$ — precisely the boundary of the region a disturbance originating at $x$ has reached after elapsed time $\alpha$, by the very definition of $f$ as reciprocal propagation speed. This single move — recognizing that the physically given travel-time function already satisfies the norm axioms, so it can be treated as defining a genuine geometry on the space of directions at each point — is the technical crux the rest of the appendix builds on: the whole problem can now be restated purely geometrically, as: given a norm $\|\cdot\|$ on the tangent space at each point of $\mathscr X$ (satisfying strict convexity), describe how a disturbance, spreading out to fill the sphere $f(x,dx)=dt$ during each infinitesimal time interval $dt$, propagates.

03The Conjugate Space $\tilde{\mathscr T}(x)$
Background primer

A primer: dual (conjugate) spaces and linear functionals (what you need, briefly)

Chapter 1, Section 3.1 already introduced linear functionals — functions $\varphi[h]$ satisfying $\varphi[h_1+h_2]=\varphi[h_1]+\varphi[h_2]$ and $\varphi[\lambda h]=\lambda\varphi[h]$ — as the objects that make a functional's variation well-defined. Here's the one new fact needed: on an $n$-dimensional space like $\mathscr T(x)$, every linear functional $\varphi[x']$ has a strikingly concrete form. Picking any basis, write $x'=(x^{1\prime},\dots,x^{n\prime})$; linearity forces
$$\varphi[x'] = p_1x^{1\prime}+\cdots+p_nx^{n\prime} = (p,x')$$
for the specific numbers $p_i=\varphi[e_i]$ (the functional's value on each basis vector $e_i$), where $(p,x')$ denotes the ordinary dot product. In other words, every linear functional on $\mathscr T(x)$ is secretly just "take the dot product with some fixed vector $p$" — exactly the intuition you already have for linear functionals on ordinary Euclidean space from linear algebra, now stated as a theorem rather than assumed. The set of all such vectors $p$ (equivalently, the set of all linear functionals on $\mathscr T(x)$) is itself an $n$-dimensional linear space, called the conjugate space (or dual space) of $\mathscr T(x)$, denoted $\tilde{\mathscr T}(x)$. A vector $p\in\tilde{\mathscr T}(x)$ is naturally written with subscript indices $p_i$ (a covariant vector) rather than the superscript indices $x^{i\prime}$ of an ordinary (contravariant) vector, purely so that a repeated-once-up-once-down index pair like $p_ix^{i\prime}$ can carry the summation convention from Chapter 7's primer automatically — a notational nicety, not a conceptual complication, though it is a distinctly different kind of object from an ordinary vector, in exactly the sense that "a function that eats a vector and outputs a number" is a different kind of thing than "a vector" itself, even once both happen to be describable by a list of $n$ numbers.

The norm on the conjugate space, and $H(x,p)$

Just as $\mathscr T(x)$ carries the norm (8), the conjugate space $\tilde{\mathscr T}(x)$ gets its own natural norm, defined by
$$\|p\| = \sup_{x'}\frac{(p,x')}{\|x'\|}, \tag{10}$$
the supremum taken over all nonzero $x'\in\mathscr T(x)$ — this is exactly the general recipe (from linear algebra and functional analysis alike) for building a norm on a dual space out of a norm on the original space: measure a linear functional by the biggest ratio of "output" to "input size" it can produce. In the present context, this quantity is renamed and written as a function of $x$ and $p$ together:
$$H(x,p) = \sup_{x'}\frac{(p,x')}{\|x'\|}. \tag{11}$$

This is the Legendre transformation in disguise. Compare (11) directly to Chapter 4, Section 18's construction: there, given a strictly convex function $f(x')$ (a function of the "velocity" $x'$ alone), the Legendre transform built a new function $H(p)=\sup_{x'}\bigl[(p,x')-f(x')\bigr]$ of the "momentum" $p=f_{x'}$. Formula (11) is exactly this construction, run on the positively-homogeneous function $f(x,x')=\|x'\|$ instead of a general convex function — and for a positively-homogeneous function specifically, the Legendre transform's usual $\sup[(p,x')-f(x')]$ collapses to exactly the ratio form in (11) (a short computation: homogeneity means $(p,x')-\lambda f(x')$ can be made to blow up in either sign unless $(p,x')\le f(x')\cdot\|x'\|/\|x'\|$ holds with equality achievable, which is precisely the supremum-of-a-ratio statement). So $H(x,p)$, arrived at here purely from wanting a norm on the dual space, is the same function Chapter 4 built from Legendre-transforming the Lagrangian $F$ — one more instance of this appendix's throughline: geometric reasoning about propagating disturbances keeps landing on exactly the objects the calculus of variations builds by an entirely different, more algebraic route.

04The Propagation Process
Background primer

A primer: envelopes of families of curves and surfaces (what you need, built from scratch)

Take a family of circles in the plane, all of radius $r$, with centers running along some curve $\gamma$. As the center slides along $\gamma$, the circles sweep out a band-shaped region, and that region has two boundary curves — one on each side of $\gamma$ — each one touching (tangent to) every circle in the family at exactly one point. Such a curve, tangent to every member of a family of curves (or, in higher dimensions, every member of a family of surfaces), is called the envelope of the family. The key fact about envelopes, the one this section actually uses: if you know a whole family of spheres, one centered at each point of a surface, the envelope of that family is built by taking, near each center, only the sphere's own point of tangency to the envelope — every other point of every sphere lies strictly on one side or the other of the envelope, not on it.

Wave fronts as envelopes

Suppose that at time $t=0$, some region $R$ of $\mathscr X$ is already excited, with boundary — the wave front — described by an equation $S(x,0)=0$. As time passes, the wave front moves outward, tracing out a family of surfaces $S(x,t)=0$, one for each $t$; the goal is to find the equation these surfaces satisfy. By the on-or-off/re-broadcasting assumption from Section 1, every point $x$ on the wave front $\sigma_t$ (the surface $S(x,t)=0$) becomes, during the next instant $dt$, a source exciting the sphere of radius $dt$ around it (in the norm (8)) — so the new wave front $\sigma_{t+dt}$ must be tangent to every one of these spheres, one centered at each point of $\sigma_t$. In other words, $\sigma_{t+dt}$ is exactly the envelope of the family of spheres $\{f(x,dx)=dt : x\in\sigma_t\}$ — this is the appendix's mathematical model of Huygens' principle, the classical picture (from optics) of every point on a wave front acting as a source of tiny secondary wavelets, whose common envelope is the next instant's wave front.

This construction has two useful consequences, both following from the strict convexity assumed back in Section 1: first, for each starting point $x\in\sigma_t$, there's a unique point $x+dx\in\sigma_{t+dt}$ that a disturbance leaving $x$ reaches fastest — the direction of propagation is completely determined at every point, with no ambiguity. Second, conversely, each point of $\sigma_{t+dt}$ traces back to a unique source point on $\sigma_t$ — the sphere for which it is the actual point of tangency, rather than an interior or exterior point.

05The Hamilton-Jacobi Equation

Now turn the geometric envelope construction of Section 4 into an explicit differential equation for $S(x,t)$. A hyperplane in the tangent space $\mathscr T(x)$ can always be written $\sum_ip_ix^{i\prime}=\text{const}$ for some covariant vector $p=(p_1,\dots,p_n)\in\tilde{\mathscr T}(x)$ (this is exactly Section 3's characterization of linear functionals, applied to describe hyperplanes rather than functionals directly — a hyperplane is a level set of a linear functional). Given a point $x+dx$ on the new wave front $\sigma_{t+dt}$, whose source (by Section 4) is a specific point $x\in\sigma_t$, the tangent hyperplane to $\sigma_{t+dt}$ at $x+dx$ has the equation
$$\sum_i\frac{\partial S}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i = c \tag{15}$$
for some constant $c$ (since $\nabla S=(\partial S/\partial x^1,\dots,\partial S/\partial x^n)$, evaluated at $x$, is exactly the covariant vector defining the tangent hyperplane there). But this same hyperplane must also be tangent to the sphere $f(x,dx)=dt$ centered at $x$ — that's exactly what "the envelope is tangent to every sphere in the family" means — and being tangent to a sphere of radius $dt$ (in the norm whose dual-space picture is exactly $H$, from Section 3) forces the constant $c$ to equal $H(x,\nabla S)$ times the radius $dt$:
$$\sum_i\frac{\partial S}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i = H(x,\nabla S)\,dt. \tag{16}$$
On the other hand, since $x$ (at time $t$) and $x+dx$ (at time $t+dt$) both lie on the zero level set of $S$, expanding $S(x+dx,t+dt)-S(x,t)=0$ to first order gives directly
$$\sum_i\frac{\partial S}{\partial x^i}\,dx^i + \frac{\partial S}{\partial t}\,dt = 0. \tag{17}$$
Comparing (16) and (17) eliminates the $dx^i$ terms entirely, leaving
$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial t} + H(x,\nabla S) = 0. \tag{18}$$

This is exactly the Hamilton–Jacobi equation already met in Chapter 4, Section 23 — arrived at here with no functional, no variation, and no Euler equation anywhere in the derivation, purely from the geometric fact that a wave front is the envelope of spheres centered on the previous wave front. It's worth sitting with how different the two derivations feel even though they land on the identical equation: Chapter 4 built $S$ as an accumulated integral of $F$ along extremals from a fixed starting point, and derived (18) by differentiating that integral; here, $S$ is built directly as "the equation of the wave front at time $t$," and (18) falls out of comparing two different ways of expressing the same tangent hyperplane.

Trajectories from a complete integral. The appendix closes this section by connecting back to Jacobi's theorem (Chapter 4, Section 23, p. 91 of the book): a one-parameter family of wave fronts $S(x,t)=0$ determines, for each choice of starting point $x_0$ on an initial wave front, a unique trajectory — and more generally, a complete integral $S(x,t,\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, depending on $n$ parameters, determines an entire $(2n-1)$-parameter family of trajectories, exactly matching Jacobi's theorem's geometric description of how a complete integral of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation packages the general solution of the corresponding Euler (here, canonical) equations.

06The Canonical Equations

The last step: derive the actual differential equations satisfied by the trajectories — the paths along which disturbances propagate. Parameterize each trajectory by the time $t$ itself (rather than an arbitrary parameter $s$), so that $f(x,dx)=dt$ directly; combined with the homogeneity of $f$ in its second argument, this says
$$f\left(x,\frac{dx}{dt}\right) = 1, \tag{21}$$
i.e., the velocity vector $dx/dt$ always has norm exactly $1$ — a clean restatement of "the trajectory's own speed, measured in the natural units the medium itself provides, is unit speed by construction."

Now bring in the covariant vector $p$ determining the tangent hyperplane to the wave front at each point (from Section 5). By the definition (11) of $H(x,p)$ as a supremum of $(p,x')/\|x'\|$ over all directions $x'$, and the fact that (16)'s $p=\nabla S$ achieves the actual tangency (not just any old direction), a short argument shows the expression $\sum_ip_i\,dx^i/dt-H(x,p)$, viewed as a function of $p$ alone, is maximized exactly when $p$ is this particular tangent-hyperplane vector. Setting the derivative of this expression (with respect to each $p_i$) to zero at the maximum gives
$$\frac{dx^i}{dt} = \frac{\partial H(x,p)}{\partial p_i} \qquad (i=1,\dots,n). \tag{22}$$

This is $n$ of the $2n$ equations needed to pin down a trajectory (since $x^1,\dots,x^n,p_1,\dots,p_n$ together are $2n$ unknown functions of $t$). The remaining $n$ equations come from differentiating $p_i=\partial S/\partial x^i$ along the trajectory and substituting the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (18) itself — a chain-rule computation, carried out carefully in the source material by tracking exactly which partial derivatives of $H$ are taken holding $t$ fixed versus holding $p$ fixed, that eventually collapses to the clean statement
$$\frac{dp_i}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H(x,p)}{\partial x^i} \qquad (i=1,\dots,n). \tag{part of 27}$$

Together, (22) and its partner form the system
$$\frac{dx^i}{dt} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \frac{dp_i}{dt} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial x^i}, \tag{27}$$
which is exactly the canonical system of Euler equations from Chapter 4, Section 16, for the variational problem (2) — and simultaneously the characteristic system associated with the Hamilton–Jacobi equation (18), Chapter 4, Section 23's construction for building a general solution of the Euler equations out of a complete integral of the Hamilton–Jacobi equation. Every major object from Chapter 4's canonical theory — the Hamiltonian, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, and the canonical equations themselves — has now been rebuilt twice over, once from a variational functional and once from nothing but a geometric picture of a spreading disturbance, meeting in exactly the same place both times.

Chapter recap

This appendix took the book's central objects — the Hamiltonian $H$, the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, and the canonical equations — and rebuilt every one of them from scratch, starting not from a functional but from a physical picture of a disturbance spreading through an inhomogeneous, anisotropic medium. Section 1 set up the geometric problem and identified Fermat's principle (fastest path wins) as the propagation mechanism. Section 2 recognized that the physically given travel-time function $f(x,x')$ already satisfies the norm axioms, turning the whole problem into a purely geometric one about a norm defined on the tangent space at each point. Section 3 built the conjugate (dual) space of that norm and showed its natural function $H(x,p)$ is exactly the Legendre transformation from Chapter 4, Section 18, now arising from measuring linear functionals rather than from convex duality. Section 4 modeled the evolution of the wave front as the envelope of a family of spheres, a precise mathematical version of Huygens' principle. Section 5 converted that envelope construction into a differential equation for the wave front — landing, remarkably, on exactly the Hamilton–Jacobi equation from Chapter 4, Section 23. Section 6 finished the job, deriving the canonical equations themselves as the trajectories along which disturbances actually propagate. The throughline, worth carrying away: the calculus of variations' central machinery is not an artifact of starting with a functional and differentiating it — the same equations emerge from the purely geometric problem of how the fastest path to anywhere from anywhere actually behaves, which is exactly why this machinery shows up equally in mechanics, optics, and the geometry of shortest paths.

Practice problems
Problem 1

1. (Homogeneous media propagate along straight lines.) Prove that if $f(x,x')$ depends on direction only (not on position $x$), then the disturbance propagates through the medium along straight lines.

Hint: With $f=f(x')$ independent of $x$, the functional (2) reduces to $\int f(dx/ds)\,ds$, and the strict convexity condition from Section 1 says any detour through an intermediate point takes strictly longer than the direct path — formalize this using the triangle-inequality-style convexity property directly on the norm (8), the same argument sketched in Section 1's discussion of why convexity holds for straight-line propagation in a homogeneous medium.

Problem 2

2. (Interpreting $f(x')$ directly as a travel time.) Prove that if $f(x,x')\equiv f(x')$ is independent of $x$, then $f(x')$ is precisely the time required to traverse the vector $x'$ (thought of as a straight-line displacement, traversed at unit parameter speed).

Hint: Use Problem 1 (straight-line trajectories) together with the defining relation $dt=f(x,dx/ds)\,ds$ from Section 1: integrate along the straight segment from $0$ to $x'$ and use homogeneity (4) to pull the computation down to a single evaluation of $f$ at $x'$ itself.

Problem 3

3. (Every linear functional comes from a dot product — the proof in full.) Prove that every linear functional $\varphi[x]$ defined on an $n$-dimensional Euclidean space of points $x=(x^1,\dots,x^n)$ has the form $\varphi[x]=p_1x^1+\cdots+p_nx^n$, for a uniquely determined vector $p=(p_1,\dots,p_n)$.

Hint: This is the claim underlying the whole primer in Section 3 — write $x=\sum_ix^ie_i$ in terms of the standard basis vectors $e_i$, use linearity to expand $\varphi[x]=\sum_ix^i\varphi[e_i]$, and set $p_i=\varphi[e_i]$. For uniqueness, note that if two vectors $p,\bar p$ both represent $\varphi$, their difference represents the zero functional, and evaluating on each basis vector $e_i$ in turn forces every component of $p-\bar p$ to vanish.

Problem 4

4. (Verifying the dual-space norm's axioms.) Verify that formula (10) actually defines a norm for the elements $p$ of the conjugate space $\tilde{\mathscr T}(x)$.

Hint: Check positivity and homogeneity directly from the supremum definition (10); for the triangle inequality $\|p+\bar p\|\le\|p\|+\|\bar p\|$, use that $(p+\bar p,x')=(p,x')+(\bar p,x')$ for every $x'$, so the supremum of the sum's ratio is bounded by the sum of the two separate suprema — the same style of argument used constantly when bounding a supremum of a sum by a sum of suprema.

Problem 5

5. (Why strict convexity, specifically, is needed.) Explain why the strict convexity condition (Section 1, and referenced again in Section 4's envelope construction) is needed in constructing wave fronts for the disturbance, rather than just the ordinary (non-strict) convexity condition (7).

Hint: Revisit Section 4's two "useful consequences" of strict convexity — a unique direction of fastest propagation from each point, and a unique source point for each point of the new wave front. Consider what could go wrong with non-strict convexity: if equality in (7) could hold for some $\bar x'$ not merely a positive multiple of $x'$, there could be more than one direction achieving the same minimal travel time from a given point, breaking the uniqueness Section 4 relies on to define a single-valued envelope and a well-defined system of trajectories.
