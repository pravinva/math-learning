00Roadmap: what's new here

Chapter 3 ended with a quiet little aside. Buried in Section 13.5, we noticed that if you package the "leftover" boundary information from the general variation formula using the substitution $p_i = F_{y_i'}$ and a new function $H=-F+\sum y_i'p_i$, the messy boundary term $F_{y'}\delta y\big|_{x_0}^{x_1}$ suddenly turns into something clean: $p\,\delta y - H\,\delta x$. We called $p_i$ and $H$ the canonical variables, used them to shorten one formula, and moved on.

Chapter 4 is what happens when you stop treating that as a footnote and ask: what if the whole subject were rebuilt around $p$ and $H$ instead of around $y'$? It turns out the answer is remarkably rich, and it's the same rebuilding that turns Newtonian mechanics into Hamiltonian mechanics — this chapter is, among other things, a from-scratch derivation of why physicists talk about "phase space" and "the Hamiltonian" at all.

Here's the itinerary:

  • Section 16 rewrites the Euler equations — a system of $n$ second-order differential equations — as a system of $2n$ first-order equations in the variables $y_i,p_i$. This is the canonical form the chapter is named after.
  • Section 17 asks: what quantities stay constant along the solutions of this new system? These are called first integrals, and there's an elegant algebraic gadget, the Poisson bracket, that tests for them.
  • Section 18 derives the canonical equations again, completely independently, via a classical piece of convex-analysis machinery called the Legendre transformation — the same tool that turns a Lagrangian into a Hamiltonian in every mechanics textbook you'll ever open.
  • Section 19 studies canonical transformations: changes of the variables $y_i,p_i$ that preserve the clean canonical form of the equations, generalizing the coordinate-invariance result from Chapter 1, Section 8.
  • Section 20 proves Noether's theorem: if a functional doesn't change under some family of transformations (a symmetry), that symmetry manufactures a first integral — a conserved quantity — for free.
  • Sections 21–22 cash all of this in on real physics: the principle of least action shows that Newton's laws themselves are the Euler equations of a certain functional, and Noether's theorem then hands you conservation of energy, momentum, and angular momentum, each from a specific symmetry of space and time.
  • Section 23 closes the chapter with the Hamilton–Jacobi equation, a single first-order partial differential equation whose solutions encode an entire family of extremals at once.

None of this involves a fundamentally new technique — the master tool is still Taylor-expand-and-integrate-by-parts, exactly as in Chapters 1–3. What's new is the viewpoint: instead of asking "which curve is optimal?", we start asking "what structure survives no matter how we describe that curve?" — and that question is what leads somewhere.

16The Canonical Form of the Euler Equations

Start with the familiar functional from Chapter 2, depending on $n$ unknown functions:
$$J[y_1,\dots,y_n] = \int_a^b F(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n')\,dx. \tag{1}$$
Its Euler equations are the system of $n$ second-order equations
$$F_{y_i} - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'} = 0 \qquad (i=1,\dots,n). \tag{2}$$

Why turn a second-order system into a first-order one? This is a standard move you may have already seen in an ODE course: any single second-order equation $y''=g(x,y,y')$ can be rewritten as a pair of first-order equations by introducing the derivative itself as a new unknown function, say $v=y'$, giving $y'=v,\ v'=g(x,y,v)$. Nothing is lost — the two descriptions carry exactly the same information — but first-order systems are the standard currency of the general existence-and-uniqueness theory for differential equations, and they're also what numerical solvers expect. Equation (2), applied the same way to each $y_i$, becomes
$$\frac{dy_i}{dx}=y_i', \qquad F_{y_i}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'}=0 \qquad (i=1,\dots,n), \tag{3}$$
now viewed as $2n$ first-order equations in the $2n$ unknown functions $y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n'$ (with $y_i'$ now treated as an independent function of $x$, not literally "the derivative of $y_i$," until you solve the system and discover that it is one).

This works, but it's not the most useful way to do it. There's a second, more symmetric route, and it's the one the rest of the chapter is built on: replace $y_1',\dots,y_n'$ not by themselves, but by the canonical variables you already met in Chapter 3, Section 13.5.

Setting up the new variables. Define the momenta
$$p_i = F_{y_i'} \qquad (i=1,\dots,n), \tag{4}$$
one for each unknown function. As long as the Jacobian determinant $\det\|F_{y_i'y_k'}\|$ is nonzero — this is exactly the same nonvanishing-Jacobian hypothesis from the coordinate-invariance argument in Chapter 1, Section 8, and it's what makes an inverse function theorem argument work here — equations (4) can be solved (at least locally) for the velocities $y_1',\dots,y_n'$ in terms of the new variables
$$x,\ y_1,\dots,y_n,\ p_1,\dots,p_n. \tag{5}$$
Next, define the Hamiltonian
$$H = -F + \sum_{i=1}^n y_i'p_i, \tag{6}$$
where on the right-hand side every occurrence of $y_i'$ has been replaced by its expression in terms of the variables (5). So $H$ is a genuine function of $x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n$ — the velocities have been eliminated entirely in favor of momenta.

Why bother? Here's the geometric picture worth holding onto. Think of $F_{y_i'}$ as "the slope of $F$'s graph in the $y_i'$-direction, at a fixed point $(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$." Equation (4) trades that slope value for the position $y_i'$ that produced it — exactly like reading off which $x$-value has a given tangent slope on a graph, then relabeling the axis by slope instead of by position. We're about to see that once everything is measured this new way, the Euler equations collapse into something almost embarrassingly clean.

Deriving the canonical equations. We want to know how the Euler equations (3) look once rewritten in terms of $x,y_i,p_i$ instead of $x,y_i,y_i'$. Rather than grinding through the change of variables directly on $F_{y_i}$ (which the book flags as "rather formidable"), there's a shortcut: work out the differential of $H$ two different ways, and match coefficients. A differential is coordinate-free — it doesn't care which variables you've chosen to describe it in — so this sidesteps the messy direct computation entirely.

From the definition (6), treating $H$ as built out of $F$ and the $y_i'$:
$$dH = -dF + \sum_i p_i\,dy_i' + \sum_i y_i'\,dp_i.$$
Expand $dF = F_x\,dx + \sum_i F_{y_i}\,dy_i + \sum_i F_{y_i'}\,dy_i'$ and substitute:
$$dH = -F_x\,dx - \sum_i F_{y_i}\,dy_i - \sum_i F_{y_i'}\,dy_i' + \sum_i p_i\,dy_i' + \sum_i y_i'\,dp_i. \tag{8}$$
Here's the payoff of setting things up this way: since $p_i = F_{y_i'}$ by definition, the two terms containing $dy_i'$ cancel exactly, leaving
$$dH = -F_x\,dx - \sum_i F_{y_i}\,dy_i + \sum_i y_i'\,dp_i. \tag{9}$$
The velocities $y_i'$ have vanished from this formula altogether — which makes sense, since $H$ is a function of $x,y_i,p_i$ only, so its differential should only ever involve $dx,dy_i,dp_i$.

Now read off the partial derivatives of $H$ directly from (9), the same way you'd read $df=f_x\,dx+f_y\,dy$ off any total differential:
$$\frac{\partial H}{\partial x} = -F_x, \qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i} = -F_{y_i}, \qquad \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} = y_i'. \tag{10}$$
The last two of these are exactly what we were after. They say $F_{y_i} = -\partial H/\partial y_i$ and $y_i' = \partial H/\partial p_i$. Substitute the first into the Euler equation $F_{y_i} - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'} = 0$, remembering $F_{y_i'}=p_i$ so $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'} = dp_i/dx$:
$$-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i} - \frac{dp_i}{dx} = 0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{dp_i}{dx} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i}.$$
Combined with $dy_i/dx = y_i' = \partial H/\partial p_i$, we've arrived at the canonical system of Euler equations:
$$\boxed{\frac{dy_i}{dx} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}, \qquad \frac{dp_i}{dx} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i} \qquad (i=1,\dots,n).} \tag{11}$$

Take a moment to appreciate the symmetry here. The equation for $y_i$ and the equation for $p_i$ are mirror images of each other, up to a single minus sign. This is not decoration — that minus sign is the entire reason phase space (the space of $(y_i,p_i)$ pairs) has the geometric structure it does, and it's what makes the Poisson bracket in the next section behave the way it does. A single scalar function $H$ — not $2n$ separate rules — now determines the entire dynamics.

17First Integrals of the Euler Equations

A first integral of a system of differential equations is a function that stays constant along every solution curve of the system — a quantity the system conserves, even as everything else about the solution changes. (You've already met one example without the name: in Chapter 1, Cases 1–2, whenever $F$ didn't depend on $x$ or on $y$, we found a combination of $F$ and its derivatives that stayed constant along extremals. That was already a first integral in disguise.)

The easy case: $F$ doesn't depend on $x$. Suppose $F=F(y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n')$, with no explicit $x$-dependence. Then by (6), $H$ also has no explicit $x$-dependence: $H=H(y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n)$. Along a solution of the canonical system, the chain rule gives
$$\frac{dH}{dx} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i}\frac{dy_i}{dx} + \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{dp_i}{dx}\right). \tag{12}$$
Now substitute the canonical equations (11) themselves — $dy_i/dx=\partial H/\partial p_i$ and $dp_i/dx=-\partial H/\partial y_i$ — directly into the right-hand side:
$$\frac{dH}{dx} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i}\right) = 0.$$
Every term cancels against its mirror-image partner — a direct consequence of the "mirror symmetry with a minus sign" we flagged at the end of Section 16. So $H$ is automatically constant along every extremal whenever $F$ doesn't explicitly involve $x$. This matches — and explains, at a deeper level — Case 2 from Chapter 1: $F-y'F_{y'}=$ const was exactly $H=$ const in disguise, for the single-function case.

The general question, and the Poisson bracket. What if we don't assume $F$ is $x$-independent, and instead ask: for a general function $\Phi=\Phi(y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n)$, when is $\Phi$ a first integral? Run the exact same computation as above, but for $\Phi$ instead of $H$:
$$\frac{d\Phi}{dx} = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y_i}\frac{dy_i}{dx}+\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p_i}\frac{dp_i}{dx}\right) = \sum_i\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i}\right).$$
This combination of $\Phi$ and $H$ — built the same way every time, out of any two functions of $y_i,p_i$ — comes up often enough to deserve its own name and notation. Define the Poisson bracket of two functions $\Phi$ and $H$ by
$$[\Phi,H] = \sum_{i=1}^n\left(\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial y_i}\right). \tag{13'}$$
Then the computation above says exactly
$$\frac{d\Phi}{dx} = [\Phi,H]. \tag{13}$$
This one identity is worth memorizing, because it converts the analytic question "does $\Phi$ stay constant along solutions?" into the purely algebraic question "does a certain sum of products of partial derivatives vanish?" — no differential equation needs to be solved at all:

A function $\Phi(y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n)$ is a first integral of the canonical Euler equations (11) if and only if the Poisson bracket $[\Phi,H]$ vanishes identically.

(A small technical note if $\Phi$ or $H$ depends on $x$ explicitly too: an existence-and-uniqueness argument — there's exactly one integral curve of (11) through each point $(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n)$ — shows the "if" direction still holds without change, and the formula for $d\Phi/dx$ just picks up an extra $\partial\Phi/\partial x$ term.)

Notice the bracket is antisymmetric: swapping the two arguments flips the sign, $[\Phi,H]=-[H,\Phi]$, which immediately tells you $[H,H]=0$ — a one-line proof, via the bracket, that $H$ is always a first integral whenever it has no explicit $x$-dependence (matching what we found directly above).

18The Legendre Transformation

Section 16 derived the canonical equations by brute-force differentiation. This section derives them again, from a completely different starting point, using a piece of machinery — the Legendre transformation — that turns out to be exactly the right lens for understanding why momentum is the natural dual variable to velocity, not just an algebraic convenience.

18.1 The Legendre transform of an ordinary function

Strip away the calculus-of-variations machinery for a moment and consider the humblest possible setting: a single strictly convex function of one variable, $f(\xi)$, meaning
$$f''(\xi) > 0 \quad\text{wherever } f \text{ is defined.}$$
Convexity means the graph curves upward everywhere, like $\xi^2$ or $e^\xi$ — no inflection points, no flat spots. Geometrically, this guarantees something useful: as $\xi$ sweeps across its domain, the slope $f'(\xi)$ increases monotonically, so distinct points of the curve have distinct tangent-line slopes, and each possible slope value $p$ is achieved at exactly one point $\xi$.

That fact licenses a change of perspective. Introduce a new variable
$$p = f'(\xi), \tag{14}$$
called the tangential coordinate — literally just the slope of the tangent line at $\xi$. Because $dp/d\xi = f''(\xi)>0$ everywhere, (14) can be inverted: $\xi$ can be written as a function of $p$. In other words, instead of specifying a point on the curve by where it is ($\xi$), you can specify it by how steep the curve is there ($p$) — since convexity guarantees no two points share a slope, this is just as much information.

Now define a brand new function of $p$:
$$H(p) = -f(\xi) + p\xi, \tag{15}$$
where $\xi$ on the right is understood as the function of $p$ obtained by solving (14). The pair of substitutions (14)–(15), carrying $(\xi,f(\xi))$ to $(p,H(p))$, is called the Legendre transformation.

Why this particular combination? One clean way to see it: differentiate (15) with respect to $p$, treating $\xi$ as a function of $p$ via the chain rule,
$$\frac{dH}{dp} = -f'(\xi)\frac{d\xi}{dp} + \xi + p\frac{d\xi}{dp} = -p\frac{d\xi}{dp}+\xi+p\frac{d\xi}{dp} = \xi, \tag{16}$$
where we used $f'(\xi)=p$ in the middle step. So the derivative of $H$ recovers $\xi$, in perfect parallel with (14), where the derivative of $f$ produced $p$. Differentiating (16) again,
$$\frac{d^2H}{dp^2} = \frac{d\xi}{dp} = \frac{1}{d p/d\xi} = \frac{1}{f''(\xi)} > 0, \tag{17}$$
so $H(p)$ is also strictly convex — the Legendre transform of a convex function is convex. (Such a pair of functions $f,H$ is said to be conjugate.) Moreover, applying the whole procedure a second time — treating $p,H(p)$ the way we treated $\xi,f(\xi)$ — takes you right back to $\xi,f(\xi)$, by (16) together with the identity
$$-H(p) + pH'(p) = f(\xi) - p\xi + p\xi = f(\xi). \tag{18}$$
So the Legendre transform is an involution: applying it twice is the identity. This is worth pausing on, because it's the abstract reason the relationship between $F$ (built from velocities) and $H$ (built from momenta) in the calculus-of-variations setting will turn out to be perfectly symmetric — momentum is dual to velocity in exactly the same reversible sense that $p$ is dual to $\xi$ here.

A worked example. Let $f(\xi)=\xi^a/a$ for some $a>1$ (so $f''(\xi)=(a-1)\xi^{a-2}>0$ for $\xi>0$, confirming convexity). Then $p=f'(\xi)=\xi^{a-1}$, so $\xi=p^{1/(a-1)}$, and
$$H = -\frac{\xi^a}{a} + p\xi = -\frac{p^{a/(a-1)}}{a} + p\cdot p^{1/(a-1)} = p^{a/(a-1)}\left(1-\frac1a\right) = \frac{p^b}{b}, \qquad \text{where } \frac1a+\frac1b=1.$$
So the Legendre transform of $\xi^a/a$ is $p^b/b$, with $a$ and $b$ related by $\frac1a+\frac1b=1$ — a pairing you may recognize from Hölder's inequality in analysis, and no coincidence: this exact conjugate pair is what makes Young's inequality (below) sharp.

The variational characterization. Here's a second, geometrically vivid way to think about $H(p)$, which the book states as a maximization principle: regard $-H(p)+\xi p$ as a function of two independent variables $\xi$ and $p$ (forget for a moment that they're secretly related by (14)). Then
$$f(\xi) = \max_p\bigl[-H(p)+\xi p\bigr]. \tag{20}$$
Why? Differentiate the right-hand side with respect to $p$ (holding $\xi$ fixed) and set it to zero:
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\bigl[-H(p)+\xi p\bigr] = -H'(p)+\xi = 0 \iff \xi = H'(p) = \xi \text{ (by (16))},$$
so the maximizing value of $p$ is exactly the $p$ satisfying (14) — and at that value, $-H(p)+\xi p$ collapses back to $f(\xi)$ by (18). It really is a maximum, not just a critical point, because $\partial^2/\partial p^2\bigl[-H(p)+\xi p\bigr] = -H''(p)<0$ by (17). Geometrically: (20) says $f(\xi)$ is the envelope from above of the family of straight lines $p\mapsto \xi p - H(p)$, one line for each value of $p$ — a picture worth sketching for yourself with $f(\xi)=\xi^2/2$, whose lines $\xi p - p^2/2$ trace out the familiar parabola as their upper envelope.

Several variables, and Young's inequality. The same construction goes through, essentially unchanged, for a function of $n$ variables $f(\xi_1,\dots,\xi_n)$ satisfying the nondegeneracy condition $\det\|f_{\xi_i\xi_k}\|\ne0$: define $p_i=f_{\xi_i}$, then $H(p_1,\dots,p_n)=-f+\sum_i \xi_ip_i$, and the extremum of $f$ is also an extremum of $-H+\sum p_i\xi_i$ viewed as a function of $2n$ independent variables. If the stronger condition holds that the matrix $\|f_{\xi_i\xi_k}\|$ is positive definite — i.e. $f$ is strictly convex in all directions at once — then the maximization statement (20) generalizes cleanly to
$$f(\xi_1,\dots,\xi_n) = \max_{p_1,\dots,p_n}\left[-H(p_1,\dots,p_n)+\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\xi_i\right]. \tag{23}$$
Since the maximum is always at least as large as the value at any particular point, (23) immediately gives, for arbitrary $p_1,\dots,p_n$ (not just the maximizing ones),
$$\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\xi_i \le H(p_1,\dots,p_n) + f(\xi_1,\dots,\xi_n), \tag{Young's inequality}$$
a useful general-purpose inequality (specializing, for the conjugate pair found above, to the classical Young's inequality $p\xi\le \xi^a/a+p^b/b$ from analysis).

18.2 Applying this to functionals

Now bring the Legendre transformation back to the calculus of variations. Given
$$J[y] = \int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,dx, \tag{24}$$
set
$$p = F_{y'}(x,y,y'), \tag{25}$$
assuming $F_{y'y'}\ne0$ so that (25) can be solved for $y'$ in terms of $x,y,p$ (this is the Legendre-transform nondegeneracy condition, applied at each fixed $(x,y)$, treating $F$ as a function of the single variable $y'$), and define
$$H(x,y,p) = -F+py'. \tag{26}$$
(This is exactly $H$ from Section 16, specialized to $n=1$ — the Legendre transform is the same operation, just now performed pointwise at each $x$, with $x$ and $y$ carried along as passive parameters throughout.)

A new, equivalent variational problem. Here's the beautiful move: introduce a brand-new functional, treating $y$ and $p$ as two completely independent unknown functions (not related by (25) in advance — that relationship will fall out of the equations, not be assumed):
$$J[y,p] = \int_a^b\bigl[-H(x,y,p)+py'\bigr]\,dx. \tag{27}$$
This looks unrelated to (24) — it has an extra unknown function $p$ that (24) never had — but it's secretly the same problem in disguise. Let's find its Euler equations, treating $y,p$ as the two unknown functions of a two-function system (Chapter 2, Section 9):
$$-\frac{\partial H}{\partial y}-\frac{dp}{dx}=0, \qquad -\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}+\frac{dy}{dx}=0, \tag{28}$$
i.e. exactly the canonical equations (11) for $n=1$. So (27) is a functional whose Euler equations are automatically the canonical form — no derivation like Section 16's chain-rule argument needed this time.

Why (24) and (27) have the same extremals. First, a useful fact carried over unchanged from the earlier derivation: applying a Legendre transform twice gets you back where you started, so
$$-H+p\frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = F. \tag{30}$$
(This is just (18) with $\xi\to y'$, restated in the new notation — check it directly: $dH=-F_xdx-F_ydy+y'dp$ by the same cancellation trick as Section 16, so $\partial H/\partial p=y'$, and then $-H+p\cdot y' = F-py'+py'=F$.)

Now here's the key structural claim: for fixed $y$, varying only $p$, the functional $J[y,p]$ is extremized exactly when $p$ equals $F_{y'}$ — i.e.
$$J[y] = \operatorname*{ext}_p J[y,p]. \tag{31}$$
Since $J[y,p]$ (unlike (24)) contains no derivative $p'$ of $p$ at all, extremizing it over $p$ alone, with $y$ fixed, is a pointwise problem — extremize the integrand itself at each $x$ separately (exactly like Case 3 from Chapter 1's special cases, where the integrand didn't depend on $y'$). So set the $p$-partial-derivative of the integrand to zero:
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial p}\bigl[-H+py'\bigr]=0 \implies y' = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p}.$$
Substituting this back and using (30), $-H+p\,\partial H/\partial p = F$ exactly — the integrand of $J[y,p]$ collapses to the integrand of $J[y]$. That proves (31), and since an extremum of $J[y,p]$ over both $y$ and $p$ at once is in particular an extremum over $p$ alone for each fixed $y$, this establishes that the two variational problems (24) and (27) — hence their Euler equations (29) and (28) — are equivalent. That's a second, entirely independent proof that the canonical equations capture the same content as the original Euler equation, this time via a change of functional rather than a change of variables in a fixed one.

Worked example

Take $F=Py'^2+Qy^2$ with $P,Q$ functions of $x$ (this is exactly the type of quadratic functional whose Euler equation you've solved before — it's a linear second-order ODE). Then
$$p = F_{y'} = 2Py' \implies y' = \frac{p}{2P}, \qquad H = -F+py' = -(Py'^2+Qy^2)+p\cdot\frac{p}{2P}.$$
Substituting $y'=p/(2P)$ throughout and simplifying, $Py'^2 = p^2/(4P)$, so $H = -\dfrac{p^2}{4P}-Qy^2+\dfrac{p^2}{2P} = \dfrac{p^2}{4P}-Qy^2$. The canonical equations follow immediately from (11):
$$\frac{dp}{dx} = -\frac{\partial H}{\partial y} = 2Qy, \qquad \frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{\partial H}{\partial p} = \frac{p}{2P},$$
while the ordinary Euler equation for the original functional is $2yQ-\frac{d}{dx}(2Py')=0$ — a single second-order equation that (11) has repackaged into the pair above.

19Canonical Transformations

Chapter 1, Section 8, proved that the Euler equation $F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ is invariant under any smooth, invertible change of the coordinates $x,y$: if you relabel the plane by new coordinates $u=u(x,y),\ v=v(x,y)$ (with nonzero Jacobian), the extremal property of a curve doesn't care which labels you used to describe it. This section asks the analogous question for the canonical variables: which changes of the phase-space variables $x,y_i,p_i$ preserve the clean canonical form (11) of the Euler equations?

There's an obvious answer available — just let $p_i$ transform however it needs to, to match whatever transformation you apply to $y_i$, as dictated by the original definition $p_i=F_{y_i'}$. But the canonical equations turn out to permit something much more generous: since $y_i$ and $p_i$ play a fully symmetric role in (11) (up to that one sign), you can transform both sets of variables into new variables
$$Y_i = Y_i(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n), \qquad P_i = P_i(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n), \tag{33}$$
mixing $y$'s and $p$'s freely, and still end up with canonical-looking equations
$$\frac{dY_i}{dx}=\frac{\partial H^*}{\partial P_i}, \qquad \frac{dP_i}{dx}=-\frac{\partial H^*}{\partial Y_i} \tag{34}$$
in the new variables, for some new Hamiltonian $H^*$ — provided the transformation (33) is chosen correctly. Not every transformation (33) has this property; the ones that do are called canonical transformations, and finding a clean test for them is the point of this section.

The trick: reformulate as equivalence of functionals. We already know (Section 18.2) that the canonical equations (11) are exactly the Euler equations of the "phase-space functional"
$$J[y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n] = \int_a^b\left(\sum_i p_iy_i'-H\right)dx, \tag{36}$$
where $y_i,p_i$ are now treated as $2n$ independent unknown functions (this is (27), generalized to $n$ functions). By the same logic, (34) is exactly the Euler-equation system of the analogous functional built from $Y_i,P_i,H^*$:
$$J^*[Y_1,\dots,Y_n,P_1,\dots,P_n] = \int_a^b\left(\sum_i P_iY_i'-H^*\right)dx. \tag{37}$$
So the question "does (33) carry the canonical system (35) into a canonical system (34)?" is exactly the question "are the variational problems (36) and (37) equivalent — do they have the same extremals?"

Now recall a fact from Chapter 1 (the remark on p. 36, if you want to look it back up): two functionals with the same unknown functions have identical extremals if their integrands differ only by a total differential — an exact $d\Phi$ contributes nothing to $\delta J$, since $\int_a^b d\Phi = \Phi(b)-\Phi(a)$ depends only on the endpoint values, not on the path taken between them. Applying that here, (36) and (37) are equivalent exactly when
$$\sum_i p_i\,dy_i - H\,dx = \sum_i P_i\,dY_i - H^*\,dx + d\Phi(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n) \tag{38}$$
for some function $\Phi$. Whenever a transformation (33) satisfies (38) for some $\Phi$, that transformation is canonical, and $\Phi$ is called its generating function (defined, like any function specified only through its differential, up to an additive constant).

Recovering the transformation from its generating function. Suppose you're handed a generating function $\Phi$, expressed conveniently as a function of the old position variables $y_i$ and the new position variables $Y_i$ (a mixed choice that turns out to make the formulas cleanest): $\Phi=\Phi(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,Y_1,\dots,Y_n)$. Rearranging (38),
$$d\Phi = \sum_i p_i\,dy_i - \sum_i P_i\,dY_i + (H^*-H)\,dx,$$
and reading off coefficients exactly as we did for $H$ back in Section 16,
$$p_i = \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial y_i}, \qquad P_i = \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial Y_i}, \qquad H^* = H+\frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial x}. \tag{39}$$
These $2n+1$ equations are the transformation itself: the first $n$ of them, solved for $Y_i$ in terms of $x,y_i,p_i$, plus the second $n$, tell you exactly what (33) is, and the last tells you the new Hamiltonian. (If $\Phi$ has no explicit $x$-dependence, $H^*=H$ — the Hamiltonian's numerical value doesn't change, only the variables it's expressed in.) You can equally well express the generating function in terms of $y_i$ and the new momenta $P_i$ instead of $Y_i$ — a short computation (rewriting (38) using $d(\Phi+\sum P_iY_i)=\sum p_idy_i+\sum Y_idP_i+(H^*-H)dx$) gives the mirror-image formulas
$$p_i = \frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial y_i}, \qquad Y_i = \frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial P_i}, \qquad H^* = H+\frac{\partial\Psi}{\partial x}, \tag{41}$$
for the new generating function $\Psi=\Phi+\sum_i P_iY_i$.

The upshot: canonical transformations are not some exotic species you'd need to search for by trial and error — every one of them comes stamped out of a single scalar function $\Phi$ (or $\Psi$) via formulas (39) (or (41)), and conversely every choice of $\Phi$ produces a valid canonical transformation. This is a large family of symmetries of the canonical equations — far larger than the coordinate changes of $x,y$ alone from Chapter 1 — and it's the starting point for the deeper Hamilton–Jacobi theory in Section 23.

20Noether's Theorem

Section 17 found that $H$ is a first integral whenever $F$ doesn't explicitly depend on $x$. There's a suggestive way to restate that hypothesis: "$F$ doesn't depend on $x$" means the functional $J[y]=\int F(y,y')\,dx$ doesn't change value if you shift every curve sideways along the $x$-axis by some fixed amount $\varepsilon$ — replace $x$ by $x^*=x+\varepsilon$ throughout, and the value of the integral is unaffected. In other words, that specific conserved quantity ($H$) is tied to a specific symmetry of the functional (invariance under horizontal shifts).

Noether's theorem says this connection is completely general: every continuous symmetry of a functional produces a first integral of its Euler equations, and there's an explicit formula for exactly which one. This is one of the most consequential ideas in all of mathematical physics — Sections 21–22 will use it to derive conservation of energy, momentum, and angular momentum, each from a specific, nameable symmetry of space and time — so it's worth building up carefully.

What "invariant under a transformation" means, precisely. Consider a functional (written now in vector shorthand, with $y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)$)
$$J[y] = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y,y')\,dx, \tag{44}$$
and a transformation carrying $(x,y,y')$ to new values
$$x^* = \Phi(x,y,y'), \qquad y_i^* = \Psi_i(x,y,y') \qquad (i=1,\dots,n). \tag{45}$$
Given any curve $\gamma:\ y=y(x)$ on $[x_0,x_1]$, substituting $y(x),y'(x)$ into (45) and eliminating $x$ produces a new curve $\gamma^*:\ y^*=y^*(x^*)$ on some new interval. The functional (44) is invariant under the transformation (45) if evaluating $J$ on the transformed curve always reproduces the same number as evaluating it on the original curve:
$$J[\gamma^*] = J[\gamma], \qquad\text{i.e.}\qquad \int_{x_0^*}^{x_1^*}F\left(x^*,y^*,\frac{dy^*}{dx^*}\right)dx^* = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}F\left(x,y,\frac{dy}{dx}\right)dx, \tag{invariance}$$
for every curve $\gamma$ and every choice of endpoints $x_0,x_1$.

Two contrasting examples, to build intuition for what invariance feels like.

Example 1. $J[y]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}y'^2\,dx$ is invariant under a horizontal shift $x^*=x+\varepsilon,\ y^*=y$. Geometrically this is obvious — sliding a curve sideways doesn't change its shape, hence not $\int y'^2\,dx$, which only depends on the curve's shape. Algebraically: if $\gamma^*$ is $\gamma$ shifted by $\varepsilon$, then $y^*(x^*)=y(x^*-\varepsilon)$, so
$$J[\gamma^*] = \int_{x_0+\varepsilon}^{x_1+\varepsilon}\left[\frac{dy(x^*-\varepsilon)}{dx^*}\right]^2dx^* = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}\left[\frac{dy(x)}{dx}\right]^2dx = J[\gamma]$$
(substituting $x=x^*-\varepsilon$ in the last step) — confirming the geometric intuition with an actual computation.

Example 2. By contrast, $J[y]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}xy'^2\,dx$ is not invariant under the same shift, precisely because the explicit factor of $x$ does care about horizontal position: repeating the calculation above picks up an extra factor of $(x+\varepsilon)$ instead of $x$, and the difference $J[\gamma^*]-J[\gamma]=\varepsilon\int_{x_0}^{x_1}[y'(x)]^2dx\ne0$ in general. This is the general lesson: a functional is invariant under a shift in some variable exactly when its integrand doesn't explicitly depend on that variable — which is why "F doesn't depend on x" and "invariant under horizontal shifts" turned out to be the same hypothesis in the motivating discussion above.

Families of transformations, and the statement of the theorem. Now consider not just one transformation, but a whole family of them, indexed by a continuous parameter $\varepsilon$:
$$x^* = \Phi(x,y,y';\varepsilon), \qquad y_i^* = \Psi_i(x,y,y';\varepsilon), \tag{47}$$
with $\varepsilon=0$ recovering the identity transformation ($\Phi(\cdot;0)=x,\ \Psi_i(\cdot;0)=y_i$), and both functions differentiable in $\varepsilon$. Think of $\varepsilon$ as a knob: turning it continuously sweeps out a continuous family of symmetries (a shift by $\varepsilon$, a rotation by angle $\varepsilon$, and so on).

Theorem (Noether). If the functional $J[y]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y,y')\,dx$ is invariant under the family (47) for every choice of endpoints $x_0,x_1$, then
$$\sum_{i=1}^n F_{y_i'}\psi_i + \left(F-\sum_{i=1}^n y_i'F_{y_i'}\right)\varphi = \text{const} \tag{50}$$
along every extremal of $J[y]$, where
$$\varphi(x,y,y') = \frac{\partial\Phi}{\partial\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}, \qquad \psi_i(x,y,y') = \frac{\partial\Psi_i}{\partial\varepsilon}\bigg|_{\varepsilon=0}.$$

The functions $\varphi,\psi_i$ are simply the rate of change of the transformation at $\varepsilon=0$ — the "velocity" of the symmetry as you first start turning the knob. So the theorem's message, in a sentence, is: every one-parameter family of transformations that leaves $J[y]$ unchanged produces its own first integral, given by formula (50), built purely out of the initial rate of change of that family. No solving of the Euler equations is needed to find the conserved quantity — only to know that the symmetry exists.

Proof. Since $\varepsilon$ is small, Taylor-expand (47) around $\varepsilon=0$, using the identity-transformation condition $\Phi(\cdot;0)=x,\ \Psi_i(\cdot;0)=y_i$:
$$x^* = x+\varepsilon\varphi(x,y,y')+o(\varepsilon), \qquad y_i^* = y_i+\varepsilon\psi_i(x,y,y')+o(\varepsilon). \tag{52}$$
Now here is the crucial move: read (52) as saying that the family (47), for small $\varepsilon$, is (to leading order) exactly the general variation of Chapter 3! Set $\delta x=\varepsilon\varphi,\ \delta y_i=\varepsilon\psi_i$ (the principal linear parts of the shifts, in the language of Chapter 3, Section 13). Then formula (11) from Chapter 3 (the general-variation formula, specialized to fixed interior behavior but free endpoints, with $h(x)$ absorbed into the boundary displacements exactly as that section's bookkeeping allows) gives the corresponding variation of $J[y]$ evaluated between the endpoints:
$$\delta J = \varepsilon\left[\sum_{i=1}^n F_{y_i'}\psi_i + \left(F-\sum_i y_i'F_{y_i'}\right)\varphi\right]_{x=x_0}^{x=x_1}.$$
But $J[y]$ is invariant under (47) by hypothesis, for every choice of endpoints — so $\delta J=0$ identically, meaning the bracketed quantity evaluated at $x_1$ must equal the same quantity evaluated at $x_0$, for every choice of $x_0,x_1$. That's precisely the statement that the bracketed quantity is constant along the extremal — which is (50). $\blacksquare$

In canonical variables. Formula (50) simplifies beautifully once rewritten using the momenta $p_i=F_{y_i'}$ and Hamiltonian $H=-F+\sum y_i'p_i$ from Section 16 — the combination $F-\sum y_i'F_{y_i'}$ is exactly $-H$, so (50) becomes
$$\sum_{i=1}^n p_i\psi_i - H\varphi = \text{const.} \tag{53}$$

Example 3, revisited

Take $F=F(y,y')$ with no explicit $x$-dependence — exactly the hypothesis of Section 17's easy case. As in Example 1, $J[y]=\int F(y,y')\,dx$ is invariant under the one-parameter shift family $x^*=x+\varepsilon,\ y_i^*=y_i$, so $\varphi=1$ and every $\psi_i=0$. Formula (53) then collapses to $H=\text{const}$ — recovering the result of Section 17 as a special case of Noether's theorem, now understood as the consequence of a specific, geometrically obvious symmetry (translation invariance in $x$) rather than as a standalone computation. This is the pattern Sections 21–22 will run again and again: name a symmetry, apply (53), read off a conservation law.

21The Principle of Least Action

Now for the physics. Everything up to this point has been developed for an abstract functional $J[y]$. This section identifies a specific functional whose Euler equations turn out to be nothing other than Newton's laws of motion — meaning classical mechanics itself is a variational problem in disguise, and everything proved in this chapter (canonical form, Poisson brackets, Noether's theorem) applies to it directly.

Background primer

A primer: kinetic energy, potential energy, force

If you haven't seen this notation before: a particle of mass $m$ moving along a path $x(t),y(t),z(t)$ in space has kinetic energy $\frac12 m(\dot x^2+\dot y^2+\dot z^2)$ (the dot denotes $d/dt$; this is just $\frac12mv^2$, generalized to three coordinates via the Pythagorean-style sum of squared velocity components). A force field is called conservative if it can be written as (minus) the gradient of some scalar function $U$ — the potential energy — meaning the force's three components are $X=-\partial U/\partial x,\ Y=-\partial U/\partial y,\ Z=-\partial U/\partial z$; the minus sign is a convention ensuring that a particle is pushed downhill, toward lower potential energy, exactly the way gravity pushes objects toward lower height. This single scalar function $U$ therefore packages an entire force field.

Consider $n$ particles, the $i$-th having mass $m_i$ and coordinates $(x_i,y_i,z_i)$. Their total kinetic energy is
$$T = \frac12\sum_{i=1}^n m_i(\dot x_i^2+\dot y_i^2+\dot z_i^2), \tag{56}$$
and suppose the system has a potential energy $U=U(t,x_1,y_1,z_1,\dots,x_n,y_n,z_n)$, so the force on the $i$-th particle is $X_i=-\partial U/\partial x_i$, and similarly for $Y_i,Z_i$. Define the Lagrangian
$$L = T-U, \tag{58}$$
a single function of time, positions, and velocities.

Theorem (Principle of Least Action). The motion of the system during $[t_0,t_1]$ is described by the functions $x_i(t),y_i(t),z_i(t)$ that minimize the action integral
$$\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L\,dt. \tag{59}$$

This is a striking claim: it says nature "chooses," among every conceivable way the particles could move between their initial and final configurations, the one making a certain integral of kinetic-minus-potential energy as small as possible — a claim about a whole trajectory at once, not (as Newton's laws are usually stated) a local, instant-by-instant rule.

Proof (that this reproduces Newton's laws). If (59) is minimized, its Euler equations (one triple per particle, exactly the system-of-several-functions machinery from Chapter 2, Section 9) must hold:
$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial x_i}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x_i}=0, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial y_i}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot y_i}=0, \qquad \frac{\partial L}{\partial z_i}-\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot z_i}=0. \tag{60}$$
Since $U$ depends only on positions (and possibly time), not on velocities, $\partial L/\partial x_i = \partial T/\partial x_i - \partial U/\partial x_i = -\partial U/\partial x_i$ (as $T$ has no explicit position-dependence). And since $T$ is a sum of squared velocities with coefficients $\frac12 m_i$, $\partial L/\partial \dot x_i=\partial T/\partial \dot x_i = m_i\dot x_i$. Substituting into (60):
$$-\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i}-\frac{d}{dt}(m_i\dot x_i)=0, \quad\text{i.e.}\quad -\frac{\partial U}{\partial x_i} = m_i\ddot x_i,$$
and similarly for $y_i,z_i$. But $-\partial U/\partial x_i$ is exactly $X_i$, the $x$-component of the force on particle $i$ — so this is precisely
$$m_i\ddot x_i = X_i, \qquad m_i\ddot y_i=Y_i, \qquad m_i\ddot z_i=Z_i,$$
which is Newton's second law, $F=ma$, applied to each particle separately, with no constraints. $\blacksquare$

So the entire content of Newtonian mechanics — for an unconstrained system of particles under conservative forces — is packaged inside one scalar function, the Lagrangian, and one variational principle. (Two caveats worth flagging, briefly: constrained systems still obey the principle, but the admissible curves must then also satisfy the constraints, turning it into the isoperimetric-flavored variational problems of Chapter 2; and, more subtly, the principle as stated is really only guaranteed to hold over sufficiently short time intervals — over longer intervals it can fail to be a genuine minimum, an issue Gelfand and Fomin return to in a later chapter on sufficient conditions.)

22Conservation Laws

With mechanics identified as a variational problem, the machinery from Sections 16–20 applies immediately — and produces, with no further physics input, the three great conservation laws of introductory mechanics.

Setting up the canonical variables. For the action functional $\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L\,dt$ (with $L=T-U$ from (58)), the canonical momenta are
$$p_{ix}=\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot x_i}=m_i\dot x_i, \qquad p_{iy}=m_i\dot y_i, \qquad p_{iz}=m_i\dot z_i \tag{momenta}$$
— literally the ordinary physical momentum ($mv$) of each particle, in each coordinate direction; this is exactly why $p_i=F_{y_i'}$ was called "momentum" all along, and now we see the name is not just an analogy. The Hamiltonian works out to
$$H = \sum_i(\dot x_ip_{ix}+\dot y_ip_{iy}+\dot z_ip_{iz})-L = 2T-(T-U)=T+U,$$
i.e. $H$ is the total energy — kinetic plus potential — of the system.

1. Conservation of energy. Suppose the system is conservative, meaning $U$ (and hence $L$) has no explicit time-dependence. Section 17's result — $H$ is a first integral whenever the integrand has no explicit dependence on the independent variable — applies immediately with $t$ playing the role of $x$: $H=T+U=\text{const}$ along the motion. The total energy of a conservative system never changes.

2. Conservation of momentum. Consider the family of transformations that shifts every particle in the system by the same amount $\varepsilon$ along the $x$-axis, leaving everything else (including time) untouched:
$$x_i^*=x_i+\varepsilon, \qquad y_i^*=y_i, \qquad z_i^*=z_i \qquad (i=1,\dots,n).$$
Here $\varphi=0$ (time itself isn't touched) and $\psi_{ix}=1,\ \psi_{iy}=\psi_{iz}=0$ for every particle. If the action integral (62) happens to be invariant under this family — physically, this says the potential energy $U$ doesn't care about a uniform sideways translation of the whole system, i.e. space is homogeneous in the $x$-direction as far as this system's interactions are concerned — Noether's theorem (formula (50), or its momentum-only special case worked out directly in the book) gives the first integral
$$\sum_{i=1}^n p_{ix} = \text{const}.$$
The same argument applies separately to shifts along $y$ and along $z$. Collecting the three components into a single vector $\mathbf P=(P_x,P_y,P_z)$ with $P_x=\sum_ip_{ix}$ and so on — the total momentum of the system — we've shown: if the action is invariant under translations in space, the total momentum of the system is conserved. This is Noether's theorem doing real physical work: the reason momentum is conserved is that space doesn't play favorites among locations.

3. Conservation of angular momentum. Now consider rotations about the $z$-axis by angle $\varepsilon$:
$$x_i^* = x_i\cos\varepsilon+y_i\sin\varepsilon, \qquad y_i^*=-x_i\sin\varepsilon+y_i\cos\varepsilon, \qquad z_i^*=z_i.$$
Differentiating with respect to $\varepsilon$ at $\varepsilon=0$ (recalling $\frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\cos\varepsilon|_{0}=0,\ \frac{d}{d\varepsilon}\sin\varepsilon|_0=1$) gives $\psi_{ix}=y_i,\ \psi_{iy}=-x_i,\ \psi_{iz}=0$. If the action is invariant under this family of rotations — physically, the system's interactions don't distinguish one direction in the $xy$-plane from another — Noether's theorem produces the first integral
$$\sum_{i=1}^n(p_{ix}y_i-p_{iy}x_i) = \text{const}. \tag{63}$$
Each term in this sum is (up to sign) the $z$-component of the cross product $\mathbf p_i\times\mathbf r_i$, where $\mathbf r_i=(x_i,y_i,z_i)$ is the position vector and $\mathbf p_i$ the momentum vector of the $i$-th particle — a quantity called the particle's angular momentum about the origin. So (63) says the $z$-component of the total angular momentum is conserved; invariance of the action under rotations about the $x$- or $y$-axis gives the other two components by the same argument. If the action is invariant under all rotations, the total angular momentum of the system is conserved.

Two illustrative examples. A single particle attracted to a fixed point, by some central force law: energy is conserved (the setup is time-independent), and angular momentum is conserved (rotating the whole configuration about the attracting point doesn't change anything), but linear momentum is not conserved — sliding the particle sideways changes its distance to the fixed attracting point, so the potential energy does change, breaking translation invariance.

A particle attracted to an infinite straight line of mass (say the $z$-axis): now energy, the $z$-component of momentum (sliding along the line doesn't matter), and the $z$-component of angular momentum (rotating about the line doesn't matter) are all conserved — but the $x$- and $y$-components of momentum and angular momentum are not, since the line singles out a direction in space. Each conserved quantity is traceable, via Noether's theorem, to one specific geometric symmetry of the physical setup — this is the general moral to take away from the whole section.

23The Hamilton–Jacobi Equation. Jacobi's Theorem

The last construction in this chapter takes a different shape than everything before it: instead of a system of ODEs (the canonical equations), it's a single first-order partial differential equation, and instead of describing one extremal at a time, it describes an entire family of them simultaneously.

Setup: the geodetic distance function. Consider the functional $J[y]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n')\,dx$ on some region $R$, and suppose $R$ has the property that exactly one extremal passes through any two given points $A,B$ in it (think of $R$ as small enough, or well-behaved enough, that extremals don't cross or bunch up — a field of extremals, in language the book develops further in a later chapter). Define
$$S = \int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n')\,dx, \tag{65}$$
evaluated along that extremal joining $A=(x_0,y_1^0,\dots,y_n^0)$ to $B=(x_1,y_1^1,\dots,y_n^1)$. Since exactly one extremal joins any pair of points, $S$ is a genuine, single-valued function of the coordinates of $A$ and $B$ — call it the geodetic distance between them (a term borrowed from the arc-length example below, but applying to any functional at all).

Three examples of what $S$ represents concretely, to keep the abstraction grounded: if $J$ computes arc length, $S$ is literally the ordinary distance between $A$ and $B$. If $J$ is the travel-time integral $T=\int \sqrt{\dot x^2+\dot y^2+\dot z^2}/v\,dt$ for light moving through a medium whose speed $v$ depends on position and direction, then by Fermat's principle (light follows the extremal, i.e. the fastest path) $S$ is the actual time light takes to travel from $A$ to $B$. And if $J$ is the action integral from Section 21, $S$ is the least-action value itself, evaluated along the true trajectory of the mechanical system between two configurations — this last case is the one Section 23 is ultimately building toward.

Deriving the governing PDE. Fix the initial point $A$ and let the final point $B=(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ vary; then $S=S(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$. To find the partial derivatives of $S$, consider a small displacement of $B$ to a nearby point $(x+dx,y_1+dy_1,\dots,y_n+dy_n)$, and let $\gamma$ be the extremal from $A$ to the original $B$, $\gamma^*$ the extremal from the (fixed) $A$ to the displaced point. Since $A$ is held fixed, the difference $\Delta S = J[\gamma^*]-J[\gamma]$ is exactly the general variation $\delta J$ from Chapter 3 (Section 13), evaluated with the left endpoint fixed and the right endpoint displaced by $dx,dy_i$:
$$dS(x,y_1,\dots,y_n) = \delta J = \sum_{i=1}^n p_i\,dy_i - H\,dx, \tag{69}$$
using formula (12) of Chapter 3, Section 13 (the canonical-variable form of the general variation), with $p_i,H$ evaluated at the endpoint $B$, along the extremal $\gamma$. Reading off the coefficients of this total differential exactly as in Sections 16 and 19,
$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial x} = -H, \qquad \frac{\partial S}{\partial y_i}=p_i, \tag{70}$$
where $p_i = F_{y_i'}[x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1'(x),\dots,y_n'(x)]$ and $H=H[x,y_1,\dots,y_n,p_1,\dots,p_n]$ — the values these functions take along the specific extremal $\gamma$ passing through $B$. Substituting the second equation of (70) into the first, so as to eliminate $p_i$ entirely in favor of $\partial S/\partial y_i$:
$$\boxed{\frac{\partial S}{\partial x} + H\left(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,\frac{\partial S}{\partial y_1},\dots,\frac{\partial S}{\partial y_n}\right) = 0.} \tag{72}$$

This is the Hamilton–Jacobi equation — a single, generally nonlinear, first-order PDE for the one unknown function $S(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$. It's worth sitting with how much has just been compressed: instead of $n$ coupled second-order ODEs (the original Euler equations), or $2n$ coupled first-order ODEs (the canonical equations), the entire family of extremals through a fixed starting point $A$ has been encoded in one PDE for one scalar function.

Theorem 1: solutions with parameters generate first integrals

Suppose $S=S(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m)$ is a solution of (72) depending on $m\ (\le n)$ extra parameters $\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_m$ (think of these as a family of solutions, one for each choice of the $\alpha$'s — not a single fixed function). Then each partial derivative $\partial S/\partial\alpha_i$, viewed as a function of $x,y_1,\dots,y_n$ (with the $\alpha$'s held at fixed values), is a first integral of the canonical Euler equations: $\partial S/\partial \alpha_i=\text{const}$ along every extremal.

Proof sketch. Differentiate (72) with respect to $\alpha_i$ (holding $x,y_j$ fixed), using the chain rule on the $H$-dependence through each $\partial S/\partial y_k$:
$$\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial x\,\partial\alpha_i} = -\sum_{k=1}^n\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial y_k\partial\alpha_i}. \tag{76}$$
Now compute $\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_i}\right)$ along an extremal $y_k=y_k(x)$, by the ordinary chain rule:
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_i}\right) = \frac{\partial^2S}{\partial x\partial\alpha_i} + \sum_{k=1}^n\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial y_k\partial\alpha_i}\frac{dy_k}{dx}.$$
Substitute (76) for the first term, and group:
$$\frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\partial S}{\partial \alpha_i}\right) = \sum_{k=1}^n\frac{\partial^2S}{\partial y_k\partial\alpha_i}\left(\frac{dy_k}{dx}-\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}\right).$$
But along any extremal, $\frac{dy_k}{dx}=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_k}$ is exactly the first canonical equation (11) — so every term in the sum vanishes, and $\frac{d}{dx}(\partial S/\partial \alpha_i)=0$ identically along extremals, which is exactly the claim. $\blacksquare$

Theorem 2 (Jacobi's theorem): from one complete solution, get every extremal

Take this one step further. Suppose $S=S(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n)$ is a complete integral of (72) — a solution depending on the maximum number, $n$, of independent parameters — with the nondegeneracy condition that the $n\times n$ matrix of mixed partials $\left\|\partial^2S/\partial \alpha_i\partial y_k\right\|$ has nonzero determinant (this is precisely the condition needed to invert the relations below). Let $\beta_1,\dots,\beta_n$ be $n$ further arbitrary constants. Then the system of $n$ algebraic equations
$$\frac{\partial S}{\partial\alpha_i}(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n) = \beta_i \qquad (i=1,\dots,n), \tag{80}$$
solved for $y_1,\dots,y_n$ in terms of $x$ and the $2n$ constants $\alpha_i,\beta_i$ (possible by the nondegeneracy condition, via the implicit function theorem), together with
$$p_i = \frac{\partial S}{\partial y_i}(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_n) \qquad (i=1,\dots,n), \tag{81}$$
constitutes the general solution of the canonical system (11) — i.e. every extremal, as the $2n$ constants $\alpha_i,\beta_i$ range over all their possible values.

This is a remarkable trade: solving one PDE with a "big enough" (n-parameter) family of solutions is exactly equivalent to solving the entire canonical ODE system, for every possible initial condition at once. (The book gives two proofs of this fact — one a direct differentiation check that (80)–(81) really do satisfy (11), essentially repeating the Theorem 1 argument twice; the other, arguably the more illuminating one, observes that a complete integral $S$ is itself the generating function of a canonical transformation (Section 19) into new variables $\alpha_i,\beta_i$ in which the transformed Hamiltonian $H^*=H+\partial S/\partial x$ vanishes identically, by the Hamilton–Jacobi equation itself — and the canonical equations for a system with $H^*\equiv0$ are trivially $d\alpha_i/dx=0,\ d\beta_i/dx=0$, i.e. every $\alpha_i,\beta_i$ is automatically constant. All the analytical difficulty of the original problem has been absorbed into finding the one function $S$.)

Chapter recap

This chapter took the canonical variables $p_i=F_{y_i'}$ and $H=-F+\sum y_i'p_i$ — introduced almost in passing at the end of Chapter 3 — and built an entire alternative universe around them. Section 16 showed the Euler equations become the strikingly symmetric canonical system $\dot y_i=\partial H/\partial p_i,\ \dot p_i=-\partial H/\partial y_i$. Section 17 introduced the Poisson bracket $[\Phi,H]$ as an algebraic test for first integrals, with $H$ itself always qualifying when there's no explicit $x$-dependence. Section 18 rederived the same canonical equations from a completely different direction — the Legendre transformation, a piece of convex duality that trades a function of $\xi$ for a conjugate function of $p=f'(\xi)$, and which turns out to be exactly the passage from "velocity language" to "momentum language" in disguise. Section 19 classified the transformations of $y_i,p_i$ that preserve canonical form, showing every one of them is stamped out of a single generating function. Section 20 proved Noether's theorem: any continuous symmetry of a functional hands you a first integral, by formula (50) or its canonical-variable form (53) — with the $H=\text{const}$ result from Section 17 falling out as the special case of translation-in-$x$ symmetry. Sections 21–22 cashed this all in on classical mechanics: the principle of least action showed Newton's second law is the Euler equation of the action integral $\int(T-U)\,dt$, and Noether's theorem then delivered conservation of energy, momentum, and angular momentum as the respective consequences of time-invariance, translation-invariance, and rotation-invariance of that same integral. Finally, Section 23 folded an entire family of extremals into a single object, the geodetic distance function $S$, governed by the first-order Hamilton–Jacobi PDE $\partial S/\partial x + H(x,y,\partial S/\partial y)=0$ — with Jacobi's theorem showing that one sufficiently rich ("complete") solution of this PDE is equivalent to solving the canonical system for every initial condition at once. Chapter 5 turns from these structural questions toward a different one: given a curve that satisfies the Euler equation, how do you actually tell whether it's a minimum, a maximum, or neither?

Practice problems
Problem 1

1. (Direct check on a familiar functional.) Use the canonical Euler equations to find the extremals of $J[y]=\int\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$, and verify they agree with those found in Chapter 1's practice problems.

Hint: The book gives the Hamiltonian as $H(x,y,p)=-\sqrt{x^2+y^2-p^2}$. Substitute into the canonical system (11) — $dp/dx = -\partial H/\partial y$ and $dy/dx=\partial H/\partial p$ — and show the resulting pair of equations has the first integral $p^2-y^2=C^2$ for a constant $C$ (differentiate this combination along solutions of your two canonical equations and check it vanishes, exactly the Section 17 first-integral technique). Then eliminate $p$ using $p=F_{y'}$ computed directly from the original $F$, and compare the resulting relation between $x,y,y'$ to whatever you found for this functional back in Chapter 1.

Problem 2

2. (Poisson brackets for the harmonic oscillator.) For the action functional of a simple harmonic oscillator, $J[x]=\frac12\int_{t_0}^{t_1}(m\dot x^2-\varkappa x^2)\,dt$, write the canonical system of Euler equations and interpret it physically. Compute the Poisson brackets $[x,p]$, $[x,H]$, and $[p,H]$. Is $p$ a first integral of the canonical equations?

Hint: First find $p=\partial L/\partial\dot x=m\dot x$ (ordinary momentum) and $H=\dot x p - L = p^2/(2m)+\frac12\varkappa x^2$ (kinetic plus potential energy, exactly as in Section 22). The canonical equations $\dot x = \partial H/\partial p = p/m$ and $\dot p=-\partial H/\partial x=-\varkappa x$ should look extremely familiar — combine them into a single second-order equation and check it's the standard oscillator equation $m\ddot x=-\varkappa x$. For the brackets, apply definition (13') directly with $n=1$: $[x,p]=\partial x/\partial x\cdot\partial p/\partial p - \partial x/\partial p\cdot \partial p/\partial x = 1\cdot1-0\cdot0=1$ (a fact worth remembering — $[x,p]=1$ is the classical shadow of the quantum-mechanical canonical commutation relation, for anyone who goes on to study quantum theory later). Compute $[x,H]$ and $[p,H]$ the same way, then use $[p,H]$ together with formula (13) to answer whether $p$ is conserved (it shouldn't be, physically — momentum oscillates back and forth as the mass swings — so the bracket had better come out nonzero).

Problem 3

3. (Setting up a central-force problem in polar coordinates.) Use the principle of least action to formulate the plane motion of a particle of mass $m$, attracted to the origin by a force inversely proportional to the square of its distance (i.e. an inverse-square law, like gravity), as a variational problem in polar coordinates $r,\theta$. Write the equations of motion, the Hamiltonian, and the canonical system. Compute the Poisson brackets $[r,p_r]$, $[\theta,p_\theta]$, $[p_r,H]$, and $[p_\theta,H]$, where $p_r=\partial L/\partial\dot r,\ p_\theta=\partial L/\partial\dot\theta$. Is $p_\theta$ a first integral?

Hint: The book gives the action functional as $J[r,\theta]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\left[\frac m2(\dot r^2+r^2\dot\theta^2)+\frac kr\right]dt$, where $k$ is a constant (the $\frac m2r^2\dot\theta^2$ term is exactly the kinetic energy contributed by the angular part of the motion — a fact worth deriving for yourself from $x=r\cos\theta,y=r\sin\theta$ if it isn't already familiar, by differentiating and computing $\dot x^2+\dot y^2$). From here, $p_r=m\dot r,\ p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta$, and you should find $[p_\theta,H]=0$ — i.e. $p_\theta$, the angular momentum, is conserved — regardless of the specific form of the radial force law, which foreshadows Problem 5 and the general angular-momentum discussion of Section 22.

Problem 4

4. (A specific canonical transformation.) Verify that the change of variables $Y_i=p_i,\ P_i=y_i$ (i.e. positions and momenta simply swap roles) is a canonical transformation, and find its generating function.

Hint: Use the second generating-function form (41), which relates $Y_i$ and $P_i$ to a function $\Psi(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,P_1,\dots,P_n)$ via $p_i=\partial\Psi/\partial y_i,\ Y_i=\partial\Psi/\partial P_i$. Since you want $Y_i=p_i$ and $P_i=y_i$ (so the new momenta are the old positions), try the simplest possible bilinear guess $\Psi=\sum_i y_iP_i$ and check it reproduces both required relations directly by differentiating.

Problem 5

5. (Noether's theorem in polar coordinates.) Verify that the functional $J[r,\theta]$ of Problem 3 is invariant under rotations (i.e. under $\theta^*=\theta+\varepsilon$, with $r$ unchanged), and use Noether's theorem to find the corresponding conservation law. What geometric fact about the particle's motion does this conservation law express?

Hint: Since the functional doesn't depend on $\theta$ at all (only on $\dot\theta$ and $r,\dot r$), invariance under a shift of $\theta$ is immediate — this is the polar-coordinate analogue of "$F$ doesn't depend on $x^{"}$ from Section 17/20's Example 3, with $\theta$ now playing the role of the "missing" variable. Apply formula (50) with $\varphi=0$ (time isn't shifted) and the $\theta$-component of $\psi$ equal to $1$ (the $r$-component is $0$, since $r$ isn't touched by the rotation) to conclude $p_\theta=mr^2\dot\theta=\text{const}$. The book's answer for the geometric meaning: this is exactly Kepler's second law — the line segment joining the particle to the origin sweeps out equal areas in equal times (a short computation with the formula for the area swept by a rotating radius, $dA=\frac12r^2\,d\theta$, converts $r^2\dot\theta=\text{const}$ directly into "area swept per unit time is constant").

Problem 6

6. (Solving a Hamilton–Jacobi equation from scratch.) Write and solve the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for $J[y]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}y'^2\,dx$, and use the result to determine the extremals of $J[y]$.

Hint: First find $H$: with $F=y'^2$, $p=F_{y'}=2y'$ so $y'=p/2$, and $H=-F+py'=-y'^2+2y'\cdot y' \cdot\frac12$... work this out directly from $H=-F+py'$ with $y'=p/2$ substituted in, to get $H=p^2/4$. The Hamilton–Jacobi equation (72) is then $\partial S/\partial x + \frac14(\partial S/\partial y)^2=0$ (the book states this as $4S_x+(S_y)^2=0$, an equivalent rescaling). Try a solution of the separated form $S=\alpha y + \beta(x)$ for a constant $\alpha$ — substitute in, solve the resulting ODE for $\beta(x)$, and then apply Theorem 1 (differentiate your complete integral with respect to the parameter $\alpha$ and set the result to a constant) to recover the extremals; you should land back on the family of straight lines you'd get from Euler's equation directly (since $F=y'^2$ has $F_y=0$, Euler's equation is just $y''=0$).

Problem 7

7. (A more general one-dimensional Hamilton–Jacobi equation.) Write and solve the Hamilton–Jacobi equation for $J[y]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}f(y)\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx$, and use the result to find the extremals.

Hint: Compute $p=F_{y'}=f(y)y'/\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ and work out $H$ in terms of $x,y,p$ (this takes a little algebra — solve for $y'$ in terms of $p$ first). The book's answer for the resulting PDE is $(\partial S/\partial x)^2+(\partial S/\partial y)^2=f^2(y)$, with complete integral $S=\alpha x+\int_{y_0}^y\sqrt{f^2(\eta)-\alpha^2}\,d\eta+\beta$. Differentiate with respect to $\alpha$ and set the result equal to a constant (Theorem 1 again) to arrive at the extremals in implicit form, $x-\alpha\int_{y_0}^y \frac{d\eta}{\sqrt{f^2(\eta)-\alpha^2}} = \text{const}$.

Problem 8

8. (Connecting back to Problem 1.) Use the Hamilton–Jacobi equation to find the extremals of the functional from Problem 1.

Hint: The book's suggested route is to guess a solution of the quadratic form $S=\frac12(Ax^2+2Bxy+Cy^2)$ for constants $A,B,C$ to be determined, substitute into the Hamilton–Jacobi equation built from the Hamiltonian $H=-\sqrt{x^2+y^2-p^2}$ from Problem 1, and match coefficients of $x^2,xy,y^2$ to pin down $A,B,C$ — a nice illustration of "guess a functional form suited to the problem's symmetry" as a general strategy for solving Hamilton–Jacobi equations by hand, which is otherwise a hard PDE to attack head-on.
