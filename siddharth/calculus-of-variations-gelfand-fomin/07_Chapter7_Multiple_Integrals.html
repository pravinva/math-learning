00Roadmap: from curves to surfaces

Every functional in Chapters 1 through 6 ate a single function of a single variable, $y(x)$, and produced a number by integrating along an interval. This chapter asks the obvious next question: what if the unknown is a function of several variables, $u(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, and the functional integrates over a whole region — a patch of the plane, a chunk of space — rather than an interval?

This is not a cosmetic generalization. It's the difference between studying the shape of a stretched rope and studying the shape of a stretched drumhead, or between studying a single particle's trajectory and studying the vibrations rippling through an entire elastic solid. The unknown function now has a graph that's a surface (or a hypersurface), and "the slope" splits into a whole vector of partial derivatives. Here's the itinerary:

  • Section 35 sets up the basic problem — minimize $J[u]=\iint_D F(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy$ over functions $u$ with prescribed boundary values on $\partial D$ — and derives the multivariable Euler equation. The one new tool required is a primer on the divergence theorem, the direct generalization of integration by parts to multiple dimensions.
  • Section 36 puts this equation to work deriving the actual equations of motion of continuous mechanical systems straight from a variational principle: the vibrating string, the vibrating membrane, and (more briefly) the vibrating plate. Along the way, a subtlety about least action versus merely stationary action gets resolved, tying directly back to Chapter 5's conjugate points.
  • Section 37 generalizes Chapter 3's "general variation" machinery — where the endpoints themselves were allowed to move — to the case where the whole region $D$ is allowed to deform, producing a general variation formula and a multivariable version of Noether's theorem.
  • Section 38 cashes all of this in on field theory: treating the fundamental fields of physics (including the electromagnetic field) as functions extremizing an action integral, and reading off conservation of energy, momentum, and angular momentum as direct consequences of Noether's theorem.

By the end, you'll see the calculus of variations not just as a tool for finding shortest paths and brachistochrones, but as the organizing principle behind field theory in physics — the single idea from which Maxwell's equations, and the conservation laws that go with them, fall out almost automatically.

35Variation of a Functional Defined on a Fixed Region
Background primer

A primer: partial derivatives, double integrals, and the divergence theorem (what you need, built from scratch)

You know single-variable derivatives and single-variable integrals cold. Here's everything else this chapter needs, with no shortcuts skipped.

Partial derivatives. If $u(x,y)$ is a function of two variables, $u_x=\partial u/\partial x$ means: freeze $y$, differentiate with respect to $x$ as if $y$ were a constant. Likewise $u_y$ freezes $x$. Geometrically, $u(x,y)$ is a surface sitting over the $xy$-plane, and $u_x,u_y$ are the slopes of that surface in the $x$- and $y$-directions respectively at a given point. Everything about ordinary differentiation — product rule, chain rule, and so on — applies to each partial derivative separately, since you're just doing ordinary one-variable calculus with the other variable held fixed.

Double integrals. $\iint_D f(x,y)\,dx\,dy$ is the natural two-dimensional generalization of $\int_a^b f(x)\,dx$: chop the region $D$ into tiny rectangles of area $dx\,dy$, multiply each by the value of $f$ there, and sum (in the limit, integrate). If $f\ge0$, this computes the volume under the surface $z=f(x,y)$ and above $D$. In practice you'll almost always evaluate one by iterating — integrating with respect to one variable while holding the other fixed, then integrating the result with respect to the second variable — which is really just doing two ordinary one-variable integrals in sequence.

The one-dimensional trick you're about to outgrow. Recall how Chapter 1 derived Euler's equation: you write $J[y+\varepsilon h]-J[y]$, expand $F$ in a Taylor series, and end up needing to move a derivative off of $h'$ and onto $h$ inside an integral — which you do via integration by parts, $\int_a^bF_{y'}h'\,dx = \bigl[F_{y'}h\bigr]_a^b-\int_a^b\frac{d}{dx}(F_{y'})\,h\,dx$, and the boundary term vanishes because $h(a)=h(b)=0$. The whole multivariable machinery of this chapter is built by finding the right replacement for that one manipulation when there are two (or more) independent variables instead of one.

The divergence theorem (Green's theorem, the version you need). Suppose $P(x,y)$ and $Q(x,y)$ are continuously differentiable on a region $D$ with a nice (piecewise-smooth) boundary curve $\partial D$. Green's theorem in the plane says
$$\iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy = \oint_{\partial D}(P\,dx+Q\,dy),$$
with the boundary traversed counterclockwise. This is already something you may have seen, but here's the form that actually generalizes integration by parts. Write the boundary curve parametrically with arclength $s$, so a tangent vector is $(dx/ds,dy/ds)$, and the outward unit normal is the tangent rotated a quarter turn: $n=(n_1,n_2)=(dy/ds,-dx/ds)$ (check this points outward by trying it on a circle traversed counterclockwise — the normal should point away from the center). Then $dx=-n_2\,ds$ and $dy=n_1\,ds$, so Green's theorem becomes
$$\iint_D\left(\frac{\partial Q}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial P}{\partial y}\right)dx\,dy = \oint_{\partial D}(Qn_1-Pn_2)\,ds.$$
Now specialize: set $Q=f$ and $P=0$. You get $\iint_D f_x\,dx\,dy=\oint_{\partial D}fn_1\,ds$. Set $Q=0$ and $P=-f$ instead, and you get $\iint_D f_y\,dx\,dy=\oint_{\partial D}fn_2\,ds$. Combined, for any continuously differentiable $f$ on $D$ and $i=1,2$ (writing $x_1=x,x_2=y$):
$$\iint_D\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_i}\,dx\,dy = \oint_{\partial D}f\,n_i\,ds. \tag{$\ast$}$$
This is exactly the multivariable replacement for integration by parts: it lets you turn an integral of a partial derivative over a region into a boundary integral, with no derivative left inside — precisely the move that finished off the single-variable computation, now available in two (and, by the identical argument with one more coordinate, $n$) dimensions. The same identity holds with $x,y$ replaced by $x_1,\dots,x_n$ and double integrals replaced by $n$-fold integrals over a region in $n$-dimensional space, with $\partial D$ now an $(n-1)$-dimensional hypersurface and $ds$ its natural surface measure — the book states this general version as its formula (5), and everything below works for $n$ variables just as it does for 2; the two-variable case is used for concreteness since it is what every worked example needs.

Setting up the problem

Consider a functional of the form
$$J[u] = \iint_D F(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy, \tag{1}$$
where $D$ is a fixed region in the $xy$-plane, $u=u(x,y)$ ranges over functions taking prescribed values on the boundary curve $\partial D$, and $F$ is a given function of its five arguments (assumed as differentiable as needed). This is the direct two-variable analogue of $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$: one independent variable has become two, and the single derivative $y'$ has become the pair $(u_x,u_y)$.

As with the classical problem, look for a weak extremum: compare $u$ to nearby functions $u+\eta$, where $\eta$ vanishes on $\partial D$ (so the comparison function still meets the prescribed boundary values) and both $\eta$ and its first partials are small. Exactly as in Chapter 1, form
$$\Delta J = J[u+\eta]-J[u] = \iint_D\bigl[F(x,y,u+\eta,u_x+\eta_x,u_y+\eta_y)-F(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\bigr]dx\,dy,$$
Taylor-expand the bracket to first order in $\eta,\eta_x,\eta_y$, and read off the linear part as the variation:
$$\delta J = \iint_D\bigl(F_u\,\eta+F_{u_x}\,\eta_x+F_{u_y}\,\eta_y\bigr)\,dx\,dy. \tag{2}$$
A necessary condition for an extremum, exactly as before, is $\delta J=0$ for every admissible $\eta$.

The Euler equation for multiple integrals

The two terms $F_{u_x}\eta_x$ and $F_{u_y}\eta_y$ in (2) are the direct analogues of the single-variable $F_{y'}h'$, and they get handled the same way: move the derivative off of $\eta$ using $(\ast)$. Apply the product rule in reverse,
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(F_{u_x}\,\eta) = \frac{\partial F_{u_x}}{\partial x}\,\eta + F_{u_x}\,\eta_x \quad\Longrightarrow\quad F_{u_x}\eta_x = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}(F_{u_x}\eta)-\frac{\partial F_{u_x}}{\partial x}\,\eta,$$
and likewise for the $y$-term. Substituting into (2) and integrating the two total-derivative terms via $(\ast)$ (with $f=F_{u_x}\eta$ and $f=F_{u_y}\eta$ respectively):
$$\iint_D\left[\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(F_{u_x}\eta)+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(F_{u_y}\eta)\right]dx\,dy = \oint_{\partial D}\bigl(F_{u_x}n_1+F_{u_y}n_2\bigr)\eta\,ds = 0,$$
where the last equality holds because $\eta\equiv0$ on $\partial D$ (the comparison function matches the boundary data). So the whole boundary contribution vanishes, exactly as $[F_{y'}h]_a^b$ vanished before — for exactly the same reason: $\eta$ is pinned to zero right where the boundary term would otherwise live. What survives is
$$\delta J = \iint_D\left(F_u-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{u_x}-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}F_{u_y}\right)\eta\,dx\,dy = 0$$
for every admissible $\eta$. This is a two-dimensional functional analogue of the fundamental lemma from Chapter 1 (an arbitrary continuous $\eta$ paired against a continuous coefficient, integrated to zero, forces the coefficient itself to vanish — the same proof by contradiction goes through unchanged, just with a small disk in $D$ playing the role of the small subinterval $[x_0-\delta,x_0+\delta]$), so the extremal $u$ must satisfy the Euler equation for double integrals:
$$F_u - \frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{u_x} - \frac{\partial}{\partial y}F_{u_y} = 0. \tag{4}$$

This is a second-order partial differential equation in $u(x,y)$ — a new species of object compared to the ordinary differential equations Euler's equation produced in one variable, and this is the central character of this whole chapter. The same derivation, run with $n$ independent variables $x_1,\dots,x_n$ and $F=F(x_1,\dots,x_n,u,u_{x_1},\dots,u_{x_n})$, gives the general form
$$F_u - \sum_{i=1}^n\frac{\partial}{\partial x_i}F_{u_{x_i}} = 0, \tag{4'}$$
the book's Euler equation for multiple integrals in full generality. Every other equation in this chapter is either this equation in disguise, or a system of several copies of it (one per unknown function, exactly as in Chapter 2's generalization to several unknown functions of one variable).

Worked example: the Dirichlet integral and Laplace's equation

Take $F=u_x^2+u_y^2$, so $J[u]=\iint_D(u_x^2+u_y^2)\,dx\,dy$ — the Dirichlet integral, a natural measure of how "steep," on average, the surface $u(x,y)$ is. Here $F_u=0$, $F_{u_x}=2u_x$, $F_{u_y}=2u_y$, so (4) reads
$$-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(2u_x)-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}(2u_y)=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad u_{xx}+u_{yy}=0.$$
This is Laplace's equation, and its solutions are the harmonic functions — among the most important functions in all of mathematical physics (steady-state temperature distributions, electrostatic potentials, and incompressible fluid flows are all harmonic). The fact that harmonic functions extremize the Dirichlet integral is itself a classical and useful characterization, called the Dirichlet principle.

36Variational Derivation of the Equations of Motion of Continuous Mechanical Systems

Chapter 4, Section 21 introduced Hamilton's principle of least action for systems of finitely many particles: the true motion extremizes $\int(T-U)\,dt$ among all nearby motions with the same endpoints in time. The real power of that principle shows up once you push it to systems with infinitely many degrees of freedom — a stretched string, say, whose configuration at each instant isn't a finite list of coordinates but an entire function $u(x,t)$ giving the displacement at every point $x$ along its length. This section derives the equations governing several such systems, straight from the Euler equation (4) applied with $t$ playing the role of one independent variable and position playing the role of the other.

36.1 The vibrating string

Background primer

A primer: kinetic energy, potential energy, and small-oscillation physics

Kinetic energy of a moving mass is $\frac12mv^2$; for a continuum, you add up (integrate) this quantity over every little piece of mass, using its local velocity. Potential energy stores the work needed to deform a system away from equilibrium (compare Chapter 4's primer on this same pair of ideas, now applied to a continuum rather than a point mass). "Small oscillations" means displacements and slopes small enough that only their lowest-order (usually quadratic) contributions to energy matter — exactly the regime where a taut string's restoring force is well-approximated by a simple, linear model.

Picture a string of length $l$, stretched along the $x$-axis under tension $T_0$, free to vibrate transversally: at time $t$, the point of the string originally at position $x$ has been displaced to height $u(x,t)$. For small transverse displacements, the string's kinetic energy (mass per unit length $\rho(x)$, velocity $u_t$) is
$$\text{KE} = \frac12\int_0^l\rho(x)\,u_t^2\,dx,$$
and its potential energy — the work done stretching it against tension $T_0$ — works out, for small slopes $u_x$, to
$$\text{PE} = \frac12\int_0^lT_0(x)\,u_x^2\,dx$$
(the arc-length element $\sqrt{1+u_x^2}\,dx\approx(1+\tfrac12u_x^2)\,dx$ for small $u_x$, so the extra length created by the displacement, and hence the work done against tension, is quadratic in the slope — this is the same small-angle/small-slope approximation that turns a pendulum's exact restoring force into the linear one used for simple harmonic motion). Hamilton's principle says the true motion extremizes the action
$$J[u] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}(\text{KE}-\text{PE})\,dt = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\int_0^l\frac12\Bigl(\rho\,u_t^2-T_0\,u_x^2\Bigr)dx\,dt,$$
which is precisely a double integral of the form (1) — with the two independent variables being $x$ and $t$ rather than $x$ and $y$, and $F=\frac12(\rho u_t^2-T_0u_x^2)$. Applying the Euler equation (4) (with $x_1=x,x_2=t$):
$$F_u-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{u_x}-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}F_{u_t}=0 \quad\Longrightarrow\quad 0-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-T_0u_x)-\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\rho u_t)=0,$$
i.e.
$$\rho(x)\,u_{tt} = \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\bigl(T_0(x)\,u_x\bigr). \tag{9}$$
For a uniform string ($\rho,T_0$ constant), this is the classical wave equation $u_{tt}=a^2u_{xx}$ with $a^2=T_0/\rho$ — derived here not by an ad hoc force-balance argument on a tiny string element (the usual introductory-physics route) but as an Euler equation, straight out of a single energy principle.

Boundary conditions, three ways. The derivation of (9) used $\eta=0$ on the entire boundary of the region (both ends $x=0,l$ and both times $t_0,t_1$) to kill the boundary term. But suppose one end, say $x=l$, is not held fixed — instead it's free to move, perhaps attached to an elastic support. Then $\eta(l,t)$ is no longer forced to vanish, and the boundary-term computation (redone carefully, tracking exactly which piece of the boundary integral survives) forces an extra condition there, alongside (9), for $\delta J=0$ to hold: this is exactly the movable-endpoint story from Chapter 3, Section 14, now playing out along a whole edge of the region instead of at a single point. Three cases, in increasing order of restriction:

  • Fixed end: $u(l,t)=0$ is prescribed outright, so $\eta(l,t)=0$ automatically, and no extra condition arises — the boundary term is killed by hand, the same way it always was for fixed endpoints.
  • Free end: nothing at all is prescribed about $u(l,t)$, so $\eta(l,t)$ is unrestricted, and the boundary term can only vanish if its own coefficient vanishes: $T_0u_x\big|_{x=l}=0$, i.e. the string's slope must be zero at a truly free end (physically: no transverse component of tension is available to pull on whatever the end is attached to, if nothing is attached).
  • Elastically attached end: the end is attached to a spring of stiffness $k$, contributing extra potential energy $\frac12k\,u(l,t)^2$ to the functional. Redoing the boundary-term bookkeeping with this extra term produces the condition $T_0u_x\big|_{x=l}=-k\,u(l,t)$ — a compromise between the two extremes above, reducing to the free-end condition as $k\to0$ and effectively pinning $u(l,t)=0$ as $k\to\infty$.

This three-way menu — fixed, free, and elastic — recurs for every continuous system in this section, and it's exactly the multivariable incarnation of the transversality conditions from Chapter 3.

36.2 Least action versus stationary action

Hamilton's principle is often stated as a principle of least action, but Euler's equation only ever certifies a stationary point of the action — extremal, not necessarily minimal. The gap between these two claims is worth making concrete, because the resolution ties directly back to Chapter 5's theory of conjugate points.

Take the simplest possible example: a single particle of mass $m$ on a spring of stiffness $k$ (simple harmonic motion), with action $J[x]=\int_0^T\frac12(m\dot x^2-kx^2)\,dt$ and $x(0)=x(T)=0$. The Euler equation gives $m\ddot x=-kx$, the familiar equation of motion, with general solution $x(t)=A\sin(\omega t+\phi)$, $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$. Compute the second variation directly (as in Chapter 5, Section 24) for a perturbation $\eta$ with $\eta(0)=\eta(T)=0$:
$$\delta^2J = \int_0^T(m\dot\eta^2-k\eta^2)\,dt.$$
Whether this is positive for every admissible $\eta$ — which is exactly what "genuine minimum" requires — turns out to hinge entirely on the length of the time interval $T$ relative to the natural period $2\pi/\omega$. Specifically (this is worked out via the Jacobi equation of Chapter 5, Section 27, applied to this quadratic functional), the point $t=0$ has a conjugate point at $t=\pi/\omega$ — half a period later. So:

  • If $T<\pi/\omega$ (less than half a period), there's no conjugate point in $(0,T)$, Jacobi's condition holds, and $x\equiv0$'s extremal is a minimum of the action.
  • If $T>\pi/\omega$, a conjugate point sits inside the interval, Jacobi's necessary condition fails, and $x\equiv0$ is merely a stationary point — not a minimum at all (there exist nearby paths, oscillating around zero, with strictly smaller action).

This is a strikingly concrete illustration of exactly the abstract machinery Chapter 5 built: the oscillator literally cannot sustain a true minimum of its own action once you watch it for longer than half a period, and the cutoff is precisely the conjugate point. It's also a useful corrective to the physics-textbook habit of saying "nature minimizes the action" without qualification — nature, more precisely, always finds a stationary point, and whether that stationary point happens to be a minimum is a separate, quantitative question with a sharp answer.

Coupled oscillators and normal coordinates (a brief remark). The same story extends to systems of several coupled oscillators (masses connected by springs to each other, not just to fixed walls): the Euler equations become a system of coupled linear ODEs, exactly Chapter 2's setting. A standard linear-algebra move — diagonalizing the quadratic form built from the kinetic and potential energies simultaneously — decouples the system into normal coordinates, each obeying its own independent simple-harmonic equation with its own frequency. The vibrating string, membrane, and plate below are the continuum limit of this same idea: infinitely many coupled oscillators (one "mass" per point of the string), whose normal modes become the familiar sine-wave standing-wave patterns of a vibrating string or drumhead.

36.3 The vibrating membrane

Now go up one dimension: a membrane occupying a region $D$ of the $xy$-plane at rest, displaced transversally to height $u(x,y,t)$. By the same reasoning as the string — kinetic energy $\frac12\iint_D\rho\,u_t^2\,dx\,dy$, potential energy $\frac12\iint_DT_0(u_x^2+u_y^2)\,dx\,dy$ (tension $T_0$ per unit length of any cut through the membrane, and the small-slope approximation to the extra surface area exactly mirrors the small-slope approximation to extra arc length used for the string) — the action is
$$J[u]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\!\!\iint_D\frac12\Bigl(\rho\,u_t^2-T_0(u_x^2+u_y^2)\Bigr)dx\,dy\,dt,$$
now a triple integral over the three independent variables $x,y,t$. The Euler equation for multiple integrals (4'), applied with three independent variables, gives
$$\rho\,u_{tt} = T_0(u_{xx}+u_{yy}) = T_0\nabla^2u,$$
the two-dimensional wave equation, where $\nabla^2u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}$ is the (spatial) Laplacian of $u$ — the same combination of second partials that showed up governing harmonic functions in Section 35's Dirichlet-integral example, here appearing as the spatial part of a wave equation instead. The same fixed/free/elastic menu of boundary conditions along the edge $\partial D$ carries over unchanged in spirit — fixed edge: $u=0$ on $\partial D$; free edge: the outward normal derivative $\partial u/\partial n=0$ on $\partial D$ (the direct generalization of $u_x=0$ at a free end of a string); elastically supported edge: a linear relation between $u$ and $\partial u/\partial n$ on $\partial D$.

36.4 The vibrating plate (a brief look)

A rigid, thin plate resists not just stretching but bending — so its potential energy depends on second derivatives of $u$ (curvature), not just first derivatives, and its variational functional is
$$F = \frac12\rho u_t^2 - \frac{D_0}{2}\Bigl[(\nabla^2u)^2 - 2(1-\mu)(u_{xx}u_{yy}-u_{xy}^2)\Bigr],$$
where $D_0$ is a flexural rigidity constant and $\mu$ is Poisson's ratio (a material constant, between $0$ and $\frac12$ for ordinary materials, measuring how much a material contracts sideways when stretched lengthwise). Because $F$ now depends on second derivatives $u_{xx},u_{xy},u_{yy}$, the Euler equation needs an extra round of the same integration-by-parts idea applied twice, producing a fourth-order equation
$$\rho\,u_{tt} = -D_0\nabla^4u, \qquad \nabla^4u := \nabla^2(\nabla^2u) = u_{xxxx}+2u_{xxyy}+u_{yyyy},$$
the biharmonic equation, governing the plate's vibration. Its boundary conditions involve two physically meaningful combinations of second and third derivatives called the bending moment $M$ and shearing force $P$ along the edge; a free edge requires both to vanish, a clamped edge fixes $u$ and $\partial u/\partial n$, and so on. The details are a genuine step up in bookkeeping from the membrane, and the book itself treats this case briefly — it's included here mainly so you recognize the pattern: however many derivatives $F$ depends on, the same "integrate by parts until every derivative is off of $\eta$" strategy produces an Euler equation, just of correspondingly higher order.

37Variation of a Functional Defined on a Variable Region

Section 35 held the region $D$ fixed and only asked how $J$ responds to changing $u$. Chapter 3's whole "general variation" program, recall, was about going further: letting the domain itself move (there, the endpoints $a,b$; here, the boundary curve $\partial D$). This section runs that program for multiple integrals.

37.1 Two kinds of change happening at once

Picture the region $D$ deforming into a nearby region $\bar D$, and simultaneously the function $u$ defined on $D$ deforming into a nearby function $\bar u$ defined on $\bar D$ — both changes controlled by the same small parameter. There are now two related, but distinctly different, notions of "how much $u$ changed" at a given point, and keeping them straight is the entire technical crux of this section (this is the multivariable payoff of a distinction Chapter 3 first introduced for movable endpoints):

  • $\Delta u$, the total increment: compare the old function's value at a point $M=(x,y)$ to the new function's value at the corresponding point $\bar M$ in the deformed region (corresponding, for instance, under the natural point-to-point map carrying $D$ to $\bar D$ that the deformation induces) — $\Delta u = \bar u(\bar M)-u(M)$. This bakes in both the change in the function itself and the fact that the point moved.
  • $\delta u$, the variation: compare the old and new functions at the same point $M$ — $\delta u = \bar u(M)-u(M)$ — exactly Chapter 1's original notion of variation, holding the point fixed and only asking how the function's value there changed.

These are related by a first-order formula worth deriving explicitly, since it's used constantly below. If the point $M=(x,y)$ moves to $\bar M=(x+\delta x,y+\delta y)$ under the deformation, then to first order,
$$\Delta u = \bar u(\bar M)-u(M) = \bigl[\bar u(M)-u(M)\bigr]+\bigl[\bar u(\bar M)-\bar u(M)\bigr] \approx \delta u + u_x\,\delta x+u_y\,\delta y, \tag{$\dagger$}$$
where the last approximation replaces $\bar u(\bar M)-\bar u(M)$ by its linear (first-order) approximation using $\bar u$'s partial derivatives, which — to the order being kept — can be taken to be $u$'s own partials. So $\Delta u\approx\delta u+u_x\delta x+u_y\delta y$: the total change splits into "the function itself changed" ($\delta u$) plus "the point moved, and the function has some slope there" (the remaining terms) — precisely analogous to the single-variable relation $\Delta y(x_1)=\delta y+y'(x)\,\delta x$ that did the same job in Chapter 3, Section 14, now with one extra term for the extra independent variable.

A worked example: rigid rotation. To get a feel for these increments concretely, take the simplest possible deformation: rotate the whole picture (both the region and the graph of $u$, rigidly) through a small angle $\alpha$. Under $x\to x-\alpha y,\ y\to y+\alpha x$ (a small rotation, to first order in $\alpha$) applied to the independent variables only, with $u$ itself unchanged as a function of position ($\bar u(\bar x,\bar y)=u(x,y)$ literally the same graph, just relabeled), a short computation using ($\dagger$) with $\delta x=-\alpha y,\ \delta y=\alpha x$ gives $\delta u = -\alpha(xu_y-yu_x)$ — the variation induced purely by relabeling coordinates under a rotation, with no change to the function's "shape" at all. This kind of computation is exactly the raw material Noether's theorem, in Section 37.4 below, turns into a conservation law.

37.2 Setting up the general variation of the functional

Consider $J[u]=\iint_DF(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy$ once more, but now let $D$ deform to $\bar D$ and $u$ deform to $\bar u$ simultaneously, and compute
$$\Delta J = \iint_{\bar D}F(\bar x,\bar y,\bar u,\bar u_{\bar x},\bar u_{\bar y})\,d\bar x\,d\bar y - \iint_DF(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy.$$
Split this into two pieces, exactly mirroring Chapter 3's split of $\Delta J$ into "change in the integrand over the fixed part" plus "extra area swept out at the boundary": (i) the change coming from $F$ itself changing (integrated over the original, common region $D$), which is exactly Section 35's $\delta J$ from formula (2); plus (ii) the change coming from the region growing or shrinking by a thin sliver along $\partial D$. That sliver, at a boundary point moving outward by a normal distance $\delta n$, contributes an extra strip of area $\delta n\,ds$, so its contribution to $\Delta J$ is $F\,\delta n$ integrated around the boundary — the two-dimensional analogue of Chapter 3's $F\big|_{x=b}\delta b$ boundary term, now smeared around an entire curve instead of concentrated at two endpoints.

37.3 Reducing the boundary term to a variation, and back to the divergence theorem

Just as Chapter 3 rewrote the endpoint term $F\,\delta b$ in terms of $\delta y$ using ($\dagger$)'s one-variable ancestor, here the plan is to rewrite the boundary integral of $F\,\delta n$ using $(\ast)$ and $(\dagger)$, so that everything is expressed through the single, uniform-looking quantity $\delta u$ (rather than mixing $\delta u$ with the geometric data of how the boundary itself is moving). After carrying this out — using $(\ast)$ in reverse to convert the surviving boundary integral back into an area integral, exactly undoing the move Section 35 made — the whole computation collapses onto a clean and memorable statement:

Theorem 1 (the general variation formula)

For $J[u]=\iint_DF(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy$ undergoing a simultaneous variation of the region $D\to\bar D$ and the function $u\to\bar u$, the first-order change in $J$ is
$$\Delta J \approx \iint_D\left(F_u-\frac{\partial}{\partial x}F_{u_x}-\frac{\partial}{\partial y}F_{u_y}\right)\delta u\,dx\,dy + \oint_{\partial D}\Bigl(F_{u_x}\,n_1+F_{u_y}\,n_2\Bigr)\delta u\,ds + \oint_{\partial D}F\,\delta n\,ds. \tag{95}$$

Read this the way you should read the analogous single-variable formula from Chapter 3: the first term is the Euler expression, exactly as before, and it vanishes identically once $u$ is chosen to be an extremal — leaving only the two boundary integrals to account for whatever happens at $\partial D$. If $u$ is an extremal, then, $\delta J=0$ reduces entirely to a statement about the boundary:
$$\delta J = \oint_{\partial D}\Bigl(F_{u_x}n_1+F_{u_y}n_2\Bigr)\delta u\,ds + \oint_{\partial D}F\,\delta n\,ds = 0. \tag{$\ast\ast$}$$
This is the exact multivariable generalization of Chapter 3's transversality condition: (95) is the formula from which every fixed/free/elastic boundary condition in Section 36 could equally well have been read off, and it's the formula from which every conservation law in Section 38 is about to be read off as well.

37.4 Noether's theorem for multiple integrals

Chapter 4, Section 20 showed that if an action integral is left unchanged by a family of transformations (of both the independent and dependent variables), then some quantity built from the Lagrangian is conserved along every extremal — a conservation law extracted from a symmetry, with no need to solve the equations of motion at all. Formula (95) is exactly the tool needed to run that same argument for multiple integrals.

Theorem 2 (Noether's theorem, multiple-integral form)

Suppose the functional $J[u]=\iint_DF(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy$ is invariant — meaning $\Delta J=0$ for every subregion $D$ — under a family of transformations of $x,y,u$ depending on a parameter $\alpha$ (with $\alpha=0$ the identity transformation). Then, along every extremal $u$,
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\Bigl(F_{u_x}\,\delta u + (F-u_xF_{u_x}-u_yF_{u_y})\,\delta x - u_yF_{u_y}\,\delta y \Bigr)\Big|_{\alpha=0} + \frac{\partial}{\partial y}\bigl(\cdots\bigr)\Big|_{\alpha=0} = 0,$$
i.e., some explicit divergence built from the transformation's infinitesimal generators vanishes identically.

The proof is the direct multivariable echo of Chapter 4, Section 20's argument: invariance forces $\Delta J\equiv0$ for the given family, for every choice of subregion $D$; formula (95)'s Euler term already vanishes on an extremal, so what remains — the two boundary integrals in ($\ast\ast$) — must vanish for every choice of boundary curve $\partial D$, which (running the fundamental-lemma argument once more, now on the boundary integral) forces the integrand itself to be an exact divergence, identically zero. The upshot in every application below is the same recipe as Chapter 4: identify a symmetry, write down its infinitesimal generators $\delta x,\delta y,\delta u$, plug into the formula, and read off a divergence that vanishes along every solution — which, by the divergence theorem $(\ast)$ run backward, is exactly a statement that some flux integrated around any closed surface is zero, the precise mathematical shape every conservation law in physics takes.

A remark on gauge transformations. One subtlety worth flagging before Section 38: sometimes a transformation changes $F$ itself (not just $u,x,y$), for instance by adding an exact divergence, $F\to F+\frac{\partial}{\partial x}G_1+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}G_2$ for some functions $G_1,G_2$. Such a change leaves $J$'s extremals completely untouched — because $\iint_D(\partial_xG_1+\partial_yG_2)\,dx\,dy$ converts via $(\ast)$ into a boundary integral depending only on the boundary values of $G_1,G_2$, contributing nothing to the Euler equation itself — even though it can change the specific numerical value of conserved quantities computed from Noether's theorem. This freedom to add an exact divergence to $F$ without changing the physics is called a gauge transformation, and it reappears, playing a starring role, in Section 38.5's treatment of electromagnetism.

38Applications to Field Theory

This section is the payoff the whole chapter has been building toward: treating the basic fields of physics — not particle trajectories, but functions defined at every point of space and time — as extremals of a single action integral, and reading off the conservation laws of energy, momentum, and angular momentum as direct instances of Noether's theorem.

Background primer

A primer: index notation and the summation convention (what you need, briefly)

Physics involving several coordinates gets unreadable fast without a shorthand. Write $x^1,x^2,x^3,x^4$ for the four spacetime coordinates (three space, one time — the superscript is a label, not an exponent, a notational choice worth committing to memory before confusion sets in), and adopt the Einstein summation convention: whenever an index (like $i$ or $k$) is repeated once as a subscript and once as a superscript in the same term, a sum over that index from $1$ to $4$ (or $1$ to $n$, in general) is implied automatically, with the $\Sigma$ symbol suppressed. So $T^i_{\ i}$ means $\sum_{i=1}^4T^i_i$, and an equation like $\partial_iT^{ik}=0$ (with $\partial_i$ short for $\partial/\partial x^i$) is shorthand for $\sum_i\partial T^{ik}/\partial x^i=0$, one equation for each value of $k$ — i.e. really four separate equations bundled into one compact statement.

A tensor is, for present purposes, simply an indexed array of functions ($T^{ik}$ has two indices, so it's a $4\times4$ array at each point of spacetime) that transforms in a specific, prescribed way under a change of coordinates — the same spirit as a vector being an array of components that transforms in the way vectors do, just with more indices and a correspondingly more elaborate transformation rule. You won't need the general transformation law here; you only need to recognize $T^{ik}$ and $M^{ikl}$ as bookkeeping devices — a way to organize several related conserved quantities (energy, momentum, angular momentum) into a single indexed object, so that one compact equation like $\partial_iT^{ik}=0$ packages several separate conservation statements at once.

38.1 The Lagrangian density and the principle of stationary action for fields

A field is a function $u(x^1,\dots,x^4)$ (or several such functions $u^{(1)},\dots,u^{(m)}$) defined throughout a region of spacetime, generalizing the discrete list of coordinates $q_1,\dots,q_n$ used for finitely many particles in Chapter 4. The action is
$$J[u] = \iiiint_D\mathcal L\bigl(x,u,u_{x^1},\dots,u_{x^4}\bigr)\,dx^1dx^2dx^3dx^4,$$
where $\mathcal L$ (a function of position, the field, and its four first partials) is called the Lagrangian density — "density" because integrating it over a spacetime region gives the actual Lagrangian-type quantity, exactly the way ordinary mass density integrates over a volume to give total mass. The principle of stationary action for fields says: the field configurations actually realized in nature are exactly the extremals of $J$ — i.e., exactly the solutions of the Euler equation (4') applied to $\mathcal L$:
$$\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial u}-\sum_{i=1}^4\frac{\partial}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial u_{x^i}}=0.$$
This single principle — choose the right $\mathcal L$, extremize — is the organizing idea behind essentially every fundamental field theory in physics; Sections 38.3–38.5 work out what it delivers for mechanical continua and, in the chapter's capstone example, for the electromagnetic field itself.

38.2 Conservation laws for fields

Applying Noether's theorem (Section 37.4) to field theory is a direct substitution — treat the four spacetime coordinates as the "$x,y$" of Theorem 2 (now four independent variables instead of two, with formula (95) and its consequences carrying over with a sum over all four coordinates in place of the two terms seen there) and the field $u$ as the dependent variable. Whatever continuous symmetry the Lagrangian density $\mathcal L$ possesses — translation in space, translation in time, rotation, or (for relativistic fields) a Lorentz transformation — produces, via Noether's theorem, a divergence-free tensor, and hence, via the divergence theorem run backward, a genuine conserved quantity. The next three subsections work through exactly this recipe for the three symmetries that matter most in classical field theory.

38.3 The energy-momentum tensor

Suppose $\mathcal L$ doesn't depend explicitly on the spacetime coordinates $x^i$ — only on $u$ and its derivatives — which is exactly the statement that the physics described is the same everywhere and at every time (translation invariance, the field-theoretic analogue of Chapter 4, Section 22's observation that a Lagrangian independent of time conserves energy, and independent of a coordinate conserves the corresponding momentum). Apply Noether's theorem with the translation $x^i\to x^i+\alpha\,c^i$ for a small parameter $\alpha$ and constant shift direction $c^i$ (so $\delta x^i=c^i$ and, since the field's value isn't relabeled by a pure translation, $\delta u=-u_{x^k}c^k$ from formula $(\dagger)$, run in reverse to hold the underlying field configuration fixed while the coordinate grid shifts). The resulting Noether divergence, worked out explicitly for each direction $c^i$, defines the energy-momentum tensor
$$T^i_{\ k} = u_{x^k}\frac{\partial\mathcal L}{\partial u_{x^i}} - \delta^i_k\,\mathcal L,$$
where $\delta^i_k$ is $1$ if $i=k$ and $0$ otherwise (the Kronecker delta, bookkeeping for "same index or not"), and Noether's theorem for this symmetry says exactly
$$\sum_{i=1}^4\frac{\partial T^i_{\ k}}{\partial x^i} = 0 \qquad (k=1,2,3,4). \tag{conservation}$$
The component $T^4_{\ 4}$ (with $x^4$ playing the role of time) is the field's energy density, and $T^i_{\ 4}$ for $i=1,2,3$ are the components of energy flux (how energy is flowing through space); by the divergence theorem $(\ast)$, integrating (conservation) over any fixed spatial volume converts it into the statement that the rate of change of total energy inside equals minus the flux of energy through the bounding surface — exactly the local, differential form of "energy is conserved," now derived from a single symmetry assumption on $\mathcal L$ rather than assumed as a separate physical law. The remaining components similarly package conservation of the three components of momentum.

38.4 The angular momentum tensor

Background primer

A primer: Lorentz transformations (what you need, briefly)

Special relativity's basic postulate is that the laws of physics look the same to every observer moving at constant velocity relative to any other — but "the same event" gets assigned different spacetime coordinates $(x^1,x^2,x^3,x^4=ct)$ by different such observers ($c$ the speed of light). A Lorentz transformation is the specific linear change of coordinates relating one observer's description to another's, and it includes, as special cases, both ordinary spatial rotations (mixing $x^1,x^2,x^3$ among themselves, leaving $x^4$ alone) and "boosts" (mixing a space coordinate with the time coordinate $x^4$, relating observers moving relative to each other). The key fact needed here is simply that Lorentz transformations form a family of linear transformations of the four coordinates depending on continuous parameters (angles, for rotations; relative velocity, for boosts) — so Lorentz invariance of a Lagrangian density is exactly a continuous symmetry, of exactly the kind Noether's theorem is built to exploit, generalizing ordinary rotational invariance in space to a rotational-type invariance mixing space and time together.

If $\mathcal L$ is invariant under ordinary spatial rotations mixing $x^i,x^k$ (a special case of Lorentz invariance, exactly generalizing the ordinary three-dimensional rotational symmetry that produces conservation of angular momentum for a single particle), Noether's theorem produces a divergence-free three-index tensor, the angular momentum tensor
$$M^{j}_{\ ik} = x_iT^j_{\ k}-x_kT^j_{\ i},$$
built directly from the energy-momentum tensor of Section 38.3 (compare this construction to the elementary-mechanics formula $\vec L=\vec r\times\vec p$ for a single particle's angular momentum — $M^j_{ik}$ is exactly this cross-product structure, now with the tensor $T$ playing the role of momentum density), satisfying its own conservation law $\sum_j\partial M^j_{\ ik}/\partial x^j=0$. If $\mathcal L$'s symmetry extends to the full Lorentz group — including boosts mixing space and time, not just spatial rotations — the same construction, now applied to a boost symmetry, produces an additional conserved quantity that (worked out in the practice problems) amounts to a statement about the motion of the field's "center of energy," the relativistic generalization of a center-of-mass theorem. The overall moral: every classical conservation law you already know from particle mechanics — energy, the three components of momentum, the three components of angular momentum — reappears here, term for term, as a Noether consequence of one of the spacetime symmetries a Lagrangian density can have.

38.5 The electromagnetic field

The chapter's capstone example: derive Maxwell's equations themselves from a variational principle. The electromagnetic field is described by a four-potential $\{A_j\}=(A_1,A_2,A_3,A_4)$ (three components matching the ordinary vector potential, one matching the scalar potential, packaged as a single four-component field), from which the physically measurable electric and magnetic fields are built as derivatives, via the antisymmetric combination $F_{ik}=\partial A_k/\partial x^i-\partial A_i/\partial x^k$ (the electromagnetic field tensor; note $F_{ik}=-F_{ki}$ and $F_{ii}=0$ automatically, from the definition).

The Lagrangian density and gauge invariance. Take $\mathcal L = -\frac14\sum_{i,k}F_{ik}F^{ik}$ (a specific quadratic combination of the field-tensor components, chosen — as will be seen — precisely so that its Euler equations reproduce Maxwell's equations). Because $\mathcal L$ is built entirely from $F_{ik}$, and $F_{ik}$ is built entirely from derivatives of $A_j$, this Lagrangian density has a remarkable extra symmetry beyond ordinary spacetime symmetries: the gauge transformation $A_j\to A_j+\partial\chi/\partial x^j$, for any differentiable function $\chi$, leaves $F_{ik}$ — and hence $\mathcal L$ itself — completely unchanged (a direct computation: $\partial_i(\partial_k\chi)=\partial_k(\partial_i\chi)$, since mixed partials commute, so the extra terms in $F_{ik}$ cancel identically). This is precisely the "add an exact divergence to $F$" freedom flagged at the end of Section 37.4, now appearing not as an artificial illustration but as the deepest structural feature of electromagnetism: the four-potential $A_j$ itself is never directly measurable, only $F_{ik}$ (equivalently, the physical $\vec E,\vec B$ fields) is, and gauge invariance is the mathematical expression of that fact. To pin down a specific $A_j$ (rather than an entire gauge-equivalent family of them), it's standard to impose the extra condition $\sum_j\partial A_j/\partial x^j=0$, called the Lorentz condition (or Lorentz gauge) — a choice, not a physical requirement, but a convenient one.

Deriving Maxwell's equations. Applying the Euler equation (4') to $\mathcal L$, treating each component $A_j$ as its own unknown field, produces (after the index-notation bookkeeping the primer above was built to make legible)
$$\sum_i\frac{\partial F_{ij}}{\partial x^i} = 0 \qquad (j=1,\dots,4),$$
which, written out in terms of the ordinary electric and magnetic field components that $F_{ik}$ packages, is exactly the source-free Maxwell's equations — $\nabla\cdot\vec E=0$ and $\nabla\times\vec B-\frac1c\partial\vec E/\partial t=0$, unified into a single tensor statement. Imposing the Lorentz condition on top of the Euler equation, and using the definition of $F_{ik}$ once more, collapses the whole system further into
$$\Box A_j := \sum_{i=1}^4\frac{\partial^2A_j}{(\partial x^i)^2} = 0 \qquad (j=1,\dots,4),$$
where $\Box$ (the d'Alembertian, the natural four-dimensional generalization of the Laplacian $\nabla^2$ from Section 35, now including a time-derivative term with the appropriate sign) is the wave operator of special relativity — so each component of the four-potential, in the Lorentz gauge, satisfies its own copy of the wave equation Section 36 derived for a vibrating string, propagating at the speed of light. This is Maxwell's theory of electromagnetic waves, recovered in full from a single scalar Lagrangian density and the Euler equation.

A worked example: the Klein–Gordon equation. As a variant worth knowing, take instead $\mathcal L=\frac12\sum_i(u_{x^i})^2-\frac12m^2u^2$ for a single scalar field $u$ (rather than the vector field $A_j$) — formally the same recipe as the wave equation's Lagrangian, with one new term added. The Euler equation gives
$$\Box u - m^2u = 0,$$
the Klein–Gordon equation, the relativistic field equation for a particle of mass $m$ (reducing to the source-free wave equation $\Box u=0$ exactly when $m=0$, matching the physical fact that the electromagnetic field's quanta, photons, are massless). It's included here mainly to underscore the chapter's central point one last time: an enormous swath of twentieth-century physics — electromagnetism, and the relativistic wave equations that came after it — is, mechanically, nothing but Chapter 1's Euler equation, run on a Lagrangian density instead of a Lagrangian, with the conservation laws that make the theory physically meaningful supplied automatically by Noether's theorem.

Chapter recap

This chapter promoted every piece of machinery built for curves in Chapters 1 through 6 up to surfaces (and beyond, to functions of arbitrarily many independent variables). Section 35 built the one truly new tool required — the divergence theorem, the multivariable heir to integration by parts — and used it to derive the Euler equation for multiple integrals, a partial rather than an ordinary differential equation, illustrated immediately by the Dirichlet integral's connection to Laplace's equation. Section 36 cashed this in on real physics: the vibrating string, membrane, and (more briefly) plate, each governed by an Euler equation derived from a single kinetic-minus-potential-energy action, with fixed, free, and elastically-supported boundary conditions falling directly out of the boundary terms in the variation — plus a resolution, via Chapter 5's conjugate points, of exactly when Hamilton's principle of "least" action is actually a minimum rather than merely a stationary point. Section 37 generalized Chapter 3's movable-endpoint machinery to a moving region, producing the general variation formula (95) and, from it, a full multivariable Noether's theorem. Section 38 spent that theorem on field theory: the Lagrangian density, the energy-momentum tensor (from translation invariance), the angular momentum tensor (from rotational and Lorentz invariance), and — as the capstone — a derivation of Maxwell's equations for the electromagnetic field straight from a single Lagrangian density, gauge invariance, and the Euler equation, capped off with the Klein–Gordon equation as a close relative. The throughline, worth carrying forward: every conservation law of classical and relativistic field physics is, underneath, a Noether consequence of a spacetime symmetry — the same idea Chapter 4 introduced for finitely many particles, now shown to scale all the way up to the fundamental fields of physics.

Problem 1

1. (The Euler equation for a specific multiple integral.) Find the Euler equation for $J[u]=\iint_D(u_x^2+u_y^2+2uf(x,y))\,dx\,dy$, where $f$ is a given function.

Hint: Apply (4) directly: $F_u=2f$, $F_{u_x}=2u_x$, $F_{u_y}=2u_y$. You should find $u_{xx}+u_{yy}=f$ — Poisson's equation, the natural inhomogeneous generalization of the Laplace's-equation example worked out in Section 35 (Poisson's equation governs, for instance, an electrostatic potential in the presence of a charge density $f$, in contrast to Laplace's equation's charge-free case).

Problem 2

2. (A minimal-surface-flavored functional.) Write down the Euler equation for $J[u]=\iint_D\sqrt{1+u_x^2+u_y^2}\,dx\,dy$ (the area of the surface $z=u(x,y)$ — the direct two-variable generalization of arc length).

Hint: Compute $F_{u_x}=u_x/\sqrt{1+u_x^2+u_y^2}$ and $F_{u_y}=u_y/\sqrt{1+u_x^2+u_y^2}$, then apply (4) with $F_u=0$. The resulting equation, after simplifying, is the minimal surface equation $(1+u_y^2)u_{xx}-2u_xu_yu_{xy}+(1+u_x^2)u_{yy}=0$ — the higher-dimensional cousin of the surface-of-revolution problem from Chapter 1, and the equation governing soap films spanning a wire boundary.

Problem 3

3. (Boundary conditions for a free membrane.) Write out the boundary condition for the vibrating-membrane problem of Section 36.3 when the entire edge $\partial D$ is completely free (no support of any kind).

Hint: Redo the boundary-term computation from Section 36.1's fixed/free/elastic discussion, but for the membrane's action integral, using formula (95) with $\delta u$ unrestricted on $\partial D$ (nothing prescribed there) and $\delta n=0$ (the region $D$ itself isn't moving, only $u$ is varying on it). Problem 4

4. (Working through the harmonic-oscillator conjugate-point computation.) Verify directly, by solving the Jacobi equation, that the conjugate point to $t=0$ for the action $J[x]=\int_0^T\frac12(m\dot x^2-kx^2)\,dt$ occurs at $t=\pi/\omega$, $\omega=\sqrt{k/m}$, as claimed in Section 36.2.

Hint: Following Chapter 5, Section 27's recipe, the Jacobi equation for this (already-quadratic) functional is exactly the original Euler equation itself, $m\ddot\eta+k\eta=0$, with the conjugate-point condition being the first zero, after $t=0$, of the solution starting at $\eta(0)=0$. Solve this explicitly ($\eta(t)=\sin\omega t$ works, up to a constant multiple) and find its first positive zero.

Problem 5

5. (Noether's theorem for time-translation invariance, worked by hand for a single particle first.) Before tackling the field-theoretic energy-momentum tensor of Section 38.3 in general, work the single-particle case explicitly: for $J[q]=\int_{t_0}^{t_1}L(q,\dot q)\,dt$ with $L$ not depending explicitly on $t$, show directly (without invoking the general multivariable theorem) that $H=\dot qL_{\dot q}-L$ is conserved along every extremal.

Hint: This is exactly Chapter 4, Section 22's derivation of energy conservation, and it's worth re-deriving here from scratch as a warm-up, since $T^4_{\ 4}=u_{x^4}\mathcal L_{u_{x^4}}-\mathcal L$ in Section 38.3 is literally the same formula, with $q\to u$ and $t\to x^4$ — confirming that the energy-momentum tensor's time-time component really is nothing but the Hamiltonian, generalized from particles to fields.

Problem 6

6. (Checking gauge invariance directly.) Verify by direct computation that $F_{ik}=\partial A_k/\partial x^i-\partial A_i/\partial x^k$ is unchanged when $A_j\to A_j+\partial\chi/\partial x^j$ for an arbitrary twice-differentiable function $\chi$, as claimed in Section 38.5.

Hint: Substitute directly: the new field tensor is $\partial_i(A_k+\partial_k\chi)-\partial_k(A_i+\partial_i\chi) = F_{ik} + (\partial_i\partial_k\chi-\partial_k\partial_i\chi)$. The parenthesized term is exactly zero because mixed partial derivatives commute (assuming $\chi$ is twice continuously differentiable) — the entire gauge-invariance argument rests on this one elementary calculus fact.

Problem 7

7. (The wave equation as a special case of the Klein–Gordon equation.) Show that setting $m=0$ in the Klein–Gordon equation of Section 38.5 recovers the source-free wave equation $\Box u=0$ derived for the electromagnetic potential, and interpret this physically.

Hint: Direct substitution into $\Box u-m^2u=0$. Physically: $m$ in the Klein–Gordon equation plays the role of the mass of the field's associated particle (in quantum field theory); a massless field's quanta — like the photon, described by $A_j$ in Section 38.5 — obey the plain wave equation, propagating at exactly the speed of light, while a field with $m>0$ obeys a modified wave equation whose solutions propagate more slowly and disperse differently.

Problem 8

8. (Building the divergence theorem primer's identity for $n=3$.) Write out the divergence-theorem identity $(\ast)$ explicitly for three independent variables $x_1,x_2,x_3$, and state what region and boundary it refers to.

Hint: $(\ast)$ becomes $\iiint_Vf_{x_i}\,dV=\iint_{\partial V}fn_i\,dS$ for $i=1,2,3$, where $V$ is now a solid three-dimensional region, $\partial V$ its bounding surface, $dV$ ordinary volume measure, and $dS$ ordinary surface measure — this is the identity actually needed (rather than the two-variable version worked out in the Section 35 primer) for the vibrating-plate discussion of Section 36.4 and for any fully three-dimensional field theory such as full electromagnetism in space.

Problem 9

9. (A symmetry that does not produce a new conservation law.) Explain, using the gauge-transformation remark at the end of Section 37.4, why adding an arbitrary exact divergence $\partial_xG_1+\partial_yG_2$ to a Lagrangian $F$ changes neither the Euler equation nor (once boundary terms are handled with care) any physically meaningful conserved quantity, even though it does change $F$ itself.

Hint: Use $(\ast)$ to rewrite $\iint_D(\partial_xG_1+\partial_yG_2)\,dx\,dy$ as a boundary integral $\oint_{\partial D}(G_1n_1+G_2n_2)\,ds$ depending only on $G_1,G_2$'s values on $\partial D$; since this contributes nothing to the interior variation used to derive the Euler equation, the extremals of $F$ and $F+\partial_xG_1+\partial_yG_2$ coincide exactly. This is the general phenomenon of which Section 38.5's gauge invariance of electromagnetism is a specific, physically essential instance.
