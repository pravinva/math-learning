00Roadmap: four independent generalizations

Chapter 1 solved exactly one problem — a single unknown function $y(x)$, first derivatives only, both endpoints pinned down — using exactly one method (perturb, Taylor-expand, integrate by parts, invoke the fundamental lemma). Chapter 2 takes that one method and points it at four different relaxations of the "simplest problem," one section at a time:

  • Section 9: what if there isn't just one unknown function $y(x)$, but several, $y_1(x),\dots,y_n(x)$ — for example, because you're looking for a curve in three-dimensional space, not a curve in the plane?
  • Section 10: what if the curve isn't conveniently described by an explicit formula $y=y(x)$, but instead given parametrically, as $x=x(t), y=y(t)$? (This is unavoidable for closed curves, or curves that double back on themselves.)
  • Section 11: what if the functional depends not just on $y$ and $y'$, but on higher derivatives $y'', y''', \dots$?
  • Section 12: what if the admissible curves aren't just required to hit fixed endpoints, but also to satisfy some extra condition along the way — like having a fixed total length, or being confined to a given surface?

Every single one of these four sections follows the identical three-step pattern from Chapter 1: (1) write down what "an admissible perturbation" means in this new setting; (2) Taylor-expand the increment $\Delta J$ and keep only the linear part to get $\delta J$; (3) integrate by parts until you can invoke a fundamental-lemma-type argument, producing a differential equation. If you keep that three-step skeleton in mind, none of the four sections below should feel like new material — just the same skeleton, dressed in new notation.

09The Fixed End Point Problem for $n$ Unknown Functions

9.1 Setting up the problem

Let $F(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,z_1,\dots,z_n)$ have continuous first and second partial derivatives in all $2n+1$ arguments. Consider
$$J[y_1,\dots,y_n] = \int_a^b F(x,y_1,\dots,y_n,y_1',\dots,y_n')\,dx, \tag{1}$$
where each $y_i(x)$ is continuously differentiable on $[a,b]$ and satisfies fixed boundary conditions
$$y_i(a)=A_i,\qquad y_i(b)=B_i \qquad (i=1,\dots,n).$$

How to picture this. Instead of thinking of $y_1(x),\dots,y_n(x)$ as $n$ separate curves, think of them as the $n$ coordinate functions of a single curve $\bigl(x, y_1(x),\dots,y_n(x)\bigr)$ living in $(n+1)$-dimensional space $\mathscr{E}_{n+1}$, parametrized by $x$. The functional (1) reads off the "position" $(y_1,\dots,y_n)$ and "velocity" $(y_1',\dots,y_n')$ at each point along this curve and feeds them into $F$. This is the natural setting for two important classes of problems:

  • Geodesics. The shortest curve joining two points on a curved surface, or more generally on any higher-dimensional curved space (a "manifold"), is called a geodesic. Finding geodesics is exactly a variational problem of the type (1) — we'll see two worked examples below.
  • Fermat's principle in optics. Light traveling through an optically inhomogeneous medium (where the speed of light depends on position) travels along the path of least time between two points. Since the path lives in 3-dimensional space, describing it needs two functions $y(x), z(x)$ (thinking of $x$ as the "independent" coordinate and $y,z$ as functions along the ray) — exactly the $n=2$ case of (1).

9.2 Deriving the system of Euler equations

Perturb each $y_i(x)$ independently by $h_i(x)$, where each $h_i$ must vanish at both ends: $h_i(a)=h_i(b)=0$ (to preserve the boundary conditions). Taylor-expanding $F$ in all $2n$ perturbed arguments $h_1,\dots,h_n,h_1',\dots,h_n'$ simultaneously (exactly the "sum the effect of nudging each input separately" idea from the Chapter 1 primer, just with $2n$ inputs instead of $2$):
$$\Delta J = \int_a^b \sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(F_{y_i}h_i + F_{y_i'}h_i'\bigr)dx + (\text{higher order}), \qquad \delta J = \int_a^b\sum_{i=1}^n\bigl(F_{y_i}h_i+F_{y_i'}h_i'\bigr)dx.$$

Here is the one new idea in this section, and it's a simple but important one: because the $n$ perturbations $h_1,\dots,h_n$ can be chosen completely independently of one another, we are free to set all of them to zero except one — say, set $h_2=h_3=\cdots=h_n\equiv 0$ and leave $h_1$ arbitrary. Then $\delta J = 0$ reduces to $\int_a^b\bigl(F_{y_1}h_1+F_{y_1'}h_1'\bigr)dx=0$ for arbitrary admissible $h_1$ — exactly Lemma 4 from Chapter 1, giving $F_{y_1}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y_1'}=0$. Repeating this, one index at a time, for $i=2,\dots,n$, gives a whole system of Euler equations:
$$\boxed{F_{y_i} - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y_i'} = 0, \qquad i=1,\dots,n.} \tag{3}$$

This is the exact functional-calculus mirror of a very familiar fact from ordinary multivariable calculus: at a critical point of $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$, every partial derivative $\partial f/\partial x_i$ vanishes, and you find this out by wiggling one coordinate at a time while freezing the rest. Here we wiggle one function at a time while freezing the rest, and get one Euler equation per function. Since (3) is a system of $n$ second-order ODEs, its general solution carries $2n$ arbitrary constants, matched by the $2n$ boundary values $A_i,B_i$.

A remark on non-uniqueness of $F$ (equivalent functionals). Just as in the single-function case, two different integrands $F$ can produce the same system of Euler equations. If $\Phi(x,y_1,\dots,y_n)$ is any twice-differentiable function, and you define
$$\Psi = \Phi_x + \sum_{i=1}^n \Phi_{y_i}y_i',$$
then $\Psi$ is exactly $\frac{d}{dx}\Phi\bigl[x,y_1(x),\dots,y_n(x)\bigr]$ along any curve — so adding $\Psi$ to $F$ only changes $\int F\,dx$ by the constant $\Phi\big|_a^b$ (a boundary term, not depending on the interior shape of the curve at all), which doesn't affect where the extremum occurs. Two functionals related this way are called equivalent — they share the same extremals, even though their integrands (and even their numerical values) differ.

9.3 Worked Example 1: light in an inhomogeneous medium

Suppose the speed of light at point $(x,y,z)$ is some prescribed function $v(x,y,z)$ (the medium's optical density varies from point to point). For a ray described as $y=y(x), z=z(x)$ joining two points $A,B$, the travel time is
$$T[y,z] = \int_a^b \frac{\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2}}{v(x,y,z)}\,dx.$$
By Fermat's principle, actual light rays are the curves that minimize $T$ — i.e., they are the extremals of this functional. Applying the system (3) with $n=2$ (writing out $F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ and $F_z-\frac{d}{dx}F_{z'}=0$ explicitly, where $F = \sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2}/v(x,y,z)$) gives the pair of coupled differential equations
$$\frac{v_y}{v^2}\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2} + \frac{d}{dx}\frac{y'}{v\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2}} = 0, \qquad \frac{v_z}{v^2}\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2} + \frac{d}{dx}\frac{z'}{v\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2}} = 0.$$
You aren't expected to solve these in general (they depend on the specific function $v$), but notice the structural point: the physical law "light minimizes travel time" and the mathematical machinery "system of Euler equations for $n=2$" are one and the same statement. This is the first hint of a theme — variational principles as a unifying language for physics — that the book returns to at length in Chapter 4 (outside our current scope, but worth knowing it's coming).

Background primer

9.4 Worked Example 2: geodesics, and a primer on the first fundamental form

Setting up arc length on a curved surface. Suppose a surface $\sigma$ in space is described by a vector-valued function $\mathbf{r}(u,v)$ — meaning that as $(u,v)$ range over some region, the point $\mathbf{r}(u,v)$ traces out the surface (think of $u,v$ as two independent "surface coordinates," the way latitude and longitude parametrize a sphere). A curve lying on this surface can then be described just by giving $u=u(t), v=v(t)$ for the two surface coordinates as functions of a parameter $t$.

To measure the length of such a curve, we need to know how a small step $du, dv$ in the surface coordinates translates into an actual distance moved in space. This is exactly the role of the first fundamental form of the surface, defined by three functions (of $u,v$):
$$E = \mathbf{r}_u\cdot\mathbf{r}_u, \qquad F = \mathbf{r}_u\cdot\mathbf{r}_v, \qquad G=\mathbf{r}_v\cdot\mathbf{r}_v$$
(using boldface for vectors, and $\cdot$ for the ordinary dot product from linear algebra — nothing exotic here, just dot products of the partial-derivative vectors $\mathbf{r}_u,\mathbf{r}_v$). These three functions let you write down the "Pythagorean theorem, adapted to the surface's own coordinate grid":
$$ds^2 = E\,du^2 + 2F\,du\,dv + G\,dv^2.$$
(If $u,v$ happened to be ordinary Cartesian coordinates on a flat plane, $E=G=1$ and $F=0$, and this collapses to the familiar $ds^2=du^2+dv^2$ — the general formula just accounts for the fact that, on a curved or obliquely-coordinatized surface, moving one unit in the $u$ direction or the $v$ direction might correspond to more or less than one unit of actual distance, and the two directions might not even be perpendicular.)

The geodesic problem as a variational problem. The length of a curve $u=u(t),v=v(t)$ between parameter values $t_0,t_1$ is
$$J[u,v] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\sqrt{E\dot u^2 + 2F\dot u\dot v + G\dot v^2}\,dt$$
(the overdot denotes $d/dt$, following the book's convention — this notation, borrowed from mechanics where dots denote time-derivatives, is used specifically to flag that $t$ here is just some parameter, not necessarily arc length itself). The geodesic connecting two points on the surface is, by definition, the curve of least length among all curves on $\sigma$ joining them — so finding geodesics is exactly the $n=2$ case of Section 9's problem, with $F$ (confusingly reusing the letter $F$ — the book does this too, and context always makes clear whether "$F$" means the fundamental-form coefficient or the variational integrand) playing the role of one of the coordinate functions and $E,F,G$ playing the role of coefficients. Writing out the Euler equations (3) for this $J[u,v]$ gives the (fairly involved-looking, but purely mechanical to derive) system of geodesic equations.

Worked example: geodesics on a cylinder. Take the circular cylinder of radius $a$, parametrized by
$$\mathbf{r}(\varphi,z) = (a\cos\varphi,\ a\sin\varphi,\ z)$$
(here the two "surface coordinates" are named $\varphi$ and $z$, playing the roles of $u,v$ above). Computing the fundamental form coefficients: $\mathbf{r}_\varphi = (-a\sin\varphi, a\cos\varphi, 0)$, so $E = \mathbf{r}_\varphi\cdot\mathbf{r}_\varphi = a^2\sin^2\varphi+a^2\cos^2\varphi = a^2$; $\mathbf{r}_z = (0,0,1)$, so $G=\mathbf{r}_z\cdot\mathbf{r}_z=1$; and $F = \mathbf{r}_\varphi\cdot\mathbf{r}_z = 0$. So the length functional (parametrizing a curve on the cylinder as $\varphi=\varphi(t), z=z(t)$) is
$$J[\varphi,z] = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\sqrt{a^2\dot\varphi^2+\dot z^2}\,dt.$$
This integrand doesn't depend on $\varphi$ or $z$ explicitly (only on their $t$-derivatives) — so both Euler equations are immediately first integrals, exactly Case 1 from Chapter 1, Section 4.3, applied to each variable in turn:
$$\frac{d}{dt}\frac{a^2\dot\varphi}{\sqrt{a^2\dot\varphi^2+\dot z^2}} = 0, \qquad \frac{d}{dt}\frac{\dot z}{\sqrt{a^2\dot\varphi^2+\dot z^2}}=0 \implies \frac{a^2\dot\varphi}{\sqrt{a^2\dot\varphi^2+\dot z^2}}=C_1,\quad \frac{\dot z}{\sqrt{a^2\dot\varphi^2+\dot z^2}} = C_2.$$
Dividing the second equation by the first eliminates the common (messy) denominator entirely:
$$\frac{dz}{d\varphi} = \frac{\dot z}{\dot \varphi} = \frac{C_2}{C_1}a^2 =: c_1 \implies z = c_1\varphi + c_2.$$
This is a helix (a two-parameter family, as expected for a second-order-ODE-pair system) — matching your intuition perfectly: the shortest path between two points on a cylinder, "unrolled" flat, is a straight line, and a straight line on the unrolled cylinder becomes a helix once you roll the cylinder back up. This is a good example of a case where you can — and should — sanity-check a variational-calculus answer against pure geometric intuition.

10Variational Problems in Parametric Form

10.1 Why we need this

So far, curves have always been given as explicit graphs $y=y(x)$. But plenty of natural curves cannot be written this way — a circle, for instance, fails the vertical-line test, so no single function $y(x)$ describes all of it; more generally, closed curves (central to the isoperimetric problem previewed in Chapter 1) and curves that double back on themselves are impossible to express as $y=y(x)$. The fix, familiar from calc 2's treatment of parametric curves, is to describe the curve by two functions of a third variable: $x=x(t), y=y(t)$.

10.2 Rewriting the "simplest" functional in parametric form

Starting from the familiar $\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y,y')\,dx$, substitute $y' = \dot y/\dot x$ (chain rule: since $y$ is really a function of $t$ through $x$, $\frac{dy}{dx} = \frac{dy/dt}{dx/dt}$) and $dx = \dot x\,dt$:
$$\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y,y')\,dx = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}F\left(x,y,\frac{\dot y}{\dot x}\right)\dot x\,dt =: \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Phi(x,y,\dot x,\dot y)\,dt. \tag{12}$$
The new integrand $\Phi$ has a special algebraic property, called positive homogeneity of degree 1 in $(\dot x,\dot y)$:
$$\Phi(x,y,\lambda\dot x,\lambda\dot y) \equiv \lambda\,\Phi(x,y,\dot x,\dot y) \quad \text{for every } \lambda>0. \tag{13}$$
Check this directly from the formula: replacing $\dot x\to\lambda\dot x,\dot y\to\lambda\dot y$ leaves the ratio $\dot y/\dot x$ (hence the value of $F$) unchanged, while the leftover factor $\dot x$ out front becomes $\lambda\dot x$ — so the whole thing scales by exactly $\lambda$. Homogeneity of degree 1 has a very natural meaning: it says the functional's value doesn't care how "fast" you traverse the curve, only which curve you traverse — reparametrizing $t$ to run twice as fast everywhere ($\lambda=2$ throughout) doubles $\dot x,\dot y$ but leaves the geometric path identical, and (13) guarantees the computed value is unaffected in the appropriate sense (we make this precise next). Note carefully that this only holds for $\lambda>0$ — reversing the direction of travel ($\lambda<0$) is a different thing, since e.g. arc length computed while traversing a curve backwards is still positive, not the negative of the forward value; (13) is specifically a statement about slowing down or speeding up without reversing direction.

10.3 The converse theorem: which parametric functionals are "geometric"?

Theorem

A functional $\int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Phi(t,x,y,\dot x,\dot y)\,dt$ depends only on the geometric curve traced in the $xy$-plane (and not on which particular parametrization was used to trace it) if and only if $\Phi$ does not involve $t$ explicitly, and $\Phi$ is positive-homogeneous of degree 1 in $(\dot x,\dot y)$.

Why homogeneity is exactly the right condition. Suppose we reparametrize by some increasing, differentiable substitution $t = t(\tau)$ (so $dt/d\tau > 0$ — the new parameter still traces the curve in the same direction, just possibly at a different "speed" at each moment). By the chain rule,
$$\frac{dx}{d\tau} = \dot x\,\frac{dt}{d\tau}, \qquad \frac{dy}{d\tau} = \dot y\,\frac{dt}{d\tau}.$$
So switching from $t$ to $\tau$ replaces $(\dot x,\dot y)$ by $(\dot x,\dot y)$ scaled by the positive factor $\lambda := dt/d\tau$ — precisely the scaling homogeneity is designed to handle. Substituting into the integral and using homogeneity degree 1 (equation (13)) plus the ordinary substitution rule $dt = \frac{dt}{d\tau}d\tau$:
$$\int_{\tau_0}^{\tau_1}\Phi\left(x,y,\frac{dx}{d\tau},\frac{dy}{d\tau}\right)d\tau = \int_{\tau_0}^{\tau_1}\Phi\left(x,y,\dot x\frac{dt}{d\tau},\dot y\frac{dt}{d\tau}\right)d\tau = \int_{\tau_0}^{\tau_1}\Phi(x,y,\dot x,\dot y)\frac{dt}{d\tau}\,d\tau = \int_{t_0}^{t_1}\Phi(x,y,\dot x,\dot y)\,dt,$$
where the last equality is just the ordinary substitution rule for single-variable integrals run in reverse. The two integrals — one computed in the $\tau$-parametrization, one in the $t$-parametrization — come out identical, exactly as claimed. (The book states this as an "if and only if"; we've shown the "if" direction in detail, since it's the direction actually used everywhere else in the chapter.)

10.4 The Euler equations in parametric form, and why there are only "really one" of them

Applying the ordinary $n=2$ system of Euler equations (Section 9) to $\Phi(x,y,\dot x,\dot y)$ (treating $t$ as the independent variable, $x,y$ as the two unknown functions) gives
$$\Phi_x - \frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{\dot x}=0, \qquad \Phi_y - \frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{\dot y}=0. \tag{15}$$
This looks like two conditions — but it can't really be, because the original problem (before we introduced the artificial parameter $t$) only had one unknown curve, $y=y(x)$, governed by a single Euler equation $F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$. The resolution is that equations (15) are not independent: they satisfy the identity
$$\dot x\left(\Phi_x-\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_x\right)... $$

More precisely, the book establishes the identity
$$\dot x\left(\Phi_x - \frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{\dot x}\right) + \dot y\left(\Phi_y-\frac{d}{dt}\Phi_{\dot y}\right) \equiv 0 \tag{16}$$
identically, for any curve at all (not just extremals) — a purely algebraic consequence of the homogeneity property (13) (differentiate the homogeneity identity with respect to $\lambda$ and set $\lambda=1$ to get a relation among the partial derivatives of $\Phi$, called Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions — see the practice problems below for a full derivation of this useful general fact). Since (16) is a linear combination of the two left-hand sides of (15) that vanishes identically, the two equations in (15) can't be independent conditions: at most one new piece of information is being imposed, matching the fact that there is really only one curve's worth of freedom to pin down.

Why does this make sense? The parametrization $t$ was an artificial bookkeeping device we introduced — the actual geometric problem has no preference for how fast you move along the correct curve, only for which curve it is. So it would be strange if the parametric Euler equations somehow determined the "timing" $t\mapsto(x(t),y(t))$ uniquely; they can only pin down the shape of the curve, leaving the parametrization free (any reparametrization of an extremal is, trivially, still an extremal of the parametric problem, by the invariance-under-reparametrization property from Section 10.3). That "one direction's worth" of left-over freedom is exactly what identity (16) reflects.

11Functionals Depending on Higher-Order Derivatives

11.1 Setting up the problem

Many applications (elasticity theory is the book's standing example — think of the bending energy of a beam, which depends on its curvature, i.e., on $y''$) require functionals that depend on second or higher derivatives:
$$J[y] = \int_a^b F(x,y,y',\dots,y^{(n)})\,dx, \tag{17}$$
where $F$ has continuous first and second partials in all its arguments. The admissible functions $y(x)$ live in $\mathscr{D}_n(a,b)$ (recall from Chapter 1, Section 2.3: functions with $n$ continuous derivatives), and the boundary conditions must now specify not just $y$ but all its derivatives up to order $n-1$ at both ends:
$$y(a)=A_0,\ y'(a)=A_1,\ \dots,\ y^{(n-1)}(a)=A_{n-1}, \qquad y(b)=B_0,\ y'(b)=B_1,\ \dots,\ y^{(n-1)}(b)=B_{n-1}. \tag{18}$$

Why so many boundary conditions? Because, as we're about to see, Euler's equation in this setting will be a differential equation of order $2n$ (not just order 2) — and a $2n$-th order ODE's general solution has $2n$ arbitrary constants, exactly matched by the $2n$ numbers in (18) (that's $n$ conditions at $a$, and $n$ more at $b$). Correspondingly, an admissible perturbation $h(x)$ must vanish, together with all its derivatives up to order $n-1$, at both endpoints:
$$h(a)=h'(a)=\cdots=h^{(n-1)}(a)=0, \qquad h(b)=h'(b)=\cdots=h^{(n-1)}(b)=0. \tag{19}$$
(If $h^{(k)}(a)$ were allowed to be nonzero for some $k<n$, then perturbing by $h$ would shift the prescribed value $y^{(k)}(a)=A_k$ away from $A_k$, so admissibility forces all these derivatives to vanish—exactly mirroring how, in the simplest problem, requiring $y(a)$ to stay fixed forced $h(a)=0$.)

11.2 Deriving the Euler–Poisson equation

Taylor-expanding $F$ in all $n+1$ perturbed quantities $h,h',\dots,h^{(n)}$:
$$\delta J = \int_a^b\bigl(F_y h + F_{y'}h' + \cdots + F_{y^{(n)}}h^{(n)}\bigr)dx.$$
Now we integrate by parts repeatedly — the term $F_{y^{(k)}}h^{(k)}$ needs to be integrated by parts $k$ times to strip all the derivatives off of $h$ and pile them onto $F_{y^{(k)}}$ instead, at the cost of a sign $(-1)^k$ each time (and each intermediate boundary term vanishes automatically, because of the boundary conditions (19) on $h$ and its derivatives!). Carrying this out for every term and collecting:
$$0 = \int_a^b \left[F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} + \frac{d^2}{dx^2}F_{y''} - \cdots + (-1)^n\frac{d^n}{dx^n}F_{y^{(n)}}\right]h(x)\,dx,$$
valid for every admissible $h$ (i.e. every $h$ satisfying (19)). Invoking the appropriate generalization of Lemma 1 from Chapter 1 (the same bump-function argument goes through unchanged — the extra vanishing derivatives in (19) are exactly what's needed to make the repeated integrations by parts legal) gives the Euler–Poisson equation:
$$\boxed{F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} + \frac{d^2}{dx^2}F_{y''} - \cdots + (-1)^n\frac{d^n}{dx^n}F_{y^{(n)}} = 0.} \tag{22}$$

This is manifestly a $2n$-th order ODE (differentiating $F_{y^{(n)}}$ a total of $n$ times generically brings in $y^{(2n)}$), matching the $2n$ boundary conditions in (18) exactly as promised.

(A technical remark, echoing the Theorem-3-style subtlety from Chapter 1: the derivation above silently uses the existence of $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'},\dots,\frac{d^n}{dx^n}F_{y^{(n)}}$. As in Chapter 1, a more careful argument — which we won't reproduce — shows these derivatives are automatically guaranteed to exist by the very argument proving (22), so nothing extra needs to be assumed in advance.)

11.3 Two useful first integrals (mirroring Chapter 1's Cases 1 and 2)

Exactly as in Chapter 1, when $F$ happens to be missing one of its arguments, the Euler–Poisson equation simplifies:

  • If $F$ does not depend on $y$: the very first term $F_y$ drops out of (22), and one can check that the whole left side becomes a total derivative, giving the first integral
    $$F_{y'} - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y''} = \text{const}$$
    (for the $n=2$ case; the general pattern continues with more terms for larger $n$) — directly generalizing Chapter 1's Case 1 ($F_{y'}=C$), just shifted one derivative-order higher because the "missing" variable is $y$ rather than $y'$.

  • If $F$ does not depend on $x$: mirroring the multiply-by-$y'$-and-recognize-a-total-derivative trick from Chapter 1's Case 2, one obtains (for $n=2$) the first integral
    $$F - y'\left(F_{y'}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y''}\right) - y''F_{y''} = \text{const}.$$

You can verify either identity the same way Chapter 1's Case 2 was verified: differentiate the right-hand-side candidate expression with respect to $x$ using the product and chain rules, substitute in the Euler–Poisson equation, and confirm everything cancels to zero.

11.4 A worked example

Problem. Find the extremals of $J[y] = \int_0^1 (1+y''^2)\,dx$ subject to $y(0)=0,\ y'(0)=1,\ y(1)=1,\ y'(1)=1$ (four boundary conditions total, matching $n=2$, i.e. a 4th-order Euler–Poisson equation).

Here $F=1+y''^2$ depends on neither $x$ nor $y$ nor $y'$ — only $y''$. So $F_y=0, F_{y'}=0, F_{y''}=2y''$, and the Euler–Poisson equation (22) with $n=2$ reads
$$0 - \frac{d}{dx}(0) + \frac{d^2}{dx^2}(2y'') = 0 \implies y'''' = 0.$$
The general solution is a cubic, $y = C_0+C_1x+C_2x^2+C_3x^3$. Imposing all four boundary conditions:
$$y(0)=0\implies C_0=0; \qquad y'(0)=1\implies C_1=1;$$
$$y(1)=1 \implies 1+C_2+C_3=1\implies C_2+C_3=0; \qquad y'(1)=1\implies 1+2C_2+3C_3=1\implies 2C_2+3C_3=0.$$
The last two equations force $C_2=C_3=0$. So the extremal is simply $y=x$ — the straight line (which makes good sense: the functional rewards small $y''$, and a straight line has $y''\equiv0$ everywhere, the smallest possible value of the integrand; the boundary data $y'(0)=y'(1)=1$ is exactly consistent with a straight line of slope 1 through the given endpoints, so no bending is needed at all).

12Variational Problems with Subsidiary Conditions

12.1 The isoperimetric problem, and the Lagrange multiplier idea

Setting up the problem. So far, admissible curves have been constrained only by their boundary values. Now we add one more kind of restriction: among curves satisfying the usual fixed endpoints $y(a)=A,y(b)=B$, we further restrict attention to those for which a second functional
$$K[y] = \int_a^b G(x,y,y')\,dx$$
takes some fixed prescribed value $l$, and we seek the extremum of $J[y]=\int_a^b F(x,y,y')\,dx$ among only these curves. This is called an isoperimetric problem, after its most famous instance: among closed curves of fixed perimeter $l$ (that's the constraint $K[y]=l$), find the one enclosing the largest area (that's the functional $J[y]$ being maximized).

The idea, borrowed from ordinary calculus. You've likely seen the Lagrange multiplier method for constrained optimization in ordinary multivariable calculus: to extremize $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ subject to a constraint $g(x_1,\dots,x_n)=c$, you instead look for an unconstrained critical point of $f+\lambda g$, for a suitable constant $\lambda$. The intuition (which uses only linear algebra you already know): at a constrained extremum, if you could move along the constraint surface $\{g=c\}$ and still change $f$ to first order, you wouldn't yet be at an extremum — you could improve $f$ while staying on the constraint. So at a genuine constrained extremum, "the direction that increases $f$" and "the directions allowed by the constraint" must be incompatible, which turns out to mean exactly that the gradient of $f$ is a scalar multiple of the gradient of $g$ — i.e. $\nabla f = -\lambda \nabla g$, i.e. $\nabla(f+\lambda g)=0$.

The functional version. The exact same logic transplants to functionals, with $\delta J/\delta y$ and $\delta K/\delta y$ (the variational derivatives from Chapter 1, Section 7) playing the role of $\nabla f$ and $\nabla g$:

Theorem 1

Let $J[y]=\int_a^b F\,dx$, with admissible curves satisfying $y(a)=A,y(b)=B$ and $K[y] = \int_a^b G\,dx=l$. If $J[y]$ has an extremum at $y=\hat y$, and $\hat y$ is not itself an extremal of $K$ alone, then there is a constant $\lambda$ such that $\hat y$ is an extremal of the unconstrained functional $\int_a^b(F+\lambda G)\,dx$ — i.e., $\hat y$ satisfies
$$F_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'} + \lambda\left(G_y - \frac{d}{dx}G_{y'}\right) = 0. \tag{27}$$

The proof, unpacked (this is a clever argument, worth following closely). Suppose $J[y]$ has an extremum at $\hat y$, subject to the constraint. The strategy is to perturb $\hat y$ at two separate, arbitrary-looking points $x_1$ and $x_2$ simultaneously — perturbing at two points, rather than just one, is exactly what lets us satisfy the constraint while still moving. Write the perturbation as $\delta_1y(x)+\delta_2y(x)$, where $\delta_1y$ is a bump concentrated near $x_1$ and $\delta_2y$ is a bump concentrated near $x_2$. Using variational derivatives (Chapter 1, Section 7.3), the resulting changes in $J$ and $K$ can be written (to leading order) as
$$\Delta J \approx \frac{\delta F}{\delta y}\bigg|_{x_1}\Delta\sigma_1 + \frac{\delta F}{\delta y}\bigg|_{x_2}\Delta\sigma_2, \qquad \Delta K \approx \frac{\delta G}{\delta y}\bigg|_{x_1}\Delta\sigma_1 + \frac{\delta G}{\delta y}\bigg|_{x_2}\Delta\sigma_2,$$
where $\Delta\sigma_1,\Delta\sigma_2$ are the (small) "areas" of the two bumps (in the sense of Chapter 1, Section 7.3).

Now, choose $x_2$ to be any point where $\delta G/\delta y \ne 0$ — such a point must exist, because we assumed $\hat y$ is not an extremal of $K$ alone (if $\delta G/\delta y$ vanished everywhere, $\hat y$ would itself solve Euler's equation for $K$). Having fixed such an $x_2$, we get to freely choose $x_1$ to be any point at all, and then use our remaining freedom — the size of the second bump, $\Delta\sigma_2$ — to exactly cancel out whatever change in $K$ the first bump caused, keeping the constraint $K=l$ satisfied to first order:
$$\Delta K = 0 \implies \Delta\sigma_2 \approx -\frac{(\delta G/\delta y)|_{x_1}}{(\delta G/\delta y)|_{x_2}}\Delta\sigma_1.$$
Substituting this forced value of $\Delta\sigma_2$ back into the expression for $\Delta J$, and defining $\lambda := -\dfrac{(\delta F/\delta y)|_{x_2}}{(\delta G/\delta y)|_{x_2}}$ (a fixed number, since $x_2$ was fixed once and for all), everything involving $x_2$ collapses into this single constant $\lambda$, and we're left with
$$\Delta J \approx \left\{\frac{\delta F}{\delta y}\bigg|_{x_1} + \lambda\,\frac{\delta G}{\delta y}\bigg|_{x_1}\right\}\Delta\sigma_1.$$
Since $\Delta J = 0$ is necessary at an extremum (Theorem 2 of Chapter 1, Section 3.4), and $x_1$ (and hence $\Delta\sigma_1$) was completely arbitrary, the bracketed coefficient must vanish at every point $x_1$:
$$\frac{\delta F}{\delta y} + \lambda\,\frac{\delta G}{\delta y} = 0 \quad \text{everywhere},$$
which is precisely (27). $\blacksquare$

What just happened, in plain language: the two-point perturbation trick is the functional-calculus version of "moving along the constraint surface." By spending one of our two perturbations entirely on maintaining the constraint, we're left with exactly the freedom to demand that $J$'s variational derivative be a fixed multiple of $K$'s variational derivative everywhere else — precisely the parallel-gradients condition from ordinary Lagrange multipliers, transplanted into function space.

Solving isoperimetric problems in practice: find the general solution of (27) — the extremals of $F+\lambda G$ — which will involve two arbitrary constants and the multiplier $\lambda$ (three unknowns total); then use the two boundary conditions $y(a)=A,y(b)=B$ and the constraint $K[y]=l$ (three equations total) to solve for all three.

Generalization to $n$ functions and $k$ constraints. Nothing changes conceptually if you have $n$ unknown functions $y_1,\dots,y_n$ and $k$ separate integral constraints $\int_a^b G_j\,dx = l_j$ ($j=1,\dots,k$, with $k<n$): you get one multiplier $\lambda_j$ per constraint, and the condition becomes
$$\frac{\partial}{\partial y_i}\Bigl(F+\textstyle\sum_j\lambda_jG_j\Bigr) - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{\partial}{\partial y_i'}\Bigl(F+\textstyle\sum_j\lambda_jG_j\Bigr) = 0 \qquad (i=1,\dots,n).$$

12.2 Finite (pointwise) subsidiary conditions

A different flavor of constraint. The isoperimetric problem's constraint, $K[y]=l$, is a single global piece of information — one number, extracted by integrating over the whole curve. Now consider a different kind of restriction: the curve must satisfy some algebraic relation at every single point, such as being confined to lie on a given surface $g(x,y,z)=0$. This is a different situation — it's not "one condition," it's "infinitely many conditions" (one for every $x$), so we should expect the multiplier to become a function of $x$, not a single constant.

Theorem 2

Let $J[y,z]=\int_a^b F(x,y,z,y',z')\,dx$, with admissible curves constrained to lie on the surface $g(x,y,z)=0$ and satisfy fixed boundary conditions $y(a)=A_1,y(b)=B_1,z(a)=A_2,z(b)=B_2$. If $J[y,z]$ has an extremum at a curve $y=y(x),z=z(x)$ along which $g_y,g_z$ are never simultaneously zero, then there is a function $\lambda(x)$ such that the curve satisfies
$$F_y+\lambda(x)g_y - \frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0, \qquad F_z+\lambda(x)g_z-\frac{d}{dx}F_{z'}=0. \tag{41}$$

Sketch of the proof, and why $\lambda$ is now a function. The proof strategy mirrors Theorem 1 closely: perturb $y$ and $z$ simultaneously near a single arbitrary point $x_1$, using the constraint $g(x,y^*,z^*)=0$ (which must hold at every $x$, including all the points near $x_1$ where we're perturbing) to solve for how much the $z$-perturbation must compensate for a given $y$-perturbation, via the implicit function theorem (assuming $g_z\ne0$ at $x_1$, which is where the "not simultaneously zero" hypothesis is used). Substituting this forced relationship back into $\Delta J$ and requiring the coefficient of the now-free $y$-perturbation to vanish, for every choice of $x_1$, produces a condition that can be written as
$$\frac{F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}}{g_y} = \frac{F_z-\frac{d}{dx}F_{z'}}{g_z} =: -\lambda(x),$$
i.e. exactly (41). Crucially: because we ran this argument separately at every point $x_1$, the resulting multiplier $\lambda$ is allowed to (and generally will) take a different value at each $x$ — hence it's a function $\lambda(x)$, not a single constant. Contrast this with the isoperimetric case, where the entire argument used exactly two points ($x_1$ arbitrary, $x_2$ fixed once and for all) and produced one single, fixed number $\lambda$. The general moral: one multiplier per independent global (integral) constraint; one multiplier field $\lambda(x)$ per pointwise (algebraic) constraint — since a pointwise constraint really is infinitely many separate conditions, one for every value of $x$, and Lagrange's method always assigns "one multiplier per condition."

Constraints of the pointwise type, when they involve only $y,z$ (not $y',z'$) directly, are called holonomic; ones that involve the derivatives (like $g(x,y,z,y',z')=0$) are called nonholonomic (a term from mechanics), and the book notes without proof that the identical machinery — replacing $F$ by $F+\lambda(x)g$ and writing Euler's equations for that — works in the nonholonomic case too.

Practical technique: eliminate the constraint instead. As in ordinary calculus (where a constraint $g(x_1,\dots,x_n)=0$ can sometimes just be solved for one variable in terms of the others, reducing an $n$-variable constrained problem to an unconstrained $(n-1)$-variable one), a holonomic constraint $g(x,y,z)=0$ can sometimes be solved directly for $z$ in terms of $x,y$, substituted into $F$, and the resulting unconstrained problem in the single function $y(x)$ solved by ordinary Euler's-equation methods (Chapter 1, Section 4). Both routes — Lagrange multipliers, or direct elimination — must give the same answer; which is more convenient depends on the specific problem (elimination is easier when $g$ solves cleanly for one variable; multipliers are easier when it doesn't, or when the symmetry of the problem is better preserved by not singling out one coordinate).

12.3 Two fully worked examples

Example 1 (isoperimetric: the largest area under a curve of given length)

Among all curves of length $l$ in the upper half-plane joining $(-a,0)$ to $(a,0)$, find the one that together with the segment $[-a,a]$ of the $x$-axis encloses the largest area.

We want to maximize $J[y]=\int_{-a}^a y\,dx$ (the area under the curve — a standard fact from calc 1/2) subject to $y(-a)=y(a)=0$ and the length constraint $K[y]=\int_{-a}^a\sqrt{1+y'^2}\,dx = l$. By Theorem 1, we form
$$J+\lambda K = \int_{-a}^a\bigl(y+\lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}\bigr)dx,$$
whose integrand $F=y+\lambda\sqrt{1+y'^2}$ doesn't depend on $x$ explicitly — Case 2 from Chapter 1! Actually, let's follow the book's slightly quicker route here: Euler's equation for this $F$ is
$$1 - \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\lambda y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}\right)=0 \implies \frac{d}{dx}\left(\frac{\lambda y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}}\right)=1 \implies \frac{\lambda y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2}} = x-C_1$$
(integrating both sides with respect to $x$ — since the right side is a pure derivative set equal to the constant $1$, one integration recovers $x$ plus a constant). Rearranging and squaring (carefully, tracking signs) leads, after simplification, to
$$(x-C_1)^2+(y-C_2)^2 = \lambda^2$$
— a family of circles. This matches the classical fact that the isoperimetric problem's solution is (an arc of) a circle. The three constants $C_1,C_2,\lambda$ are pinned down by the two boundary conditions $y(\pm a)=0$ and the length constraint $K[y]=l$.

Example 2 (finite constraint: geodesics on a sphere via Lagrange multipliers)

Among curves on the sphere $x^2+y^2+z^2=a^2$ joining two given points, find the shortest. This is the same geodesic problem we could set up directly via the first-fundamental-form method of Section 9.4 — but here's the alternative Lagrange-multiplier route, treating "lying on the sphere" as a finite subsidiary condition on a curve $y=y(x),z=z(x)$ in ordinary space, rather than reparametrizing the sphere with surface coordinates $u,v$ from scratch.

We minimize $J[y,z]=\int\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2}\,dx$ subject to $g(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2+z^2-a^2=0$. Form the auxiliary functional $\int\bigl[\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2}+\lambda(x)(x^2+y^2+z^2-a^2)\bigr]dx$ and apply (41): since $g_y=2y, g_z=2z$,
$$2y\lambda(x) - \frac{d}{dx}\frac{y'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2}} = 0, \qquad 2z\lambda(x)-\frac{d}{dx}\frac{z'}{\sqrt{1+y'^2+z'^2}}=0.$$
Solving this system (together with the constraint equation itself) gives a family of curves depending on four constants, pinned down by the two given endpoints — one can check that these turn out to be exactly the great circle arcs on the sphere, matching everyone's intuition about shortest paths on a globe. (We won't carry out the full explicit integration here — the point of this example is the setup, illustrating the Lagrange-multiplier route to a problem you could also attack, as in Section 9.4, by parametrizing the constraint surface directly and using the first-fundamental-form method instead. Both routes must agree, and comparing them is a good exercise in flexibility.)

The general lesson from these two routes to geodesics. The book makes an explicit remark worth internalizing: a geodesic problem can be attacked either by treating the surface constraint as a Lagrange-multiplier condition on a curve in ambient space (Section 12, this section), or by first eliminating the constraint via a direct surface parametrization $u,v$ and solving an unconstrained problem in those two surface coordinates (Section 9.4's method). This is a useful piece of problem-solving flexibility: whenever you have a choice between eliminating a constraint and multiplying it in, both work, and you should pick whichever route makes the resulting Euler equations easier to actually solve.

Chapter recap

Every one of this chapter's four sections took the exact skeleton from Chapter 1 (perturb → Taylor-expand → integrate by parts → invoke a fundamental-lemma-type argument) and applied it in a new setting: several independent unknown functions (where the key new idea was that independent perturbations can be varied one at a time, exactly like partial derivatives), curves given parametrically (where the key new idea was recognizing positive homogeneity of degree 1 as the precise condition for a functional to be "geometric," and understanding why the resulting Euler equations come out dependent on each other), functionals of higher derivatives (where repeated integration by parts produces the Euler–Poisson equation, an ODE of order $2n$ matched by $2n$ boundary conditions), and constrained problems (where the Lagrange multiplier idea from ordinary calculus transplants directly into function space, with a genuine and important distinction between a single constant multiplier for a global integral constraint and a whole multiplier function for a pointwise algebraic constraint). With all four of these tools now in hand — together with everything from Chapter 1 — you have essentially the complete "first-order" toolkit of the classical calculus of variations: the tools that answer "which curve is a candidate extremum?" Chapter 3 turns to a question we've only glimpsed once so far (in Chapter 1, Section 6): what happens to all of this when the endpoints themselves are allowed to move freely, in full generality?

Practice problems
Problem 1

1. (System of Euler equations, $n=2$.) Find the extremals of $J[y,z]=\int_0^{\pi/2}(y'^2+z'^2+2yz)\,dx$ with $y(0)=0,y(\pi/2)=1,z(0)=0,z(\pi/2)=1$.

Hint: Write out both equations of system (3): $F_y-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ gives $2z-2y''=0$, i.e. $y''=z$; similarly $z''=y$. This is a coupled linear system — differentiate one equation and substitute into the other to decouple it (you'll get a single 4th-order linear ODE for $y$ alone, solvable by the usual characteristic-equation method from an ODE course, or by calc-2-level techniques for linear recursions in the exponents $e^{\pm x}$).

Problem 2

2. (Parametric form and Euler's theorem on homogeneous functions.) Prove Euler's theorem: if $f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ is continuously differentiable and positive-homogeneous of degree $k$ (meaning $f(\lambda x_1,\dots,\lambda x_n)=\lambda^k f(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ for all $\lambda>0$), then $\sum_i x_i\,\partial f/\partial x_i = kf$.

Hint: Differentiate both sides of the homogeneity identity with respect to $\lambda$ (using the chain rule on the left side, since each $\lambda x_i$ depends on $\lambda$), then set $\lambda=1$. This single-variable-calculus trick (differentiate an identity in an auxiliary variable, then plug in a convenient value) is exactly how identity (16) in Section 10.4 gets proved, so working through this problem is directly useful preparation for that derivation.

Problem 3

3. (Euler–Poisson equation, first integral practice.) Show that the Euler equation for $\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(x,y,y',y'')\,dx$ has the first integral $F_{y'}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y''}=\text{const}$ if $F$ does not depend on $y$, and the first integral $F-y'\bigl(F_{y'}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y''}\bigr)-y''F_{y''}=\text{const}$ if $F$ does not depend on $x$.

Hint: For the first: the Euler–Poisson equation (22) with $n=2$ and $F_y\equiv0$ reads $-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}+\frac{d^2}{dx^2}F_{y''}=0$, i.e. $\frac{d}{dx}\bigl(F_{y'}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y''}\bigr)=0$ directly — integrate once. For the second: differentiate the claimed constant expression with respect to $x$ using the product rule on each term, substitute the Euler–Poisson equation for $F_y$ (there is no explicit $x$-dependence to worry about since $F$ doesn't depend on $x$), and check everything telescopes to zero — this is the same style of verification used for Case 2 in Chapter 1, just with one more layer of derivatives to track.

Problem 4

4. (Isoperimetric problem, full solve.) Find the extremals of $J[y]=\int_0^1(y'^2+x^2)\,dx$ subject to $y(0)=0,y(1)=0,$ and $\int_0^1 y^2\,dx=2$.

Hint: Form $F+\lambda G = y'^2+x^2+\lambda y^2$. Euler's equation: $2\lambda y - \frac{d}{dx}(2y')=0$, i.e. $y''=\lambda y$ — a familiar linear ODE whose character (oscillatory solutions $\sin,\cos$ versus exponential solutions $\sinh,\cosh$) depends on the sign of $\lambda$. Since $y(0)=y(1)=0$, think about which sign of $\lambda$ can actually produce a nonzero solution satisfying both boundary conditions (compare to the eigenvalue problems you may have seen for vibrating strings) — then use the area-style constraint $\int_0^1y^2\,dx=2$ to fix the remaining amplitude constant.

Problem 5

5. (Finite subsidiary condition, straightforward case.) Find the extremals of $J[y,z]=\int_{x_0}^{x_1}F(y',z')\,dx$ (no $x,y,z$ dependence at all, only the derivatives), given that $F_{y'y'}F_{z'z'}-(F_{y'z'})^2 \ne 0$ throughout.

Hint: This isn't a constrained problem in the Section 12 sense, but it's good practice for the "$n$ functions" system (3): both $F_y=0$ and $F_z=0$ automatically (no explicit $y,z$ dependence), so both Euler equations immediately reduce to $\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}=0$ and $\frac{d}{dx}F_{z'}=0$, i.e. $F_{y'}=C_1, F_{z'}=C_2$ — two equations in the two unknowns $y',z'$. The nonvanishing-Jacobian-type hypothesis given is exactly what guarantees (via the implicit function theorem — the same tool that underlies Theorem 2's proof in Section 12.2) that these two equations can be solved for constant values of $y'$ and $z'$ — from which you can read off the shape of every extremal without further work. (Book's answer: a family of straight lines in three dimensions — does your reasoning explain why that has to be the answer, even before doing any algebra?)
