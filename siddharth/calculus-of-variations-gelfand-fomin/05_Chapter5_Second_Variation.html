00Roadmap: necessary versus sufficient, and why that gap matters

Here's a fact worth sitting with before diving in: everything proved in Chapters 1 through 3 was a necessary condition. Euler's equation tells you a curve must satisfy a certain differential equation to be an extremum — but plenty of curves satisfy Euler's equation without being extrema at all (an inflection point of an ordinary function satisfies $f'=0$ too, without being a max or a min). We've been finding candidates, not verdicts.

Compare this to single-variable calculus. There, the necessary condition for a minimum is $f'(\xi)=0$; the matching sufficient condition is $f'(\xi)=0$ and $f''(\xi)>0$. The second derivative is what separates "flat point" from "actual minimum." This chapter builds the calculus-of-variations analogue of $f''$ — called the second variation $\delta^2J$ — and uses it to write down, for the first time in this book, conditions that actually guarantee a weak extremum rather than just narrowing down candidates for one.

The path there has several stops:

  • Section 24 sets up the necessary linear-algebra language (bilinear and quadratic functionals) and defines $\delta^2J$ precisely, by direct analogy with the ordinary-calculus fact that $f''\ge0$ is necessary for a minimum.
  • Section 25 computes $\delta^2J$ explicitly for the simplest variational problem, arriving at a clean quadratic functional $\int(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$, and extracts from it Legendre's necessary condition — a direct analogue of $f''\ge0$.
  • Section 26 studies that quadratic functional in its own right, introducing the single most important new idea in the chapter: conjugate points, and the theorem that positivity of $P$ plus the absence of conjugate points is exactly what makes the quadratic functional positive definite.
  • Section 27 ties this back to the original problem, producing Jacobi's necessary condition (no conjugate points in $(a,b)$) as the analogue of "$f''\ge0$ at every point," and gives several equivalent ways to picture what a conjugate point actually is.
  • Section 28 finally assembles a genuine sufficient condition — Legendre's condition strengthened to a strict inequality, plus Jacobi's condition strengthened to the closed interval — and proves it really does guarantee a weak minimum.
  • Section 29 redoes all of this for $n$ unknown functions, trading scalar $P,Q$ for symmetric matrices and single conjugate-point determinants for full $n\times n$ determinants.
  • Section 30 closes the loop by connecting the whole infinite-dimensional theory back to a finite-dimensional fact you already know from linear algebra: Sylvester's criterion for a quadratic form to be positive definite, recovered here as a limit of larger and larger matrices.

One theme worth tracking throughout: necessary conditions in this subject are always local — checkable one point at a time — while truly sufficient conditions (like "no conjugate points in the whole interval") are inescapably global, because they depend on how the interval $[a,b]$ relates to itself as a whole, not just on pointwise data. Section 25's discussion of great-circle arcs makes this distinction vivid, and it's worth watching for as the chapter unfolds.

24Quadratic Functionals. The Second Variation of a Functional
Background primer

A primer: bilinear and quadratic functionals

You've seen bilinear forms in linear algebra: a function $B(x,y)$ of two vectors $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^n$ that's linear in each argument separately, $B(x,y)=\sum_{i,k}b_{ik}\xi_i\eta_k$ in coordinates. Setting $y=x$ produces a quadratic form $A(x)=B(x,x)=\sum_{i,k}b_{ik}\xi_i\xi_k$. Everything here is the exact same idea, just replacing "vector in $\mathbb{R}^n$" with "element of a normed linear space" (which, as in Chapters 1–3, will usually mean a space of functions). A functional $B[x,y]$ depending on two elements $x,y$ of a normed linear space $\mathscr{R}$ is bilinear if it's linear in $y$ for fixed $x$, and linear in $x$ for fixed $y$:
$$B[x+y,z]=B[x,z]+B[y,z], \qquad B[\alpha x,y]=\alpha B[x,y],$$
and likewise in the second slot. Setting $y=x$ gives a quadratic functional $A[x]=B[x,x]$. Such an $A[x]$ is positive definite if $A[x]>0$ for every nonzero $x$.

Four examples to build intuition, moving from finite to infinite dimensions. $B[x,y]=\int_a^b x(t)y(t)\,dt$ is bilinear on the space $\mathscr{C}$ of continuous functions on $[a,b]$, with corresponding (positive definite — nothing but the zero function integrates its own square to zero) quadratic functional $A[x]=\int_a^bx^2(t)\,dt$. More generally $B[x,y]=\int_a^b\alpha(t)x(t)y(t)\,dt$, for a fixed function $\alpha(t)$, gives $A[x]=\int_a^b\alpha(t)x^2(t)\,dt$, positive definite whenever $\alpha(t)>0$ throughout $[a,b]$. A richer example on the space $\mathscr{D}_1$ of continuously differentiable functions is
$$A[x] = \int_a^b\bigl[\alpha(t)x^2(t)+\beta(t)x(t)x'(t)+\gamma(t)x'^2(t)\bigr]\,dt,$$
which mixes a function and its derivative — this is precisely the shape the second variation of a variational problem will turn out to have. And a fourth example, $B[x,y]=\int_a^b\int_a^bK(s,t)x(s)y(t)\,ds\,dt$ for a fixed two-variable kernel $K(s,t)$, shows the idea generalizes further still, though we won't need this last form here.

Defining the second variation. Recall from Chapter 1 how the (first) variation was defined: a functional $J[y]$ is differentiable at $y$ if its increment splits as
$$\Delta J[h] = J[y+h]-J[y] = \varphi[h]+\varepsilon\|h\|,$$
where $\varphi[h]$ is linear and $\varepsilon\to0$ as $\|h\|\to0$; the linear piece $\varphi[h]$ is the variation $\delta J[h]$. This is exactly the functional-space analogue of writing an ordinary function's increment as $\Delta f = f'(\xi)\Delta\xi + o(\Delta\xi)$ — the derivative is whatever's left after stripping off everything that vanishes faster than linearly.

Push the analogy one step further, to $\Delta f = f'(\xi)\Delta\xi+\frac12f''(\xi)\Delta\xi^2+o(\Delta\xi^2)$. A functional $J[y]$ is twice differentiable if its increment can be written
$$\Delta J[h] = \varphi_1[h]+\varphi_2[h]+\varepsilon\|h\|^2,$$
where $\varphi_1[h]$ is linear (this is just $\delta J[h]$ again), $\varphi_2[h]$ is a quadratic functional, and $\varepsilon\to0$ as $\|h\|\to0$. The quadratic piece $\varphi_2[h]$ is the second variation, written $\delta^2J[h]$. (As with the first variation, it can be shown to be uniquely determined — the same style of argument as Chapter 1's uniqueness proof for $\delta J$ applies here essentially unchanged. From now on we assume every functional in sight is twice differentiable.)

Theorem 1. A necessary condition for $J[y]$ to have a minimum at $y=\hat y$ is that $\delta^2J[h]\ge0$ for all admissible $h$ (with $\le0$ for a maximum).

Proof. By definition,
$$\Delta J[h] = \delta J[h]+\delta^2J[h]+\varepsilon\|h\|^2.$$
At a minimum, $\delta J[h]=0$ for every admissible $h$ (Chapter 1's necessary condition), so this collapses to $\Delta J[h]=\delta^2J[h]+\varepsilon\|h\|^2$. For sufficiently small $\|h\|$, the sign of $\Delta J[h]$ must therefore match the sign of $\delta^2J[h]$ — and if $\delta^2J[h_0]<0$ for some admissible $h_0$, then $\delta^2J[\alpha h_0]=\alpha^2\delta^2J[h_0]$ (quadratic functionals scale like squares) can be made negative for arbitrarily small $\|\alpha h_0\|$ just by shrinking $\alpha$, forcing $\Delta J$ negative arbitrarily close to $\hat y$ — contradicting that $\hat y$ is a minimum. $\blacksquare$

This is exactly the calculus-of-variations version of "$f''(\xi)\ge0$ is necessary for a minimum," and just as in ordinary calculus, it's not sufficient — $f''(\xi)\ge0$ allows $f''(\xi)=0$, which doesn't rule out an inflection point in disguise. To get an actual sufficient condition, we need something a shade stronger than mere nonnegativity:

Definition. A quadratic functional $\varphi_2[h]$ on a normed linear space is strongly positive if there's a constant $k>0$ with $\varphi_2[h]\ge k\|h\|^2$ for all $h$.

(In a finite-dimensional space, strong positivity turns out to be equivalent to ordinary positive definiteness — a fact worth filing away, since it means the distinction we're about to draw is a purely infinite-dimensional phenomenon, with no finite-dimensional counterpart to build intuition from directly.)

Theorem 2. If $\delta J[h]$ vanishes at $y=\hat y$ and $\delta^2J[h]$ is strongly positive there, then $J[y]$ has a minimum at $y=\hat y$.

Proof. As above, $\Delta J[h]=\delta^2J[h]+\varepsilon\|h\|^2\ge k\|h\|^2+\varepsilon\|h\|^2 = (k+\varepsilon)\|h\|^2$. Since $\varepsilon\to0$ as $\|h\|\to0$, there's some $\varepsilon_1>0$ with $|\varepsilon|<\frac12k$ whenever $\|h\|<\varepsilon_1$ — and then $\Delta J[h]>\frac12k\|h\|^2>0$ for all sufficiently small nonzero $h$, exactly what a (weak) minimum requires. $\blacksquare$

So the game plan for the rest of the chapter is set: compute $\delta^2J$ explicitly for actual variational problems, then find checkable conditions guaranteeing it's strongly positive.

25The Formula for the Second Variation. Legendre's Condition

Take the simplest variational problem, $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$ with fixed endpoints $y(a)=A,\ y(b)=B$, and an admissible perturbation $h(x)$ satisfying $h(a)=h(b)=0$. Taylor's theorem with remainder, applied to the two-variable increment $F(x,y+h,y'+h')-F(x,y,y')$ and kept to second order this time (rather than stopping at first order, as in Chapters 1–3), gives
$$\Delta J[h] = \int_a^b(F_yh+F_{y'}h')\,dx + \frac12\int_a^b\bigl(\bar F_{yy}h^2+2\bar F_{yy'}hh'+\bar F_{y'y'}h'^2\bigr)\,dx,$$
where the overbars mean the derivatives are evaluated along intermediate curves $y+\theta h,\ y'+\theta h'$ for some $0<\theta<1$ (this is exactly the remainder-term bookkeeping from ordinary two-variable Taylor's theorem, just carried along in the integral). Replacing the overbarred (intermediate-point) derivatives by the corresponding derivatives evaluated at $(x,y(x),y'(x))$ itself introduces an error that — because $F_{yy},F_{yy'},F_{y'y'}$ are continuous — is of higher order than $\|h\|_1^2$, and can be absorbed into the $\varepsilon\|h\|^2$ remainder term from the definition in Section 24. The upshot: the first integral above is exactly $\delta J[h]$ (matching Chapter 1), and the second is $\delta^2J[h]$:
$$\delta^2J[h] = \frac12\int_a^b\bigl(F_{yy}h^2+2F_{yy'}hh'+F_{y'y'}h'^2\bigr)\,dx.$$

Cleaning up via integration by parts. The middle, "cross" term $2F_{yy'}hh'$ can be simplified using integration by parts and the boundary condition $h(a)=h(b)=0$:
$$\int_a^b2F_{yy'}hh'\,dx = -\int_a^b\left(\frac{d}{dx}F_{yy'}\right)h^2\,dx$$
(this is the same "integrate a $hh'$-type product to turn it into a pure $h^2$" trick used repeatedly in Chapter 1). Substituting this in and collecting terms gives the tidy form we'll use for the rest of the chapter:
$$\boxed{\delta^2J[h] = \int_a^b\bigl(Ph'^2+Qh^2\bigr)\,dx,} \qquad P=\frac12F_{y'y'}, \qquad Q=\frac12\left(F_{yy}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{yy'}\right). \tag{10}$$
So no matter how complicated the original $F$ was, the second variation of the simplest problem is always a quadratic functional of this one specific shape — built from just two functions $P(x),Q(x)$, both derived from $F$ evaluated along the extremal being studied.

A useful companion formula. If $y=y(x)$ is actually an extremal (so $\delta J[h]=0$ identically), a slightly more refined bookkeeping of the same Taylor expansion — this time tracking the remainder terms explicitly rather than just noting they're higher-order — gives
$$\Delta J[h] = \int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx + \int_a^b(\xi h^2+\eta hh')\,dx, \qquad \xi,\eta\to0 \text{ as } \|h\|_1\to0. \tag{12}$$
This formula will resurface when we prove sufficiency in Section 28 — it's the precise statement that $\Delta J$ and $\delta^2J$ agree up to a controllably small correction.

Extracting a necessary condition directly from (10). We already know (Theorem 1, Section 24) that $\delta^2J[h]\ge0$ is necessary for a minimum. Can we translate this abstract nonnegativity requirement on the integral (10) into something checkable pointwise? Here's the key qualitative idea, worth internalizing before the formal argument: given $h(a)=0$, if $h(x)$ is small throughout $[a,b]$, its derivative $h'(x)$ need not be small — you can build a function that stays tiny in value but oscillates wildly in slope (picture a thin, rapidly-wiggling spike). The converse fails, though: a small derivative does force a small function. This asymmetry means the term $Ph'^2$ can dominate the quadratic functional (10) in a way that $Qh^2$ never can reciprocate — so it's the sign of $P$, not $Q$, that should control whether (10) can ever go negative.

Lemma

A necessary condition for $\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$, over all $h\in\mathscr{D}_1(a,b)$ with $h(a)=h(b)=0$, to be nonnegative is that $P(x)\ge0$ throughout $[a,b]$.

Proof. Suppose not: $P(x_0)=-2\beta<0$ for some $x_0$ and $\beta>0$. By continuity, $P(x)<-\beta$ on some small interval $[x_0-\alpha,x_0+\alpha]\subset[a,b]$. Build a bump function concentrated exactly on that interval:
$$h(x) = \begin{cases}\sin^2\dfrac{\pi(x-x_0)}{\alpha} & x_0-\alpha\le x\le x_0+\alpha \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,}\end{cases}$$
which is continuously differentiable (it and its derivative both taper to zero at the interval's edges) and vanishes outside a shrinking neighborhood of $x_0$. A direct computation of the integral over just that small interval gives
$$\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx < -\frac{2\beta\pi^2}{\alpha}+2M\alpha, \qquad M=\max_{a\le x\le b}|Q(x)|,$$
(the first term comes from bounding $P<-\beta$ against the explicitly computable $\int h'^2$, which scales like $1/\alpha$ because the bump gets steeper as it narrows; the second bounds $Qh^2$ crudely). For $\alpha$ small enough, the $-2\beta\pi^2/\alpha$ term — which blows up toward $-\infty$ as $\alpha\to0$ — dominates the $2M\alpha$ term — which shrinks to $0$ — making the whole expression negative. $\blacksquare$

Theorem (Legendre)

A necessary condition for $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$, with fixed endpoints, to have a minimum at $y=y(x)$, is that $F_{y'y'}\ge0$ at every point of the curve.

This follows immediately: $\delta^2J[h]\ge0$ is necessary (Theorem 1, Section 24), and by the Lemma this forces $P=\frac12F_{y'y'}\ge0$ throughout.

Why the naive strengthening doesn't work. Legendre himself tried to show that the strict inequality $F_{y'y'}>0$ — the strengthened Legendre condition — is sufficient for a (weak) minimum, by attempting to complete the square in (10). His idea: introduce an arbitrary differentiable function $w(x)$, and note that
$$0 = \int_a^b\frac{d}{dx}(wh^2)\,dx = \int_a^b(w'h^2+2whh')\,dx$$
(again using $h(a)=h(b)=0$), so adding this — a total-integral-zero quantity — to (10) doesn't change its value, but does change its integrand:
$$\delta^2J[h] = \int_a^b\bigl[Ph'^2+2whh'+(Q+w')h^2\bigr]\,dx.$$
If $w$ could be chosen to make this a perfect square, say $P\left(h'+\frac{w}{P}h\right)^2$, positivity would follow immediately (since $P>0$). Matching this expansion against the bracketed expression forces $w$ to satisfy
$$P(Q+w') = w^2, \tag{20}$$
a nonlinear first-order ODE (a Riccati equation — we'll meet this type of equation properly in Section 26). Unfortunately, as Legendre discovered, (20) is only guaranteed to be solvable locally; on a long enough interval it can fail to have a solution defined on all of $[a,b]$. (A concrete instance: if $P=-1,Q=1$, equation (20) becomes $w'+1+w^2=0$, solved by $w=\tan(c-x)$ — but tangent has vertical asymptotes, so if $b-a>\pi$, no choice of $c$ keeps $w$ finite across the whole interval.)

A structural reason the strengthened Legendre condition can never be enough by itself. Here's an argument, independent of the failed completing-the-square attempt, that settles the matter for good: the condition $F_{y'y'}[x,y(x),y'(x)]>0$, like Euler's equation itself, is local — it's a statement about the curve at each individual point, not about the curve as a whole. And no purely local condition, applied pointwise, can ever be sufficient for a global extremum property, because of the following. If a local condition holds along curve $AB$ and also along curve $BC$, it automatically holds along the combined curve $AC$ — but a functional having an extremum on each piece separately doesn't at all imply it has an extremum on the whole. The book's example: on a sphere, a great-circle arc that's less than a half-circle really is the shortest path between its endpoints, but a great-circle arc of more than a half-circle is an extremal (it satisfies every local condition, including the strengthened Legendre condition) without being shortest — you can always find a shorter path once you've gone more than halfway around. So Legendre's condition, however strengthened, is fundamentally the wrong kind of statement to be sufficient on its own — we need to bring in something unavoidably global, and that's exactly what conjugate points will supply.

26Analysis of the Quadratic Functional $\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$

Rather than pursuing the original problem directly, it pays to study the quadratic functional
$$\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx, \qquad h(a)=h(b)=0, \tag{23}$$
as an object in its own right, forgetting for now that $P,Q$ came from formula (10). We already know $P(x)\ge0$ throughout is necessary for (23) to be $\ge0$ for every admissible $h$; this section finds conditions that are both necessary and sufficient for the strict statement — (23) positive definite — under the standing assumption $P(x)>0$.

The Euler equation of (23), and conjugate points. Since (23) is itself a functional of the form $\int F(x,h,h')\,dx$ with $F=Ph'^2+Qh^2$, it has its own Euler equation:
$$-\frac{d}{dx}(Ph')+Qh=0. \tag{26}$$
This is a linear, homogeneous, second-order ODE — so it has the constant function $h\equiv0$ as an obvious solution satisfying $h(a)=h(c)=0$ for any $c$. The interesting question is whether it has any other, nontrivial solution satisfying the same kind of vanishing condition at two points.

Definition

The point $\tilde a\ (\ne a)$ is conjugate to $a$ if equation (26) has a solution that vanishes at both $x=a$ and $x=\tilde a$ without being identically zero.

(If $h$ is such a nontrivial solution, so is any nonzero multiple $Ch$ — solutions of a linear homogeneous ODE form a vector space — so it's convenient to normalize by requiring $h'(a)=1$, which pins down a single specific solution to track.)

Theorem 1 (the positive case)

If $P(x)>0$ throughout $[a,b]$, and $[a,b]$ contains no point conjugate to $a$, then (23) is positive definite for all $h$ with $h(a)=h(b)=0$.

Proof. The strategy — completing the square, but this time made to actually work globally — mirrors Legendre's failed attempt in Section 25, with one twist that saves it. Add $\frac{d}{dx}(wh^2)$ to the integrand of (23) for an arbitrary differentiable $w(x)$ (this changes nothing, by the same total-derivative argument as before), and look for a $w$ making the result a perfect square: exactly as before, this requires $w$ to solve the matrix — here scalar — Riccati equation $P(Q+w')=w^2$. The trick for actually solving this equation on the whole interval is a substitution:
$$w = -\frac{u'}{u}P, \tag{32}$$
for a new unknown function $u$. Substituting (32) into the Riccati equation and simplifying (a computation the book carries out directly) reduces it to
$$-\frac{d}{dx}(Pu')+Qu=0, \tag{33}$$
which is nothing but equation (26) itself, now solved for $u$ instead of $h$! In other words, the nonlinear Riccati equation for $w$ has been converted, via the one substitution (32), into the linear equation (26) we already understand.

Now: if $[a,b]$ has no point conjugate to $a$, then the solution $u(x)$ of (26) satisfying $u(a)=0,u'(a)=1$ never vanishes again on $(a,b]$ — that's precisely what "no conjugate point" means. So $u(x)\ne0$ throughout $(a,b]$, meaning $w=-u'P/u$ from (32) is well-defined (no division by zero) on the whole interval, except possibly right at $x=a$ — a removable difficulty the book handles with a small continuity argument (starting the construction from a point $a-\varepsilon$ slightly to the left, using that "no conjugate points" is an open condition that survives small perturbations). With $w$ now defined across all of $[a,b]$, the integrand of (23) becomes the perfect square $P\left(h'+\frac{w}{P}h\right)^2\ge0$ everywhere, so (23) itself is $\ge0$. And it can only equal zero if $h'+\frac{w}{P}h\equiv0$ throughout — a first-order linear ODE for $h$, whose only solution satisfying $h(a)=0$ is $h\equiv0$ (uniqueness for first-order linear equations) — establishing strict positivity for every nonzero admissible $h$. $\blacksquare$

The converse. It's also true — and this is the harder, more interesting direction — that positive definiteness of (23) forces the absence of conjugate points:

Theorem 2

If $P(x)>0$ throughout $[a,b]$ and (23) is positive definite for all admissible $h$, then $[a,b]$ contains no point conjugate to $a$.

Proof sketch. The idea is to interpolate continuously between (23) and the trivial functional $\int_a^b h'^2\,dx$ (for which "no conjugate points" is obvious — its Euler equation is just $h''=0$, whose only solution vanishing at two points is $h\equiv0$), via the one-parameter family
$$\int_a^b\bigl[t(Ph'^2+Qh^2)+(1-t)h'^2\bigr]\,dx, \qquad 0\le t\le1,$$
which is positive definite for every $t$ (at $t=1$ by hypothesis; the two terms being combined are each nonnegative-friendly, so intermediate $t$ inherits positivity too). Track the normalized solution $h(x,t)$ of the Euler equation for this family (with $h(a,t)=0,\ h_x(a,t)=1$ for every $t$) as $t$ sweeps continuously from $0$ to $1$. If a conjugate point $\tilde a$ appeared somewhere in $(a,b)$ at $t=1$, the curve of points $(x,t)$ where $h(x,t)=0$ would have to "arrive" at $(\tilde a,1)$ from somewhere — and a careful case analysis (the book works through five geometrically distinct ways this arriving curve could behave, in a picture worth sketching for yourself: it can't terminate inside the rectangle, can't reach the far edge $x=b$, can't cross back to $t=1$ at a second point, can't come from $t=0$ where the only zero is at $x=a$ itself, and can't sneak up along $x=a$) shows every one of the possibilities leads to a contradiction with either the continuous dependence of $h$ on $t$, the positive-definiteness hypothesis, or the uniqueness theorem for the underlying linear ODE. So no such conjugate point can exist. (We record the logical shape of this argument here without repeating every case in full — the payoff, Theorem 3 below, is what matters going forward.) $\blacksquare$

Combining the two directions, and their nonstrict cousins Theorem $2'$ (nonnegativity of (23), rather than strict positivity, permits only $\tilde a=b$ itself as an interior-excluded exception):

Theorem 3

If $P(x)>0$ throughout $[a,b]$, then $\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$ is positive definite for all $h$ with $h(a)=h(b)=0$ if and only if $[a,b]$ contains no point conjugate to $a$.

This is the central result of the chapter: an "if and only if" trading an infinite-dimensional positivity question for a concrete, checkable geometric one — does a specific, explicitly solvable linear ODE have a second zero?

27Jacobi's Necessary Condition. More on Conjugate Points

Now translate Section 26's abstract result back to the original problem. Since $\delta^2J[h]=\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$ with $P=\frac12F_{y'y'},\ Q=\frac12(F_{yy}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{yy'})$ evaluated along a specific extremal $y=y(x)$, equation (26) — the Euler equation of the second variation — earns its own name here:

Definition 1

The equation $-\frac{d}{dx}(Ph')+Qh=0$ is the Jacobi equation of the original functional $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$.

Definition 2

The point $\tilde a$ is conjugate to $a$ with respect to the functional $J$ if it's conjugate to $a$ in the sense of Section 26, applied to this particular Jacobi equation.

Theorem (Jacobi's necessary condition)

If the extremal $y=y(x)$ minimizes $J[y]$, and $F_{y'y'}>0$ along this extremal, then the open interval $(a,b)$ contains no point conjugate to $a$.

Proof. Combine two facts already in hand: nonnegativity of $\delta^2J$ is necessary for a minimum (Theorem 1, Section 24), and — by the nonnegative-version companion to Theorem 3 (called Theorem $2'$ in the book) — a nonnegative quadratic functional of the form (23) with $P>0$ can have no conjugate point in the open interval $(a,b)$ (a conjugate point exactly at $b$ is not excluded by mere nonnegativity — only by strict positive definiteness). $\blacksquare$

Where Jacobi's equation actually comes from, a second way — and why it deserves the name "variational equation." Here's a more concrete, hands-on way to arrive at the same equation, worth walking through because it explains what a conjugate point really represents geometrically. Suppose $y(x)$ solves Euler's equation, and consider a neighboring extremal $y^*(x)=y(x)+h(x)$, also required to solve Euler's equation. Substitute $y+h$ into $F_y(x,y+h,y'+h')-\frac{d}{dx}F_{y'}(x,y+h,y'+h')=0$ and Taylor-expand around $h=0$, using that $y(x)$ itself already solves the unperturbed Euler equation to cancel the zeroth-order terms. What survives, to first order in $h$, is exactly
$$\left(F_{yy}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{yy'}\right)h - \frac{d}{dx}(F_{y'y'}h') = 0,$$
i.e. the Jacobi equation once more. This general phenomenon — linearizing a nonlinear differential equation around one particular solution to get a linear equation describing nearby solutions — is called forming the variational equation, and it shows up throughout differential equations, not just here: it's the same "first-order sensitivity" idea used to study, say, how orbits shift under a small perturbation to a physical system. So Jacobi's equation isn't some special-purpose auxiliary construction — it's precisely the equation governing how a family of nearby extremals spreads apart from the extremal you started with, and a conjugate point is exactly the place where a spreading family sneaks back together again.

Several equivalent pictures of a conjugate point. This spreading-apart language motivates two further, geometrically vivid (and provably equivalent) definitions of a conjugate point, beyond the original "Jacobi equation has a second zero":

Definition 3. $\tilde a$ is conjugate to $a$ if, for any neighboring extremal $y^*(x)$ through the same initial point $(a,y(a))$, the difference $y^*(x)-y(x)$, right at $x=\tilde a$, is smaller than $\|y^*-y\|_1$ by more than a linear factor — i.e. the two nearby extremals come back together at $\tilde a$ faster than they separated anywhere else.

Definition 4. $\tilde M=(\tilde a,y(\tilde a))$ is conjugate to $M=(a,y(a))$ if it's the limiting point of intersection, as $\|y^*-y\|_1\to0$, of the extremal $y(x)$ with a family of neighboring extremals through the same starting point $M$ — i.e., $\tilde M$ is where the whole pencil of extremals fanning out from $M$ appears to refocus, in the limit of extremals arbitrarily close to $y$.

The equivalence of these (proved by tracking the same normalized solution $h(x)$ of Jacobi's equation through each definition in turn) confirms the picture: a conjugate point is where nearby extremals emanating from a common starting point come back together.

A vivid worked example: geodesics on a sphere. Great-circle arcs are exactly the extremals of the arc-length functional on a sphere. Fix a starting point $M$; every great circle through $M$ passes through the same second point — its antipode $\tilde M$, the diametrically opposite point on the sphere. So the conjugate point of $M$, for the arc-length functional on a sphere, is exactly its antipode, no matter which direction the extremal initially heads — a strikingly clean picture of "the whole pencil of extremals from $M$ refocuses at $\tilde M$." (This all-directions refocusing is special to the sphere's constant curvature; deform the sphere slightly — say, into a nearby ellipsoid — and the refocusing point becomes different for each initial direction.) This example also explains, retroactively, the great-circle counterexample from Section 25: a great-circle arc longer than a half-circle has already passed its conjugate point (the antipode, reached at the halfway mark) by the time it ends — so Jacobi's necessary condition fails for it, exactly the mechanism (rather than a mere coincidence) behind why such an arc fails to minimize.

Summary of necessary conditions collected so far. If $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$ has a weak extremum at $y=y(x)$, then: (1) $y(x)$ satisfies Euler's equation; (2) $F_{y'y'}\ge0$ along $y(x)$ for a minimum (Legendre, Section 25); (3) $(a,b)$ contains no point conjugate to $a$ (Jacobi, this section). These three, together, are the complete necessary-condition toolkit of classical variational theory — and, remarkably, strengthening exactly two of them (Legendre's to strict, Jacobi's to the closed interval) turns out to be sufficient, which is exactly Section 28's business.

28Sufficient Conditions for a Weak Extremum
Theorem

Suppose that for an admissible curve $y=y(x)$, the functional $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$, with $y(a)=A,\ y(b)=B$, satisfies: (1) $y(x)$ is an extremal; (2) $P(x)=\frac12F_{y'y'}[x,y(x),y'(x)]>0$ throughout $[a,b]$ (the strengthened Legendre condition); (3) $[a,b]$ contains no point conjugate to $a$ (the strengthened Jacobi condition — note the closed interval, in contrast to Jacobi's necessary condition's open interval). Then $J[y]$ has a weak minimum at $y=y(x)$.

Proof. The strategy: show $\delta^2J[h]$ is strongly positive (Section 24's Theorem 2 then finishes the job). Since $[a,b]$ has no conjugate points and $P(x)>0$ on the closed interval, continuity lets us find a slightly larger interval $[a,b+\varepsilon]$ on which $P$ stays positive and which still has no points conjugate to $a$ (both "no conjugate point" and "$P>0$" are open conditions, stable under small enough extension of the interval — this is exactly why the strengthened, closed-interval hypothesis matters: it buys the wiggle room to extend past $b$ at all).

Now consider the auxiliary quadratic functional
$$\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx - \alpha^2\int_a^bh'^2\,dx, \tag{45}$$
for a small parameter $\alpha$, with Euler equation $-\frac{d}{dx}[(P-\alpha^2)h']+Qh=0$. Because $P-\alpha^2$ stays positive on $[a,b+\varepsilon]$ for small enough $\alpha$, and because the solution of this Euler equation satisfying $h(a)=0,h'(a)=1$ depends continuously on $\alpha$ (a standard fact about solutions of ODEs depending on a parameter), that solution — which for $\alpha=0$ has no zero in $(a,b]$ by hypothesis — still has no zero in $(a,b]$ for $\alpha$ small enough. By Theorem 1 of Section 26, applied with $P-\alpha^2$ in place of $P$, this makes (45) positive definite for all sufficiently small $\alpha$ — meaning there's a constant $c>0$ with
$$\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx > c\int_a^bh'^2\,dx$$
for all admissible $h$. Since $h(a)=0$, the Schwarz inequality gives $h^2(x)=\left(\int_a^xh'\,dt\right)^2\le(x-a)\int_a^xh'^2\,dt\le(x-a)\int_a^bh'^2\,dt$, and integrating over $x$ shows $\int_a^bh^2\,dx\le\frac{(b-a)^2}{2}\int_a^bh'^2\,dx$ — meaning $\int_a^bh'^2\,dx$ controls $\int_a^bh^2\,dx$ too, and hence controls $\|h\|_1^2$ altogether up to a fixed constant. Chaining these two facts together shows $\delta^2J[h]=\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$ is strongly positive after all, in the sense of Section 24's definition.

Finally, recall the refined formula (12) from Section 25: since $y(x)$ is an extremal,
$$\Delta J[h] = \int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx + \int_a^b(\xi h^2+\eta hh')\,dx, \qquad \xi,\eta\to0 \text{ as } \|h\|_1\to0.$$
Using the same Schwarz-inequality trick as just above to bound the second integral by $\varepsilon\cdot\text{const}\cdot\int_a^bh'^2\,dx$ for arbitrarily small $\varepsilon$ (once $\|h\|_1$ is small enough), and combining with the strong-positivity bound on the first integral, gives $\Delta J[h]>0$ for all sufficiently small nonzero admissible $h$ — precisely a weak minimum. $\blacksquare$

Compare the three necessary conditions from Section 27's summary against these three sufficient ones: Euler's equation is unchanged, Legendre's condition is strengthened from $\ge0$ to $>0$, and Jacobi's condition is strengthened from "$(a,b)$ open" to "$[a,b]$ closed" — the exact same flavor of tightening that turns $f'(\xi)=0,f''(\xi)\ge0$ (necessary) into $f'(\xi)=0,f''(\xi)>0$ (sufficient) in ordinary calculus.

29Generalization to $n$ Unknown Functions

Every idea from Sections 24–28 carries over to $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y')\,dx$ with $y=(y_1,\dots,y_n)$ a vector of $n$ unknown functions — the scalar $P,Q$ simply become symmetric $n\times n$ matrices, and the single conjugate-point condition becomes a determinant condition. We'll move through this more briskly, flagging exactly where a real new phenomenon (rather than mere relabeling) shows up.

29.1 The second variation. With $h=(h_1,\dots,h_n)$ satisfying $h(a)=h(b)=0$, an entirely parallel Taylor-expansion argument gives
$$\delta^2J[h] = \frac12\int_a^b\left[\sum_{i,k}F_{y_iy_k}h_ih_k+2\sum_{i,k}F_{y_iy_k'}h_ih_k'+\sum_{i,k}F_{y_i'y_k'}h_i'h_k'\right]dx.$$
Introducing the matrices $F_{yy}=\|F_{y_iy_k}\|,\ F_{yy'}=\|F_{y_iy_k'}\|,\ F_{y'y'}=\|F_{y_i'y_k'}\|$, and writing $(u,v)=\sum_iu_iv_i$ for the ordinary vector dot product, this compresses to
$$\delta^2J[h] = \frac12\int_a^b\bigl[(F_{yy}h,h)+2(F_{yy'}h,h')+(F_{y'y'}h',h')\bigr]dx,$$
and the same integration-by-parts trick as in Section 25 (now applied component-by-component) reduces this to
$$\delta^2J[h] = \int_a^b\bigl[(Ph',h')+(Qh,h)\bigr]dx, \qquad P=\tfrac12F_{y'y'}, \qquad Q=\tfrac12\left(F_{yy}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{yy'}\right), \tag{54}$$
where now $P(x),Q(x)$ are symmetric $n\times n$ matrices (symmetric because $F_{y_iy_k'}=F_{y_ky_i'}$ and $F_{y_i'y_k'}$ is automatically symmetric, by equality of mixed partials, given enough smoothness of $F$). Theorem 1 records the matrix analogue of the scalar Lemma from Section 25: a necessary condition for (54) to be $\ge0$ is that the matrix $P(x)$ be nonnegative definite at every point (i.e. $(P(x)\alpha,\alpha)\ge0$ for every vector $\alpha$) — the multidimensional Legendre condition.

29.2 Investigating the quadratic functional (54). Its Euler equations (a system now, one per unknown function) are
$$-\frac{d}{dx}(Ph')+Qh=0. \tag{56}$$
To define a conjugate point in this vector setting, take $n$ linearly independent solutions of (56), $h^{(1)},\dots,h^{(n)}$, distinguished by the initial conditions $h^{(i)}(a)=0$ and $h^{(i)}_k{}'(a)=\delta_{ik}$ (the $i$-th solution's derivative vector starts out as the $i$-th standard basis vector — this is the vector generalization of the single normalized solution used in Section 26). Arrange these $n$ solution-vectors as the rows of an $n\times n$ matrix, itself a function of $x$; the point $\tilde a$ is conjugate to $a$ if the determinant of this matrix vanishes at $x=\tilde a$ — i.e., if some nontrivial linear combination of the $n$ basic solutions manages to return to zero at $\tilde a$, even though no single one of them individually needs to.

Theorem 2

(the positive analogue of Section 26's Theorem 1): if $P$ is positive definite throughout $[a,b]$ and $[a,b]$ has no conjugate point, then (54) is positive definite. The proof again completes the square, this time via a matrix Riccati equation $Q+W'=WP^{-1}W$ for an unknown symmetric matrix function $W(x)$, solved by the substitution $W=-PU'U^{-1}$ for a matrix $U(x)$ — which converts the matrix Riccati equation into the linear matrix equation $-\frac{d}{dx}(PU')+QU=0$, exactly the matrix form of (56), so the same "no conjugate points guarantees the linear equation's solution stays invertible, hence $W$ stays well-defined" argument as the scalar case goes through, using the matrix square root $P^{1/2}$ (which exists and is symmetric and invertible, since $P$ is positive definite) to write the resulting perfect-square integrand as $\left(P^{1/2}h'+P^{-1/2}Wh,\ P^{1/2}h'+P^{-1/2}Wh\right)$.

Theorem 3

is the converse (positive definiteness of (54) forces no conjugate points), proved by the same continuous-deformation-in-a-parameter-$t$ argument as Section 26's Theorem 2, and Theorem 4 combines both directions into the vector version of Section 26's central "if and only if" result. 29.3–29.4 then repeat Sections 27–28's arguments essentially verbatim, with matrices in place of scalars throughout, to arrive at the vector forms of Jacobi's necessary condition (Theorem 5) and the full sufficient-condition package (Theorem 6). The real new content in this generalization is entirely contained in the shift from "a single solution has a second zero" to "a determinant of $n$ solutions vanishes" — everything else is bookkeeping, carried out faithfully component by component.

30Connection between Jacobi's Condition and the Theory of Quadratic Forms

This closing section answers a question you might already be asking: conjugate points and Jacobi's equation feel like a fairly specialized, calculus-of-variations-specific piece of machinery — is there a more familiar, purely linear-algebra fact hiding underneath? There is, and making the connection explicit is worthwhile both for its own sake and because it shows the whole theory of this chapter as a faithful, carefully-taken-limit generalization of something you already trust completely.

Background primer

A primer, if it's rusty: Sylvester's criterion

A quadratic form $\sum_{i,k}a_{ik}\xi_i\xi_k$ (with $a_{ik}=a_{ki}$) on $\mathbb{R}^n$ is positive definite exactly when all its descending principal minors — the determinants of the top-left $1\times1$, $2\times2$, ..., $n\times n$ submatrices of $\|a_{ik}\|$ — are positive. This is a standard tool for checking positive definiteness without computing eigenvalues.

Discretizing the quadratic functional. By Theorem 3 of Section 26, $\int_a^b(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$ (with $P>0$, $h(a)=h(b)=0$) is positive definite exactly when $[a,b]$ has no point conjugate to $a$ — think of this integral as an "infinite-dimensional quadratic form." To connect it to the finite-dimensional Sylvester criterion, approximate it by an ordinary finite-dimensional quadratic form: partition $[a,b]$ into $n+1$ equal pieces of width $\Delta x=(b-a)/(n+1)$ via points $a=x_0,x_1,\dots,x_n,x_{n+1}=b$, and approximate the derivative $h'$ by the forward difference quotient $(h_{i+1}-h_i)/\Delta x$, giving the finite-dimensional quadratic form
$$\sum_{i=0}^n\left[P_i\left(\frac{h_{i+1}-h_i}{\Delta x}\right)^2+Q_ih_i^2\right]\Delta x,$$
in the $n$ free variables $h_1,\dots,h_n$ (with $h_0=h(a)=0,\ h_{n+1}=h(b)=0$ fixed by the boundary conditions). Expanding and collecting terms by which $h_i$'s multiply which, this is a quadratic form whose matrix is tridiagonal — a Jacobi matrix, with nonzero entries only on the main diagonal and the two neighboring diagonals — reflecting the fact that the discretized functional only ever couples adjacent mesh points to each other.

A three-term recurrence for the minors. Tridiagonal matrices have a special, exploitable structure: expanding the descending principal minor $D_i$ along its last row gives the three-term recurrence $D_i=a_iD_{i-1}-b_{i-1}^2D_{i-2}$ (with $D_0=1,D_{-1}=0$ as bookkeeping conventions), letting every minor be computed from the previous two without ever building the full determinant from scratch. Sylvester's criterion then says: the discretized quadratic form is positive definite exactly when every one of these successively-computed $D_i$ is positive.

Taking the mesh to zero. The recurrence for $D_i$ has coefficients that blow up as $\Delta x\to0$ (they involve $1/\Delta x$ and $1/(\Delta x)^2$, since finer meshes make adjacent points more tightly coupled), so a direct limit is impossible — but a clever rescaling, $D_i = \dfrac{P_1\cdots P_iZ_{i+1}}{(\Delta x)^{i+1}}$, converts the recurrence for $D_i$ into a recurrence for the rescaled quantities $Z_i$ that does have a well-behaved limit as $\Delta x\to0$: substituting and simplifying turns the discrete recurrence into
$$Q_iZ_i - \frac{1}{\Delta x}\left(P_i\frac{Z_{i+1}-Z_i}{\Delta x}-P_{i-1}\frac{Z_i-Z_{i-1}}{\Delta x}\right)=0,$$
which is manifestly a difference-quotient approximation to a second derivative — and passing to the limit $\Delta x\to0$ turns it directly into
$$-\frac{d}{dx}(PZ')+QZ=0,$$
which is exactly the Jacobi equation from Section 26! Moreover, the initial scaling conditions built into the definition of $Z_i$ correspond precisely to $Z(a)=0,\ Z'(a)=1$ — the exact normalization used to define the conjugate-point solution all along. So Sylvester's discrete positivity condition ("every $D_i>0$") becomes, in the limit, exactly "the solution $Z(x)$ of the Jacobi equation with $Z(a)=0,Z'(a)=1$ never returns to zero on $(a,b]$" — which is precisely "no point conjugate to $a$."

The moral. Conjugate points and Jacobi's condition are not an exotic, purpose-built device — they are Sylvester's criterion for positive-definiteness, the same one from introductory linear algebra, carried faithfully through a limiting process from finite-dimensional quadratic forms to the infinite-dimensional quadratic functional $\delta^2J$. The whole apparatus of this chapter is, at bottom, linear algebra done on an infinite-dimensional space of functions — a theme that recurs throughout more advanced analysis, and one worth carrying forward as you continue further into the subject.

Chapter recap

This chapter built, for the first time in the book, conditions that actually guarantee an extremum rather than merely narrowing down candidates for one — the calculus-of-variations analogue of upgrading $f'=0$ to $f'=0,f''>0$. Section 24 defined the second variation $\delta^2J[h]$ by direct analogy with ordinary Taylor expansion, and showed $\delta^2J\ge0$ necessary, $\delta^2J$ strongly positive sufficient, for a minimum. Section 25 computed $\delta^2J$ explicitly for the simplest problem, landing on the quadratic functional $\int(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$ with $P=\frac12F_{y'y'}$, and extracted Legendre's necessary condition $F_{y'y'}\ge0$ — while also showing, via the great-circle counterexample, why no purely local condition (however strengthened) could ever be sufficient on its own. Section 26 introduced the chapter's central new idea, the conjugate point — a place where a family of nearby extremals from a common start refocuses — and proved the sharp "if and only if" connecting positive definiteness of $\int(Ph'^2+Qh^2)\,dx$ to the absence of conjugate points, via the Riccati-equation completing-the-square trick. Section 27 translated this into Jacobi's necessary condition for the original problem, and gathered several equivalent geometric pictures of what a conjugate point represents. Section 28 assembled the payoff: Euler's equation, plus the strengthened Legendre condition, plus the strengthened (closed-interval) Jacobi condition, together guarantee an actual weak minimum. Section 29 repeated the entire arc for $n$ unknown functions, with matrices replacing scalars and a determinant condition replacing a single vanishing solution. And Section 30 closed the loop by showing conjugate points are nothing but Sylvester's positive-definiteness criterion, inherited from ordinary linear algebra through a careful discretize-then-take-a-limit argument. Chapter 6 pushes further still, from weak extrema (measured against nearby curves with nearby slopes) to strong extrema (measured against nearby curves with arbitrary slopes) — a harder question in its own right, addressed there with the machinery of fields of extremals and the Weierstrass E-function.

Practice problems
Problem 1

1. (Second variation of several standard functional types.) Calculate the second variation of each of: (a) $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y)\,dx$ (no derivative at all); (b) $J[y]=\int_a^bF(x,y,y',\dots,y^{(n)})\,dx$ (higher derivatives, as in Chapter 2, Section 10); (c) $J[u]=\iint_RF(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy$ (a double integral, previewing Chapter 7).

Hint: For (a), since there's no $y'$-dependence, the whole Taylor-expansion argument of Section 25 collapses to $\delta^2J[h]=\int_a^bF_{yy}h^2\,dx$ — no integration by parts is even needed, since there's no cross term to eliminate. For (b), repeat Section 25's argument but Taylor-expand in all $n+1$ variables $y,y',\dots,y^{(n)}$ at once, producing a sum of $\binom{n+2}{2}$ cross-derivative terms $F_{y^{(i)}y^{(k)}}h^{(i)}h^{(k)}$ (with $y^{(0)}:=y$), each pair possibly needing repeated integration by parts to collect into pure $h^{(j)}h^{(j)}$-type terms. For (c), this is the double-integral analogue: Taylor-expand $F$ to second order in $u,u_x,u_y$ and see problem 6 below for the resulting matrix that must be nonnegative definite.

Problem 2

2. (Second variation of a linear functional.) Show that the second variation of a linear functional is zero. State and prove a converse.

Hint: If $J[y]$ is linear, $\Delta J[h]=J[h]$ exactly (no higher-order terms at all — this is the defining feature of linearity), so comparing against $\Delta J[h]=\delta J[h]+\delta^2J[h]+\varepsilon\|h\|^2$ forces $\delta^2J[h]\equiv0$. For the converse, ask: if $\delta^2J[h]\equiv0$ for every twice-differentiable functional, must $J$ be linear? Think about what $\delta^2 J \equiv 0$ combined with $\Delta J[h] = \delta J[h] + o(\|h\|)$ actually pins down, and whether a linear-plus-higher-than-quadratic functional could be a counterexample.

Problem 3

3. (Twice-differentiability of a quadratic functional.) Prove that a quadratic functional $A[y]$ (in the sense of Section 24) is itself twice differentiable, and find its own first and second variations.

Hint: Write $A[y+h]-A[y]$ directly using bilinearity, $A[y+h]=B[y+h,y+h]=B[y,y]+2B[y,h]+B[h,h]$ (using symmetry $B[y,h]=B[h,y]$, which every quadratic functional's underlying bilinear form can be assumed to have without loss of generality). Identify which piece is linear in $h$ (that's $\delta A$) and which is exactly quadratic with no remainder at all (that's $\delta^2A$, and notice the remainder term $\varepsilon\|h\|^2$ can be taken to be identically zero here — a quadratic functional's Taylor expansion terminates after two terms, exactly like a quadratic polynomial's).

Problem 4

4. (Second variation of a composed functional.) Calculate the second variation of $e^{J[y]}$, where $J[y]$ is a twice-differentiable functional. (Book's answer: $\delta^2e^{J[y]} = \bigl[(\delta J)^2+\delta^2J\bigr]e^{J[y]}$.)

Hint: This is the functional-calculus analogue of computing $(e^{f(x)})''=[(f'(x))^2+f''(x)]e^{f(x)}$ via the ordinary chain and product rules — literally substitute $\Delta J[h]=\delta J[h]+\delta^2J[h]+\varepsilon\|h\|^2$ into the Taylor expansion of $e^u$ around $u=J[y]$, i.e. $e^{J[y]+\Delta J}=e^{J[y]}\left(1+\Delta J+\frac12(\Delta J)^2+\cdots\right)$, and collect terms by their order in $h$ (remembering $(\delta J[h])^2$ is itself already quadratic in $h$, so it belongs with $\delta^2J[h]$ at second order, not with $\delta J[h]$ at first).

Problem 5

5. (Strong positivity really is stronger than positive definiteness.) Give an example showing that in Theorem 2 of Section 24, the strong-positivity hypothesis $\delta^2J[h]\ge k\|h\|^2$ cannot be weakened to the merely ordinary positive-definiteness hypothesis $\delta^2J[h]>0$ (for $h\ne0$).

Hint: You need a quadratic functional that's positive for every individual nonzero $h$, yet gets arbitrarily "thin" relative to $\|h\|^2$ as $h$ ranges over a whole sequence of admissible functions — try building a sequence $h_n$ of increasingly rapid low-amplitude oscillations (in the flavor of the bump functions from Section 25's Lemma) for which $\int Ph_n'^2\,dx$ stays bounded while $\|h_n\|_1$ does not shrink to match, so no single constant $k>0$ can work uniformly across the whole sequence even though each individual value is positive.

Problem 6

6. (Legendre's condition for a double integral.) Derive the analogue of Legendre's necessary condition for $J[u]=\iint_RF(x,y,u,u_x,u_y)\,dx\,dy$, where $u$ vanishes on the boundary of $R$. (Book's answer: the matrix $\begin{pmatrix}F_{u_xu_x} & F_{u_xu_y}\\F_{u_yu_x} & F_{u_yu_y}\end{pmatrix}$ should be nonnegative definite.)

Hint: This runs Section 25's bump-function argument in two dimensions instead of one: build a perturbation concentrated in a tiny disk (rather than a tiny interval) around a point $(x_0,y_0)$, oscillating rapidly in some fixed direction — this makes the term built from $u_x,u_y$ (the two-dimensional analogue of $Ph'^2$) dominate the term built from $u$ alone, exactly as in the one-dimensional Lemma, forcing the $2\times2$ matrix of second partials with respect to $u_x,u_y$ to be nonnegative definite (rather than merely each diagonal entry being nonnegative) — since the oscillation direction is arbitrary, every possible quadratic combination of $u_x,u_y$ must come out nonnegative.

Problem 7

7. (A concrete positive-definiteness range.) For which values of $a,b$ is $\int_0^a[f'^2(x)-bf^2(x)]\,dx$ nonnegative for all $f$ with $f(0)=f(a)=0$? Deduce an inequality from the answer.

Hint: This is exactly the quadratic functional (23) with $P=1>0$ (constant) and $Q=-b$ (also constant). Its Euler equation $-h''-bh=0$, i.e. $h''+bh=0$, is the ordinary simple-harmonic-oscillator equation — solve it directly (cases $b>0,b=0,b<0$ look different) and find the first conjugate point of $x=0$ as a function of $b$; then apply Theorem 3 of Section 26 (nonnegative rather than positive definite — matching this problem's $\ge$ rather than strict $>$ — corresponds to the conjugate point landing at or past $x=a$, i.e. no conjugate point in the open interval $(0,a)$). The resulting threshold on $b$ in terms of $a$, rearranged, is exactly a form of the classical Wirtinger/Poincaré inequality relating $\int f'^2$ and $\int f^2$ for functions vanishing at both endpoints — a useful inequality in its own right, and worth remembering it shows up here as a byproduct.

Problem 8

8. (A functional with no conjugate points at all.) Show that the extremals of any functional of the form $\int_a^bF(x,y')\,dx$ (no explicit $y$-dependence at all) have no conjugate points.

Hint: Since $F$ doesn't depend on $y$ at all, $F_{yy}=F_{yy'}=0$ identically, so $Q=\frac12(F_{yy}-\frac{d}{dx}F_{yy'})=0$ identically too — the Jacobi equation (26) collapses all the way down to $-\frac{d}{dx}(Ph')=0$, i.e. $Ph'=\text{const}$. With $P=\frac12F_{y'y'}>0$ (assuming the nondegenerate case), solve this directly for $h'$ and then $h$ in terms of an antiderivative of $1/P$, and show the solution satisfying $h(a)=0,h'(a)=1$ is a nonzero constant times a strictly monotonic function of $x$ — hence it cannot return to zero anywhere in $(a,b]$, so there's no conjugate point of $a$ anywhere in the interval, regardless of how long the interval is.
