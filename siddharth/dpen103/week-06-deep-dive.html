Binary Phase Diagrams and the Lever Rule

A phase diagram is a map. Give it a composition and a temperature and it tells you what is inside the material — which phases exist, what each is made of, and how much of each there is. The reading is mechanical once you know the steps, which makes this one of the most reliably scoring weeks in the subject.

What this week is really asking
At this composition and this temperature, what is actually in there?

Three questions, three steps, always in the same order. Which phases are present? What is each one made of? How much of each is there? The third needs the lever rule, and the lever rule is nothing more than mass balance.

Where this sits. This is card 6 of the core map. It uses the phase vocabulary from Week 5 and explains where the microstructures behind Week 3 strengthening actually come from. Two worked diagrams for this appear on the reading the pictures page. Textbook sections: Callister Ch9 §9.7–9.14.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
Reading the map
Composition, \(C_0\)the overall alloy compositionHow much of each ingredient you started with, in weight percent. It does not change as you cool — only how it is distributed changes.
Liquidus—The line above which everything is liquid.
Solidus—The line below which everything is solid. Between the two lines you have a mush of both.
Solvus—A line showing how much of one solid dissolves in another as temperature changes.
Isomorphous—The simplest kind of diagram, where the two ingredients dissolve in each other completely. Just a lens shape with liquidus above and solidus below.
Eutectic—The special composition that melts at the lowest temperature of the whole system, and freezes all at once instead of over a range. Solder sits near it.
Tie-line—A horizontal line drawn across a two-phase region at your temperature. Where it hits the boundaries tells you what each phase is made of.
Lever rule—The arithmetic turning a tie-line into "how much of each phase". It is mass balance, nothing more.
Symbols

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 What the map shows

Temperature up the side, composition along the bottom. Every point on the diagram is a specific alloy at a specific temperature, and the region it lands in tells you what phases exist there.

The three-step reading, always in this order
  • Step 1 — plot the point. Composition across, temperature up. Note which region it falls in.
  • Step 2 — is it one phase or two? If one, you are done: everything is that phase, and its composition is \(C_0\).
  • Step 3 — if two, draw the tie-line. A horizontal line at your temperature, stretching across the two-phase region to the boundary on each side.

The tie-line answers two different questions and it is essential to keep them apart. Where its ends land gives you the compositions of the two phases. The lengths of its two segments give you the amounts. Ends for composition, lengths for amount.

One thing that confuses people early: \(C_0\) never changes as the alloy cools. You did not add or remove anything. What changes is how that fixed overall composition is divided between phases.

Feeds: the lever rule below, and every microstructure prediction in the subject.
Say it cold: "Plot the point, check if it is a two-phase region, then draw a horizontal tie-line: the ends give compositions and the lengths give amounts."

2 The lever rule, derived

It looks like a rule to memorise and it is not. It falls straight out of the fact that mass cannot be created or destroyed, and deriving it takes three lines.

The derivation
\[W_\alpha+W_\beta=1\]

The two fractions must account for all of the material.

\[C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta\]

All the alloying element present must be inside one phase or the other.

Substitute \(W_\beta=1-W_\alpha\) into the second equation and solve for \(W_\alpha\):

\[W_\alpha=\frac{C_\beta-C_0}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}\qquad W_\beta=\frac{C_0-C_\alpha}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}\]

The lever rule. The bottom is always the full tie-line length.

The feature that catches everyone: to get the fraction of a phase you use the length of the segment on the opposite side. That is why it is called the lever rule — like a seesaw, the far arm tells you the near weight. If your point sits close to the \(\alpha\) boundary, you have mostly \(\alpha\), and the opposite-arm arithmetic is what gives that.

Feeds: microstructure sketching, and any question about how much of a phase forms during cooling.
Say it cold: "Fraction of a phase equals the opposite segment over the whole tie-line, and it comes straight from mass balance."

3 Eutectic diagrams

A more useful shape than the simple lens, with one special point where liquid turns into two solids simultaneously at a single temperature.

Most real alloys do not dissolve in each other without limit, so their diagram has a V shape dipping down to a lowest point. That point is the eutectic: the composition that melts at the lowest temperature in the whole system, and the only one that freezes at a single temperature rather than over a range.

What to be able to do with one
  • Name every region. Single-phase regions sit against the edges and just below the eutectic point; two-phase regions sit between them.
  • Identify the eutectic point — the bottom of the V, where liquid becomes \(\alpha+\beta\) at one temperature.
  • Read maximum solubility, which is the widest point of a single-phase solid region.
  • Apply the tie-line and lever rule exactly as before. Nothing about the method changes.

Why the eutectic matters practically: freezing at one temperature rather than over a range gives a sharp melting point and a fine, strong two-phase structure. That is precisely what you want in a solder.

Feeds: the steel diagram, and Week 3 precipitation strengthening, which relies on solubility changing with temperature.
Say it cold: "The eutectic is the lowest melting composition, and it freezes all at once into two solid phases instead of over a range."

4 The checks that catch mistakes

The lever rule is easy to get inverted, and an inverted answer looks perfectly plausible. Three checks catch nearly every error, and they take seconds.

  • Do the fractions add to 1? If not, arithmetic slipped somewhere.
  • Is each between 0 and 1? A negative fraction or one above 1 means \(C_0\) was placed outside the tie-line, or the compositions were swapped.
  • Does the answer lean the right way? If \(C_0\) sits close to \(C_\alpha\), you should get mostly \(\alpha\). Getting mostly \(\beta\) means the arms are inverted.

The third check is the one that catches the classic error, because the first two still pass when you invert the arms. Do it every single time.

One more: if \(C_0\) is exactly midway between \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_\beta\), the answer must be 0.5 and 0.5. That is a free sanity check whenever the numbers happen to be symmetric.

Feeds: exam accuracy more than anything else this week — the method is easy, so the marks are lost to inversion rather than misunderstanding.
Say it cold: "Fractions add to one, both sit between zero and one, and the bigger fraction is the phase whose composition is nearer to C-nought."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — the standard lever rule calculation

Given: \(C_\alpha=20\) wt%, \(C_\beta=80\) wt%, \(C_0=35\) wt%.
  1. Tie-line length: \(C_\beta-C_\alpha=80-20=60\).
  2. For \(W_\alpha\), use the opposite arm — the segment from \(C_0\) to \(C_\beta\): \(80-35=45\).
  3. \(W_\alpha=45/60=0.75\).
  4. For \(W_\beta\), the other arm: \(35-20=15\), so \(W_\beta=15/60=0.25\).
  5. Check: they sum to 1.00. And \(C_0=35\) sits nearer \(C_\alpha=20\), so mostly \(\alpha\) is correct.
What it means: 75% \(\alpha\) and 25% \(\beta\) by mass at that temperature. Both checks passed, so the arms were not inverted.

Worked example 2 — the symmetric case

Given: \(C_\alpha=10\), \(C_\beta=70\), \(C_0=40\) wt%.
  1. Tie-line length: \(70-10=60\).
  2. \(W_\alpha=(70-40)/60=30/60=0.5\).
  3. \(W_\beta=(40-10)/60=30/60=0.5\).
  4. Sanity: \(C_0=40\) is exactly midway between 10 and 70, so equal fractions are exactly what should appear.
What it means: Half and half. When the numbers are symmetric the answer is forced, which makes this a good self-test that your method is right.

Worked example 3 — predicting the trend without numbers

If \(C_0\) moves closer to \(C_\beta\) while the temperature stays fixed, what happens to the phase amounts?

  1. The tie-line endpoints are set by temperature, so \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_\beta\) do not move.
  2. The denominator \(C_\beta-C_\alpha\) is therefore unchanged.
  3. The numerator for \(W_\beta\) is \(C_0-C_\alpha\), which grows as \(C_0\) moves right.
  4. So \(W_\beta\) rises and \(W_\alpha\) falls by the same amount.
What it means: Which matches intuition: put more of ingredient B in and you get more of the B-rich phase. Running this check before calculating catches an inverted lever rule immediately.

The weighted-average pattern

The lever rule is a weighted average read backwards, and that same shape returns in Week 11 as the rule of mixtures for composites.

There, \(E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m\) combines two ingredient properties using their volume fractions. Here, \(C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta\) combines two phase compositions using their mass fractions. Same structure — the difference is that composites give you the fractions and ask for the property, while phase diagrams give you the property and ask for the fractions.

Spotting this saves genuine effort in Week 11, because you have already done the algebra.

Where marks get lost

  • Inverting the lever arms. The single biggest source of lost marks this week. Always check which phase should dominate before writing the answer down.
  • Reading the tie-line at the wrong temperature. Phase compositions change with temperature, so a tie-line drawn 20 °C off gives entirely wrong endpoints.
  • Using \(C_0\) as a phase composition. \(C_0\) is the overall alloy. \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_\beta\) come from where the tie-line meets the boundaries.
  • Applying a tie-line in a single-phase region. There is nothing to divide. Everything is that one phase, at composition \(C_0\).
  • Mixing weight percent with atomic percent. Pick whichever the diagram uses and stay in it.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

A horizontal tie-line is drawn across a two-phase region at the temperature of interest. What do its endpoints and its two segment lengths tell you?

  1. The endpoints give the amounts of each phase; the segment lengths give the compositions.
  2. The endpoints give the compositions of each phase; the segment lengths give the amounts.
  3. Both the endpoints and the lengths give compositions, at two different temperatures.
  4. Neither — a tie-line only shows where melting begins.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Ends for composition, lengths for amount. Where the tie-line meets each phase boundary you read \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_\beta\), the compositions of the two phases in weight percent (wt%, composition by mass). The two segment lengths then feed the lever rule to give the mass fractions \(W_\alpha\) and \(W_\beta\). A is the inverted version of the same sentence and is the most common way this is misremembered. Design meaning: these are two genuinely different questions — "what is each phase made of" and "how much of each is there" — and a full answer needs both.
Q2 · Multiple choiceTrap

An alloy of overall composition \(C_0=40\) wt% B is cooled slowly from the fully liquid state into a two-phase liquid-plus-solid region. What happens to \(C_0\) during the cooling?

  1. It falls, because the solid that forms removes B from the alloy.
  2. It rises, because the liquid becomes enriched as it cools.
  3. It stays at 40 wt% B; only the way that B is divided between the phases changes.
  4. It becomes undefined once two phases are present.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. \(C_0\) is the overall composition of the alloy — how much of each ingredient you started with. Nothing is added or removed by cooling, so mass balance fixes it at 40 wt% B for the whole cooling path. What changes is the distribution: the phase compositions \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_L\) move along the sloping boundaries, and the fractions of each phase change with them. A and B confuse the phase compositions, which really do change, with the overall composition, which cannot. Design meaning: this is why the lever rule works at all — \(C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta\) is a statement that the alloying element you put in is still all there, inside one phase or the other.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

At the temperature of interest a tie-line gives \(C_\alpha=15\) wt% and \(C_\beta=95\) wt%, and the alloy has \(C_0=35\) wt%. What is \(W_\alpha\)?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.44
  3. 0.75
  4. 0.80
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. The tie-line length is \(C_\beta-C_\alpha=95-15=80\) wt%. For \(W_\alpha\) take the opposite arm, the segment from \(C_0\) to \(C_\beta\): \(95-35=60\) wt%. So \(W_\alpha=60/80=0.75\), and \(W_\beta=(35-15)/80=20/80=0.25\). Checks: the fractions sum to 1.00; both lie between 0 and 1; and \(C_0=35\) sits much nearer \(C_\alpha=15\) than \(C_\beta=95\), so the answer must be mostly \(\alpha\) — which 0.75 is. Option A is the same arithmetic with the arms inverted, and it passes the first two checks while failing the third. Design meaning: if the \(\beta\) phase were the brittle one, inverting the arms would turn a 25% brittle-phase structure into a predicted 75% and lead to the wrong conclusion about toughness.
Q4 · Multiple choiceOne step

A binary A–B diagram has two liquidus lines sloping down from each pure end and meeting at a lowest point at 40 wt% B and 250 °C, with a single-phase \(\alpha\) region against the left edge and \(\beta\) against the right. An alloy of exactly 40 wt% B is cooled slowly from the liquid. What happens as it passes 250 °C?

  1. It gradually freezes over a temperature range, forming \(\alpha\) first and then \(\beta\).
  2. The whole liquid transforms at that single temperature into a mixture of \(\alpha\) and \(\beta\).
  3. It stays fully liquid, because 250 °C is the lowest temperature on the diagram.
  4. It forms a single solid phase whose composition then drifts on further cooling.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. The lowest point where the two liquidus lines meet is the eutectic point, and 40 wt% B is the eutectic composition. It is the only composition in the system that freezes at a single temperature instead of over a range, and it does so by transforming liquid directly into two solid phases at once, \(L\rightarrow\alpha+\beta\). A describes what happens either side of the eutectic composition, not at it. C confuses the lowest melting temperature in the system with an absolute floor on the diagram. Design meaning: freezing at one temperature gives a sharp melting point and a fine, strong two-phase structure, which is exactly what a solder needs.
Q5 · True/falseTrap

True or false: Weight percent and atomic percent may be mixed within a single lever-rule calculation, provided both phase compositions come from the same diagram.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. Weight percent (wt%) is composition by mass; atomic percent (at%) is composition by number of atoms. They are different measures and convert into one another only through the atomic masses of the components, so the two are not interchangeable. The lever rule is a mass balance, \(C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta\), and it is only valid if every composition in it is expressed on the same basis. Mixing them produces fractions that may still look plausible — between 0 and 1, summing to 1 — while being wrong. Design meaning: read the axis label on the diagram before doing any arithmetic, and stay in whichever unit it uses for the whole calculation.
Q6 · True/falseQuick

True or false: In the lever rule, the denominator is always the full tie-line length, \(C_\beta-C_\alpha\).

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Both fractions share the same denominator: \(W_\alpha=\dfrac{C_\beta-C_0}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}\) and \(W_\beta=\dfrac{C_0-C_\alpha}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}\). Only the numerators differ, each being the length of the arm on the opposite side from the phase being calculated. That shared denominator is why the two fractions automatically sum to 1: the two numerators add up to the whole tie-line, \((C_\beta-C_0)+(C_0-C_\alpha)=C_\beta-C_\alpha\). Design meaning: if your two fractions do not sum to 1, you have almost certainly used different denominators, so check that before suspecting anything deeper.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

An isomorphous alloy of \(C_0=40\) wt% nickel is held at 1250 °C, in the liquid-plus-solid region. At that temperature the tie-line endpoints are read as \(C_L=32\) wt% Ni on the liquidus and \(C_\alpha=45\) wt% Ni on the solidus. (a) Find the mass fraction of each phase. (b) Run the three checks. (c) State what happens to the fractions as the alloy is cooled slightly.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: \(W_\alpha=0.62\) and \(W_L=0.38\) (to two decimal places).
(a) Tie-line length: \(C_\alpha-C_L=45-32=13\) wt% Ni. For the solid \(\alpha\), take the opposite arm — the segment from \(C_L\) to \(C_0\): \(W_\alpha=\dfrac{C_0-C_L}{C_\alpha-C_L}=\dfrac{40-32}{13}=\dfrac{8}{13}=0.615\). For the liquid: \(W_L=\dfrac{C_\alpha-C_0}{C_\alpha-C_L}=\dfrac{45-40}{13}=\dfrac{5}{13}=0.385\).
(b) Checks. They sum to \(0.615+0.385=1.000\). Both lie between 0 and 1. And \(C_0=40\) is 8 wt% from \(C_L\) but only 5 wt% from \(C_\alpha\), so it sits nearer the solid composition and the answer should be mostly solid — which 0.615 is. A mass-balance check confirms it: \(0.615(45)+0.385(32)=27.7+12.3=40.0\) wt% Ni, recovering \(C_0\) exactly.
(c) Cooling slightly moves the tie-line down, where the liquidus and solidus are further left, so both \(C_L\) and \(C_\alpha\) fall while \(C_0\) stays at 40 wt%. \(C_0\) therefore sits relatively further from \(C_L\) and closer to \(C_\alpha\), so \(W_\alpha\) rises and \(W_L\) falls. More solid on cooling is exactly what should happen.
Design meaning: this is how a casting solidifies — a progressively larger solid fraction whose composition keeps changing, which is the origin of the compositional segregation found in cast parts.
Self-mark: one point for the correct tie-line length and opposite-arm choice; one for both fractions; one for at least two checks including the lean check or the mass balance; one for the correct cooling trend with a reason.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A 2.0 kg casting of overall composition \(C_0=32\) wt% B sits in a two-phase region where the tie-line gives \(C_\alpha=8\) wt% B and \(C_\beta=68\) wt% B. Find the mass of each phase present, and verify the result by a mass balance on element B.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: 1.2 kg of \(\alpha\) and 0.8 kg of \(\beta\).
Step 1 — tie-line length. \(C_\beta-C_\alpha=68-8=60\) wt% B.
Step 2 — fractions by the lever rule. \(W_\alpha=\dfrac{C_\beta-C_0}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}=\dfrac{68-32}{60}=\dfrac{36}{60}=0.60\); \(W_\beta=\dfrac{C_0-C_\alpha}{60}=\dfrac{32-8}{60}=\dfrac{24}{60}=0.40\). They sum to 1.00, and \(C_0=32\) is nearer \(C_\alpha=8\) (24 wt% away) than \(C_\beta=68\) (36 wt% away), so mostly \(\alpha\) is correct.
Step 3 — convert fractions to masses. These are mass fractions, so multiply by the total mass: \(m_\alpha=0.60\times2.0\text{ kg}=1.2\) kg and \(m_\beta=0.40\times2.0\text{ kg}=0.8\) kg. The masses sum to 2.0 kg as they must.
Mass-balance verification on B. Total B present \(=m_\alpha C_\alpha+m_\beta C_\beta=1.2(0.08)+0.8(0.68)=0.096+0.544=0.640\) kg. As a fraction of the whole casting: \(0.640/2.0=0.32=32\) wt% B, which is \(C_0\). The books balance, so the arms were not inverted.
Design meaning: converting to real masses is what makes a phase calculation usable — for instance to work out how much of an expensive alloying element ends up locked into a phase that does no useful work.
Self-mark: one point for both fractions with the lean check; one for the conversion to kilograms; one for a mass balance on B that recovers 32 wt%.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Picture a eutectic A–B diagram: temperature up the side, wt% B along the bottom. Two liquidus lines slope down from the two pure melting points and meet at the eutectic point. A narrow single-phase \(\alpha\) region hugs the left edge, widening to its maximum width at the eutectic temperature before narrowing again below it; \(\beta\) mirrors it on the right. Describe (a) how you would name the regions, (b) how you would read the maximum solubility of B in \(\alpha\), and (c) what a hypoeutectic alloy — one to the left of the eutectic composition — passes through as it cools from the liquid.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: single-phase regions against the edges, two-phase regions between them; maximum solubility at the widest point of \(\alpha\); a hypoeutectic alloy forms \(\alpha\) first, then the remaining liquid goes eutectic.
(a) Naming the regions. Above both liquidus lines everything is liquid, \(L\). Against each edge is a single-phase solid solution, \(\alpha\) on the left and \(\beta\) on the right. Between a liquidus line and the solid region below it lies a two-phase region — \(L+\alpha\) on the left and \(L+\beta\) on the right. Below the eutectic temperature, between the two solid regions, is the two-phase \(\alpha+\beta\) field. The general pattern is that single-phase fields are separated from each other by two-phase fields, so a two-phase field always names the two neighbours it lies between.
(b) Maximum solubility. Read across at the widest point of the \(\alpha\) region, which occurs at the eutectic temperature, and take the composition of its right-hand boundary. That boundary is the solvus line, and the fact that it slopes back towards the left edge at lower temperatures is the statement that solubility falls as the material cools.
(c) Cooling a hypoeutectic alloy. It stays liquid until it crosses the left liquidus, then enters \(L+\alpha\) and begins to form \(\alpha\) — this is where a tie-line and the lever rule apply, and the \(\alpha\) fraction grows as it cools. When it reaches the eutectic temperature the remaining liquid has been enriched all the way to the eutectic composition, and that liquid transforms at one temperature into \(\alpha+\beta\). Below that the structure is the \(\alpha\) formed earlier sitting in a eutectic \(\alpha+\beta\) matrix.
Design meaning: the falling solvus in (b) is the basis of precipitation strengthening — dissolve the second element at high temperature, then cool so that solubility drops and fine particles are forced out of solution to obstruct dislocations.
Self-mark: one point for naming all five regions with the single/two-phase alternation; one for locating maximum solubility at the widest point of \(\alpha\) and naming the solvus; one for the hypoeutectic cooling sequence ending in a eutectic transformation; one for linking the sloping solvus to a use.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A student inverts the lever arms and reports \(W_\alpha=0.30\), \(W_\beta=0.70\) when the correct answer is the other way round. Explain why two of the three standard checks fail to catch this, which check does catch it, and what the consequence of the error would be if \(\beta\) were a brittle phase.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: the sum-to-one and between-zero-and-one checks are both blind to inversion; only the lean check catches it.
Why two checks pass. Inverting the arms swaps the two numerators while leaving the denominator — the full tie-line length \(C_\beta-C_\alpha\) — untouched. The two numerators still add up to the whole tie-line, so the fractions still sum to 1.00, and each is still a positive number smaller than the total, so each still lies between 0 and 1. Both checks are satisfied by any pair of numbers that partition the tie-line, in either order.
The check that works. Compare where \(C_0\) sits. The larger fraction must belong to the phase whose composition is nearer to \(C_0\), because you have more of the phase the overall alloy more closely resembles. If \(C_0\) is close to \(C_\alpha\) and the answer says 30% \(\alpha\), the arms are inverted. This is the one check that is sensitive to direction, so it has to be done every time.
Consequence with a brittle \(\beta\). The reported structure would be 70% brittle phase when it is actually 30%. That flips the predicted behaviour: a mostly ductile material with isolated brittle regions would be described as a predominantly brittle one. A designer acting on it might reject a perfectly serviceable alloy, or in the reverse case accept one whose real brittle fraction is far higher than reported — the dangerous direction of the same error.
Design meaning: the arithmetic this week is easy, so marks and real engineering errors both come from direction rather than from difficulty. One sentence naming which phase should dominate, written before the calculation, removes the whole failure mode.
Self-mark: one point for explaining that inversion preserves both the sum and the range; one for naming the lean check and why it is directional; one for a concrete consequence tied to the brittle phase; one for the practical habit of predicting the dominant phase first.
