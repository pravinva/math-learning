Strengthening, Fracture and Fatigue

Two ways of failing that both ignore the yield strength you spent Week 2 measuring. A cracked part fails from the crack, at a stress the intact material would shrug off. A repeatedly loaded part fails from the repetition, at a stress that is perfectly safe once. Both need their own maths.

What this week is really asking
This part is below yield. Why is it going to fail anyway?

Week 2 asked how strong the material is. This week asks a harder question — how strong is this particular part, given the flaw it already contains and the number of load cycles ahead of it. That is usually a much smaller number.

Where this sits. This is card 7 and part of card 10 of the core map. It builds directly on the flaws from Week 3, and Week 12 applies the same fracture equation to ceramics, where it dominates everything. Textbook sections: Callister Ch7 §7.8–7.9, Ch8 §8.1–8.8.

Working through the week

Read this section first if the topic is new. It builds the ideas in order. Everything below — the glossary, cards, worked examples and exam practice — stays as your summary and drill.

When yield is not the failure mode

Week 2 taught you to design below yield. Many failures happen while the nominal stress is still below \(\sigma_y\). Two mechanisms dominate: a sharp crack concentrating stress, and cyclic loading that damages the material a little on each cycle.

Linear elastic fracture mechanics asks: given a crack of length \(a\) in a part under stress \(\sigma\), what is the stress intensity \(K\)? When \(K\) reaches the material's fracture toughness \(K_{Ic}\), fast fracture follows. A small crack can be fatal at a stress the uncracked material would survive.

Fatigue: death by repetition

Apply a stress once and the part may be fine. Apply it a million times at half the yield stress and it may still fail. Fatigue limit and S–N curves describe life versus stress amplitude. The curve has an endurance limit for steels — below it, life is effectively infinite — but many alloys have no such knee.

Surface finish, stress concentration and mean stress all shift the curve. Design against fatigue means keeping working stress below the allowable for the required life, not just below yield.

Strengthening mechanisms revisited

Cold work, solid solution, precipitation hardening and grain refinement all raise yield by impeding dislocations. Each makes the stress–strain curve taller and often narrower — more strength, less ductility. Pick a process to match the failure mode you fear: do not cold-work a part that must absorb impact energy.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Fracture

\(a\)crack lengthHalf the length for a crack inside the material, the full length for one at the surface. Getting this convention wrong is a common error.

\(K_{IC}\)fracture toughnessThe largest stress intensity that material can survive. This is the limit, and it is a material property.

Brittle fracture—Snaps suddenly with no deformation. No warning at all.
Fatigue
Fatigue—Failure caused by a load repeating many times, at a level that is completely safe if applied once.

1 Why a crack changes everything

Stress flows through a part rather like water through a channel. A crack forces all of it around a sharp tip, and the local stress there can be many times the average you calculated.

This is why fracture needs its own framework. Comparing average stress with yield strength tells you nothing useful about a cracked part, because the average is not what the crack tip experiences.

Sharpness matters as much as size. A long crack with a rounded end can be less dangerous than a short, sharp one, which is why drilling a hole at the end of a crack is a genuine field repair — it blunts the tip.

Feeds: the equation in the next card, and every design rule about fillets, radii and avoiding sharp internal corners.
Say it cold: "A crack concentrates stress at its tip, so the average stress in the part no longer tells you whether it is safe."

2 The fracture equation

One equation for the demand, one material property for the limit, and failure when the first reaches the second.

\[K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\]

\(K_I\) is stress intensity, \(Y\) is the geometry factor, \(\sigma\) the applied stress and \(a\) the crack length. Units are MPa·√m.

\[\text{Fracture when }K_I\ge K_{IC}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\sigma_{\text{allow}}=\frac{K_{IC}}{Y\sqrt{\pi a}}\]

Rearranged to give the largest stress the part can carry with that crack in it.

Keep the two symbols straight, because they look almost identical and mean opposite things. \(K_I\) is what you are doing to the part — it changes if you change the load or the crack grows. \(K_{IC}\) is what the material can take, and it only changes if you change material.

The shape to remember
  • Allowable stress goes as \(1/\sqrt{a}\).
  • Four times the crack length halves the allowable stress.
  • Twice the crack length divides it by \(\sqrt2\), about 0.71.
Feeds: Week 12, where ceramic strength is entirely governed by this relationship, and inspection intervals in real engineering practice.
Say it cold: "K-one is the demand and K-one-C is the limit; allowable stress falls as one over the square root of crack length."

3 Fatigue — failure by repetition

A load far below yield, applied enough times, will still break the part. Most in-service metal failures are this, not overload.

The sequence is always the same. A tiny crack starts, usually at a surface imperfection or a stress concentration. Each cycle pushes it a fraction further. It grows quietly for most of the life of the part, then reaches the size where the fracture equation above takes over, and the final break is instant.

That silent growth is what makes fatigue dangerous: there is no warning deflection, no visible deformation, and the final failure looks brittle even in a ductile metal.

Reading the S–N curve
  • Cyclic stress amplitude runs up the side; cycles to failure run along the bottom, on a log scale.
  • Lower stress, longer life — the curve falls to the right.
  • Steel flattens into a horizontal endurance limit: below that stress it survives indefinitely.
  • Aluminium never flattens, so there is no safe stress — only a life at a stated number of cycles.
Feeds: the design fixes below, and the reason Week 8 selection answers must state the loading type.
Say it cold: "Fatigue is a crack growing quietly under repeated load below yield, and steel has an endurance limit while aluminium does not."

4 What actually fixes a fatigue problem

Fatigue starts at surfaces, so the effective fixes are mostly about surfaces. Notably, a stronger alloy is often the least useful change.

  • Improve the surface finish. Machining marks are crack starters. Polishing can extend life substantially at no material cost.
  • Put the surface into compression — shot peening or rolling. A crack struggles to open against compression. This is one of the cheapest and most effective interventions available.
  • Remove stress concentrations. Generous fillet radii instead of sharp internal corners. A sharp step is a crack waiting to happen.
  • Lower the stress amplitude. A thicker section, or reduce the load swing. Amplitude drives the damage.
  • Change material last. A higher-strength alloy is often more notch-sensitive, so it can perform worse in fatigue despite the better tensile numbers.

The last point catches people out and makes a good exam answer. Fatigue resistance and tensile strength are related but not the same, and optimising the wrong one is a real engineering mistake.

Feeds: Weeks 8 and 13, where "how would you improve this part" is exactly the question asked.
Say it cold: "Fatigue starts at surfaces, so finish, compressive residual stress and generous radii beat a stronger alloy."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — allowable stress with a known crack

Given: \(K_{IC}=40\) MPa·√m, \(Y=1.1\), crack \(a=2\) mm \(=0.002\) m.
  1. Rearrange: \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\).
  2. Compute the root: \(\sqrt{\pi\times0.002}=\sqrt{0.00628}=0.0793\).
  3. Denominator: \(1.1\times0.0793=0.0872\).
  4. \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=40/0.0872\approx459\) MPa.
  5. Convert the crack length to metres before anything else — millimetres here give an answer wrong by a factor of about 32.
What it means: With that flaw present the part is limited to roughly 459 MPa, regardless of what its yield strength happens to be.

Worked example 2 — the crack grows

Given: Same part, but the crack has grown from 2 mm to 8 mm.
  1. The crack is four times longer, and \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}\propto1/\sqrt a\).
  2. So it scales by \(1/\sqrt4=1/2\).
  3. New allowable stress \(\approx459/2\approx230\) MPa.
What it means: A crack growth of 6 mm halved the load the part can carry. This is exactly why inspection intervals exist, and why "it passed inspection last year" is not a safety argument.

Worked example 3 — doubling the crack

If crack size doubles with everything else fixed, what happens to allowable stress?

  1. \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}\propto1/\sqrt a\).
  2. Doubling \(a\) scales it by \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.707\).
  3. So it drops to about 71% of the original — a 29% reduction.
What it means: Worth memorising the two anchor values: double the crack gives 0.71, quadruple gives 0.50. Most exam variants are one of these two.

The square-root pattern

Crack length sits under a square root, so its effect is strong but not linear. This same shape appears in Week 12 for ceramics and is worth recognising instantly.

Four times the crack halves the allowable stress. Nine times the crack divides it by three. The pattern is that the allowable stress scales by one over the square root of the growth factor.

Compare it with the Hall–Petch relationship in Week 3, which uses the same square root in the opposite direction: smaller grains give more strength, longer cracks give less.

Where marks get lost

  • Leaving crack length in millimetres. \(K\) values are in MPa·√m, so \(a\) must be in metres.
  • Confusing \(K_I\) with \(K_{IC}\). One is the load situation and changes as the crack grows; the other is a fixed material property.
  • Halving the allowable stress when the crack doubles. Doubling gives \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.71\), not 0.5. Quadrupling gives 0.5.
  • Explaining fatigue as gradual weakening of the material. The material does not weaken. A crack grows. That distinction is worth marks.
  • Recommending a stronger alloy for a fatigue problem. Often the worst option, since higher-strength alloys are frequently more notch-sensitive. Fix the surface first.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

Which statement correctly separates \(K_I\) from \(K_{IC}\)?

  1. \(K_I\) is a material property; \(K_{IC}\) changes as the load or crack changes
  2. \(K_I\) is the demand created by the load and crack; \(K_{IC}\) is the material's limit
  3. Both are material properties, measured on different specimen shapes
  4. Both describe the applied load, one in tension and one in compression
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. \(K_I\) is the stress intensity — one number, from \(K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\), combining how hard you are pulling with how big the crack is. It is what you are doing to the part, so it rises if the load increases or the crack grows. \(K_{IC}\) is fracture toughness, the largest stress intensity that material can survive, and it only changes if you change material. Fracture occurs when \(K_I\ge K_{IC}\). Both are quoted in MPa·√m. Why it matters: the two symbols differ by one subscript letter and mean opposite things — demand versus limit — so mixing them up inverts the conclusion of every fracture question.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

On an S–N curve (stress amplitude against number of cycles to failure), what distinguishes a typical steel from a typical aluminium alloy?

  1. The steel curve flattens into a horizontal endurance limit; the aluminium curve keeps falling
  2. The aluminium curve flattens into a horizontal endurance limit; the steel curve keeps falling
  3. Both flatten, but the steel does so at a lower number of cycles
  4. Neither flattens, so both materials have a finite life at any stress
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. On an S–N curve — S for stress amplitude up the side, N for cycles to failure along the bottom on a logarithmic scale — lower stress always means longer life, so the curve falls to the right. Steels flatten into an endurance limit: a stress low enough that the part survives indefinitely, no matter how many cycles. Aluminium alloys never flatten, so there is no safe stress at all — only a life quoted at a stated number of cycles. Design meaning: a steel component can be designed for infinite life by keeping the stress amplitude below the plateau. An aluminium component cannot, so it must be given a finite design life and a replacement or inspection schedule — which is precisely how aircraft structures are managed.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A plate carries \(\sigma=200\) MPa and contains a crack with \(a=5\) mm. Take \(Y=1.0\) and \(K_{IC}=30\) MPa·√m. What is \(K_I\), and does the plate survive?

  1. \(K_I\approx1.4\) MPa·√m — survives with a very large margin
  2. \(K_I\approx25\) MPa·√m — survives, but with only a modest margin
  3. \(K_I\approx25\) MPa·√m — fractures immediately
  4. \(K_I\approx141\) MPa·√m — fractures immediately
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Convert the crack length to metres first, because \(K\) values are quoted in MPa·√m: \(a=5\) mm \(=0.005\) m. Then \(\sqrt{\pi a}=\sqrt{\pi\times0.005}=\sqrt{0.0157}=0.125\) √m, and \(K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}=1.0\times200\times0.125=25.1\) MPa·√m. Since \(25.1<30\), the plate does not fracture — but the margin is only about 1.2, so very little crack growth is needed to reach the limit. Option D is what you get by leaving \(a\) in millimetres, which inflates the answer by \(\sqrt{1000}\approx32\). Design meaning: a margin of 1.2 against sudden fracture is not a comfortable place to be, because unlike yielding this failure gives no warning.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

A crack grows to three times its original length, with the geometry factor, material and everything else unchanged. What happens to the allowable stress?

  1. It falls to one third of its original value
  2. It falls to about 58% of its original value
  3. It falls to about 71% of its original value
  4. It halves
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. From \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), the allowable stress varies as \(1/\sqrt a\). Tripling \(a\) scales it by \(1/\sqrt3=0.577\), so it falls to about 58% — a 42% reduction. Option A is the trap: crack length sits under a square root, so its effect is strong but not linear. Option C corresponds to doubling (\(1/\sqrt2=0.71\)) and option D to quadrupling (\(1/\sqrt4=0.50\)). The pattern to carry: allowable stress scales by one over the square root of the growth factor. Doubling gives 0.71, tripling 0.58, quadrupling 0.50, and nine times the crack divides the allowable stress by three.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: fatigue failure happens because a crack grows a little on each cycle, not because the material gradually loses strength.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. The sequence is always the same: a tiny crack initiates, usually at a surface imperfection or a stress concentration; each load cycle pushes it a fraction further; it grows quietly for most of the life of the part; then it reaches the size at which \(K_I\) reaches \(K_{IC}\) and the final break is instant. Throughout all of this the material's yield strength, ultimate tensile strength and Young's modulus are unchanged — nothing has weakened. Why the distinction earns marks: "the material got tired and weakened" predicts gradually increasing deflection, which you would notice. The crack-growth explanation correctly predicts what actually happens: no warning deflection, no visible deformation, and a final surface that looks brittle even on a ductile metal.
Q6 · True/falseTrap

True or false: shot peening extends fatigue life mainly because hammering the surface with beads work-hardens it, making the surface layer stronger in tension.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. Shot peening does harden the surface layer, but that is a side effect. The mechanism that matters is the compressive residual stress it leaves behind: the surface is locked in compression, and a fatigue crack cannot easily open against compression. Since fatigue cracks almost always start at the surface, suppressing initiation there is worth far more than a local strength increase. Why the trap works: the stated outcome — longer fatigue life — is correct, and the stated mechanism sounds plausible, so the statement passes a quick reading. Exam statements of this shape are testing whether you can name the mechanism, not just the result. Design meaning: the same logic explains why surface rolling helps, and why a tensile residual stress left by careless grinding or welding does the opposite and can shorten life dramatically.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

A plate with \(K_{IC}=55\) MPa·√m and \(Y=1.2\) is found to contain a 3 mm crack. Its yield strength is 650 MPa. Calculate the allowable stress, compare it with yield, and state which mode of failure governs the design.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}\approx472\) MPa, which is below the 650 MPa yield strength, so fracture governs.
1. Use the rearranged fracture condition: \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\).
2. Convert the crack length before anything else: \(a=3\) mm \(=0.003\) m.
3. Root term: \(\sqrt{\pi a}=\sqrt{\pi\times0.003}=\sqrt{0.00942}=0.0971\) √m.
4. Denominator: \(Y\sqrt{\pi a}=1.2\times0.0971=0.1165\) √m.
5. Divide: \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=55\text{ MPa}\sqrt{\text m}/0.1165\sqrt{\text m}=472\) MPa. The √m units cancel, leaving MPa, which is the check that the algebra is right.
6. Compare: \(472<650\) MPa, so the plate will fracture from the crack before it ever yields.
Sanity check: the answer must be a stress of the same order as the material's strength — a few hundred MPa. Leaving \(a\) in millimetres would have given about 14,900 MPa, which is obviously impossible for a metal and is the fastest way to catch that error.
Design meaning: the yield strength of 650 MPa is now the wrong number to design against. Once a flaw of known size is present, the part is limited to 472 MPa regardless of how strong the intact material is, and the failure it is heading for gives no warning. Either the working stress comes down, or the flaw is removed and the surface blended.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A pressure-vessel wall operates at \(\sigma=300\) MPa in a material with \(K_{IC}=40\) MPa·√m, with \(Y=1.0\). Find the critical crack length \(a_c\) at which fast fracture begins, and say what that number tells the inspection team.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(a_c\approx5.7\) mm, so the inspection method must reliably find cracks well below that — a few millimetres at most.
1. Fracture begins when \(K_I=K_{IC}\), so set \(Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a_c}=K_{IC}\).
2. Rearrange for \(a_c\): \(a_c=\dfrac{1}{\pi}\left(\dfrac{K_{IC}}{Y\sigma}\right)^2\).
3. Inner bracket: \(K_{IC}/(Y\sigma)=40/(1.0\times300)=0.1333\) √m.
4. Square it: \(0.1333^2=0.01778\) m.
5. Divide by \(\pi\): \(a_c=0.01778/3.1416=5.66\times10^{-3}\) m \(=5.7\) mm.
Sanity checks: the units work out to metres, as a length must. The trend also has to be right — raise the working stress and \(a_c\) must fall, and indeed \(a_c\propto1/\sigma^2\), so operating at 600 MPa instead would cut the critical crack to a quarter of this, about 1.4 mm. That squared sensitivity is worth noticing.
What it tells the inspection team: 5.7 mm is the failure point, not the acceptance limit. The detectable flaw size of the chosen method — dye penetrant, ultrasonic, radiographic — must sit comfortably below it, and the inspection interval must be short enough that a crack cannot grow from just-undetectable to 5.7 mm between visits. A method that reliably finds 4 mm cracks offers almost no margin here.
Design meaning: critical crack size, working stress and inspection capability are one linked decision. Lowering the working stress buys a larger tolerable flaw quadratically, which is often cheaper than buying a more sensitive inspection technique.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Two S–N curves are plotted on the same axes, with stress amplitude up the side and cycles to failure along the bottom on a log scale. Curve S starts high, falls steadily to about \(10^6\) cycles, then runs perfectly horizontal at 250 MPa out to \(10^9\). Curve L starts lower and keeps sloping downward across the whole range, passing 140 MPa at \(10^7\) cycles with no flattening. A bracket must survive \(10^8\) cycles. Explain how you would use each curve, and what design commitment each material forces on you.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: curve S is a steel with an endurance limit of 250 MPa; curve L is an aluminium alloy with no endurance limit. S allows a design for indefinite life below 250 MPa; L forces a finite-life design with retirement or inspection.
Reading curve S. The horizontal plateau at 250 MPa is the endurance limit — a stress amplitude below which the part never fails from fatigue, however many cycles it sees. Because the requirement of \(10^8\) cycles lies inside the flat region, you do not read a life off the curve at all. You simply keep the stress amplitude below 250 MPa, with a margin for scatter and for surface condition, and the fatigue requirement is satisfied outright.
Reading curve L. There is no plateau, so there is no safe stress — only a life at a stated stress. To use it, enter the horizontal axis at \(10^8\) cycles, read up to the curve and across to the stress axis, and that value is the maximum amplitude for the required life. It will be below the 140 MPa read at \(10^7\), because the curve is still descending.
The design commitment each forces. Curve S supports an infinite-life design and no fatigue-driven retirement date. Curve L supports only a safe-life design: the component is given a scheduled life shorter than the curve predicts, and is then inspected or replaced whether or not it looks damaged. That is a maintenance obligation for the whole service life of the product, not a one-off design decision.
Two reading habits worth keeping. First, the horizontal axis is logarithmic, so a small drop in stress can buy an order of magnitude in life — this is why lowering the stress amplitude is such an effective fix. Second, S–N data is generated on smooth polished laboratory specimens, so a real bracket with machining marks and a fillet radius will perform below either curve. The curve is the starting point, not the answer.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A steel lifting arm passed a static proof-load test at 1.5 times its service load when it was commissioned. Two years later it failed suddenly in normal use, with no bending or visible deformation beforehand. Explain how both facts can be true, and identify what the proof-load test could never have shown.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: the proof test measured resistance to a single overload, while the failure was fatigue — a crack that initiated and grew over two years of cycling until \(K_I\) reached \(K_{IC}\) and fracture became instantaneous.
What the proof test actually demonstrated. Applying 1.5 times the service load once and finding no permanent deformation shows only that the peak stress stayed below yield on that day, with the arm in the condition it was in on that day. It is a single-cycle strength check.
Why that says nothing about two years of service. Fatigue is failure caused by a load repeating many times at a level that is entirely safe once. A small crack starts at a surface imperfection or a stress concentration — a machining mark, a sharp internal corner, a weld toe — and advances a fraction of a millimetre per cycle. Nominal stress stays below yield the whole time, so no calculation comparing stress with yield strength would ever have flagged it.
Why there was no warning. The crack does its growing quietly, and the surrounding material never yields, so there is no increasing deflection and no visible deformation to notice on inspection. When the crack finally reaches the length at which \(K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\) equals the material's fracture toughness \(K_{IC}\), the fracture propagates immediately. The final break looks brittle even though the steel is ductile, because almost none of the material had a chance to deform.
What the proof test could never show. Three things: whether the geometry contains stress concentrations that will initiate a fatigue crack; how many load cycles the arm will actually see and at what amplitude; and — most importantly — the size of any flaw already present or subsequently grown. A static test loads the intact section. Fracture and fatigue are governed by the worst flaw, and the arm at failure was a different component from the arm that passed the test.
What should have been done instead. Characterise the load spectrum and design against the S–N curve, keeping the stress amplitude below the endurance limit if the steel has one. Remove the sharp geometry with generous fillet radii, improve the surface finish, and consider shot peening to leave the surface in compression. Then set an inspection interval from the critical crack size, because a single successful load test two years ago is not a safety argument.
