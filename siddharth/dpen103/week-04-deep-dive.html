Strengthening, Fracture and Fatigue

Two ways of failing that both ignore the yield strength you spent Week 2 measuring. A cracked part fails from the crack, at a stress the intact material would shrug off. A repeatedly loaded part fails from the repetition, at a stress that is perfectly safe once. Both need their own maths.

What this week is really asking
This part is below yield. Why is it going to fail anyway?

Week 2 asked how strong the material is. This week asks a harder question — how strong is this particular part, given the flaw it already contains and the number of load cycles ahead of it. That is usually a much smaller number.

Where this sits. This is card 7 and part of card 10 of the core map. It builds directly on the flaws from Week 3, and Week 12 applies the same fracture equation to ceramics, where it dominates everything. Textbook sections: Callister Ch7 §7.8–7.9, Ch8 §8.1–8.8.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
Fracture
Flaw / crack—A sharp defect. Stress concentrates enormously at its tip, which is why it matters out of all proportion to its size.
\(a\)crack lengthHalf the length for a crack inside the material, the full length for one at the surface. Getting this convention wrong is a common error.
\(K_I\)stress intensityOne number combining how hard you are pulling with how big the crack is. This is the demand on the material.
\(K_{IC}\)fracture toughnessThe largest stress intensity that material can survive. This is the limit, and it is a material property.
\(Y\)geometry factorA dimensionless number, usually near 1, accounting for the shape of the part and where the crack sits.
Ductile fracture—Tears slowly with visible deformation and necking first. Gives warning.
Brittle fracture—Snaps suddenly with no deformation. No warning at all.
Fatigue
Fatigue—Failure caused by a load repeating many times, at a level that is completely safe if applied once.
Cycle—One complete load-and-unload. Fatigue life is counted in these.
S–N curvestress versus number of cyclesThe standard fatigue graph: cyclic stress up the side, cycles to failure along the bottom. That is genuinely all the name means.
Stress amplitude—Half the difference between the highest and lowest stress in a cycle. This is what drives fatigue damage.
Mean stress—The average level the cycling happens around. A higher mean makes the same amplitude more damaging.
Endurance limit—A stress low enough that the part never fails from fatigue, no matter how many cycles. Steels have one; aluminium does not.
Shot peening—Blasting a surface with small beads to leave it in compression, which makes it much harder for fatigue cracks to start.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 Why a crack changes everything

Stress flows through a part rather like water through a channel. A crack forces all of it around a sharp tip, and the local stress there can be many times the average you calculated.

This is why fracture needs its own framework. Comparing average stress with yield strength tells you nothing useful about a cracked part, because the average is not what the crack tip experiences.

Sharpness matters as much as size. A long crack with a rounded end can be less dangerous than a short, sharp one, which is why drilling a hole at the end of a crack is a genuine field repair — it blunts the tip.

Feeds: the equation in the next card, and every design rule about fillets, radii and avoiding sharp internal corners.
Say it cold: "A crack concentrates stress at its tip, so the average stress in the part no longer tells you whether it is safe."

2 The fracture equation

One equation for the demand, one material property for the limit, and failure when the first reaches the second.

\[K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\]

\(K_I\) is stress intensity, \(Y\) is the geometry factor, \(\sigma\) the applied stress and \(a\) the crack length. Units are MPa·√m.

\[\text{Fracture when }K_I\ge K_{IC}\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad\sigma_{\text{allow}}=\frac{K_{IC}}{Y\sqrt{\pi a}}\]

Rearranged to give the largest stress the part can carry with that crack in it.

Keep the two symbols straight, because they look almost identical and mean opposite things. \(K_I\) is what you are doing to the part — it changes if you change the load or the crack grows. \(K_{IC}\) is what the material can take, and it only changes if you change material.

The shape to remember
  • Allowable stress goes as \(1/\sqrt{a}\).
  • Four times the crack length halves the allowable stress.
  • Twice the crack length divides it by \(\sqrt2\), about 0.71.
Feeds: Week 12, where ceramic strength is entirely governed by this relationship, and inspection intervals in real engineering practice.
Say it cold: "K-one is the demand and K-one-C is the limit; allowable stress falls as one over the square root of crack length."

3 Fatigue — failure by repetition

A load far below yield, applied enough times, will still break the part. Most in-service metal failures are this, not overload.

The sequence is always the same. A tiny crack starts, usually at a surface imperfection or a stress concentration. Each cycle pushes it a fraction further. It grows quietly for most of the life of the part, then reaches the size where the fracture equation above takes over, and the final break is instant.

That silent growth is what makes fatigue dangerous: there is no warning deflection, no visible deformation, and the final failure looks brittle even in a ductile metal.

Reading the S–N curve
  • Cyclic stress amplitude runs up the side; cycles to failure run along the bottom, on a log scale.
  • Lower stress, longer life — the curve falls to the right.
  • Steel flattens into a horizontal endurance limit: below that stress it survives indefinitely.
  • Aluminium never flattens, so there is no safe stress — only a life at a stated number of cycles.
Feeds: the design fixes below, and the reason Week 8 selection answers must state the loading type.
Say it cold: "Fatigue is a crack growing quietly under repeated load below yield, and steel has an endurance limit while aluminium does not."

4 What actually fixes a fatigue problem

Fatigue starts at surfaces, so the effective fixes are mostly about surfaces. Notably, a stronger alloy is often the least useful change.

  • Improve the surface finish. Machining marks are crack starters. Polishing can extend life substantially at no material cost.
  • Put the surface into compression — shot peening or rolling. A crack struggles to open against compression. This is one of the cheapest and most effective interventions available.
  • Remove stress concentrations. Generous fillet radii instead of sharp internal corners. A sharp step is a crack waiting to happen.
  • Lower the stress amplitude. A thicker section, or reduce the load swing. Amplitude drives the damage.
  • Change material last. A higher-strength alloy is often more notch-sensitive, so it can perform worse in fatigue despite the better tensile numbers.

The last point catches people out and makes a good exam answer. Fatigue resistance and tensile strength are related but not the same, and optimising the wrong one is a real engineering mistake.

Feeds: Weeks 8 and 13, where "how would you improve this part" is exactly the question asked.
Say it cold: "Fatigue starts at surfaces, so finish, compressive residual stress and generous radii beat a stronger alloy."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — allowable stress with a known crack

Given: \(K_{IC}=40\) MPa·√m, \(Y=1.1\), crack \(a=2\) mm \(=0.002\) m.
  1. Rearrange: \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\).
  2. Compute the root: \(\sqrt{\pi\times0.002}=\sqrt{0.00628}=0.0793\).
  3. Denominator: \(1.1\times0.0793=0.0872\).
  4. \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=40/0.0872\approx459\) MPa.
  5. Convert the crack length to metres before anything else — millimetres here give an answer wrong by a factor of about 32.
What it means: With that flaw present the part is limited to roughly 459 MPa, regardless of what its yield strength happens to be.

Worked example 2 — the crack grows

Given: Same part, but the crack has grown from 2 mm to 8 mm.
  1. The crack is four times longer, and \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}\propto1/\sqrt a\).
  2. So it scales by \(1/\sqrt4=1/2\).
  3. New allowable stress \(\approx459/2\approx230\) MPa.
What it means: A crack growth of 6 mm halved the load the part can carry. This is exactly why inspection intervals exist, and why "it passed inspection last year" is not a safety argument.

Worked example 3 — doubling the crack

If crack size doubles with everything else fixed, what happens to allowable stress?

  1. \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}\propto1/\sqrt a\).
  2. Doubling \(a\) scales it by \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.707\).
  3. So it drops to about 71% of the original — a 29% reduction.
What it means: Worth memorising the two anchor values: double the crack gives 0.71, quadruple gives 0.50. Most exam variants are one of these two.

The square-root pattern

Crack length sits under a square root, so its effect is strong but not linear. This same shape appears in Week 12 for ceramics and is worth recognising instantly.

Four times the crack halves the allowable stress. Nine times the crack divides it by three. The pattern is that the allowable stress scales by one over the square root of the growth factor.

Compare it with the Hall–Petch relationship in Week 3, which uses the same square root in the opposite direction: smaller grains give more strength, longer cracks give less.

Where marks get lost

  • Leaving crack length in millimetres. \(K\) values are in MPa·√m, so \(a\) must be in metres.
  • Confusing \(K_I\) with \(K_{IC}\). One is the load situation and changes as the crack grows; the other is a fixed material property.
  • Halving the allowable stress when the crack doubles. Doubling gives \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.71\), not 0.5. Quadrupling gives 0.5.
  • Explaining fatigue as gradual weakening of the material. The material does not weaken. A crack grows. That distinction is worth marks.
  • Recommending a stronger alloy for a fatigue problem. Often the worst option, since higher-strength alloys are frequently more notch-sensitive. Fix the surface first.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open. An answer you can only recognise is not an answer you can write in an exam.

1. A crack grows from 2 mm to 8 mm. What happens to allowable stress?
Since \(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), allowable stress scales as \(1/\sqrt a\). A fourfold crack gives \(1/\sqrt4=1/2\), so the allowable stress halves.
2. If crack size only doubles, by what factor does allowable stress change?
By \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.707\), so it falls to about 71% of its original value — not half. Halving requires a fourfold increase in crack length.
3. Why can a component fail in fatigue at stresses well below yield?
Because failure is driven by a growing crack, not by yielding. Each cycle advances a small crack that started at a surface flaw or stress concentration. Nominal stress stays below yield throughout, but once the crack reaches the size where \(K_I\) reaches \(K_{IC}\), fracture is immediate.
4. Name two ways to increase fatigue life without changing the alloy.
Improve the surface finish, since machining marks are crack initiation sites; and introduce compressive residual stress by shot peening, since a crack cannot easily open against compression. Enlarging fillet radii to cut stress concentration is a third.
5. Why is choosing a higher-strength alloy sometimes the wrong response to a fatigue failure?
Because fatigue life is governed by crack initiation and growth at the surface, not by tensile strength. Higher-strength alloys are often more notch-sensitive and less tolerant of small flaws, so the same surface defect can be more damaging. Surface finish, compressive residual stress and geometry usually deliver far more improvement per unit cost.
