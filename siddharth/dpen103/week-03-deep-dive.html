Imperfections and Dislocations

Real crystals are full of mistakes, and that turns out to be the point. A perfect crystal would be far stronger than any metal you have ever handled, and also impossible to bend into a useful shape. This week is about the flaws that make metals workable, and how controlling them controls strength.

What this week is really asking
Why is a real metal so much weaker than a perfect crystal, and how do we use that?

Everything here is one idea seen from four angles: dislocations let metals bend, and anything that blocks dislocations makes them stronger. Once you have that sentence, the four strengthening methods stop being a list to memorise.

Where this sits. This is card 4 of the core map, and it feeds more directions than any other card — strengthening, fracture and eventually ceramics. It explains the curve you measured in Week 2, and Week 4 pushes it into failure. Textbook sections: Callister Ch4 §4.1–4.3 and §4.5–4.7, Ch7 §7.1–7.7 and §7.10–7.13.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
Kinds of defect
Point defect—A fault at a single atomic site — a missing atom or a foreign one.
Vacancy—An empty site where an atom should be. Every metal has them, and heating creates exponentially more.
Substitutional atom—A different element sitting in place of a host atom. Usually a similar size.
Interstitial atom—A small foreign atom wedged into a gap between host atoms. Carbon in iron is the important one.
Dislocation—A line-shaped fault in the pattern. It is how metals bend: the fault shuffles along and one layer slips.
Slip—One plane of atoms sliding over another. Plastic deformation, seen at the atomic scale.
Grain—One small crystal inside the metal. A metal is a mosaic of thousands of them, each pointing a different way.
Grain boundary—The mismatched join between two grains. Dislocations get stuck here, which is why fine grains are stronger.
Symbols
\(n_v\)number of vacanciesHow many empty sites there are in a given amount of material.
\(Q_v\)vacancy formation energyThe energy cost of creating one vacancy. A property of the material.
\(k\)Boltzmann's constant\(1.38\times10^{-23}\) J/K, or \(8.62\times10^{-5}\) eV/K. Match its units to \(Q_v\).
\(T\)absolute temperatureIn kelvin, always. Using °C here is a guaranteed wrong answer.
\(d\)grain diameterThe average size of one grain, usually in micrometres.
\(\sigma_0,\;k_y\)—Two constants fitted to a given material in the Hall–Petch equation.
Processing
Cold work—Deliberately deforming a metal at room temperature. It multiplies dislocations, so the metal gets stronger and less ductile.
Annealing—Heating a worked metal to soften it again and restore ductility.
Recrystallisation—During annealing, brand-new strain-free grains grow and replace the deformed ones. This is where most of the softening happens.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 Dislocations — why metals bend at all

A dislocation is a line where the atomic pattern is interrupted. Metals bend because these lines shuffle through the crystal, letting a plane slip one row at a time instead of all at once.

The comparison worth remembering is moving a heavy rug. Dragging the whole rug at once is nearly impossible. Pushing a ripple along it and letting the ripple travel the length of the rug moves it easily. The dislocation is the ripple.

This is why real metals yield at stresses maybe a hundred times below what a perfect crystal would need. You are never breaking a whole plane of bonds at once; you are breaking a handful at the moving front of the ripple.

The consequence that runs the rest of the week
  • Easy dislocation movement means a soft, ductile metal.
  • Obstructed dislocation movement means a strong, less ductile metal.
  • Every strengthening method is a way to put obstacles in the path.
Feeds: all four strengthening routes below, and the reason ceramics are brittle in Week 12 — they have no easy dislocation movement, so stress has nowhere to go but into a crack.
Say it cold: "Metals bend because dislocations shuffle along like a ripple in a rug, so only a few bonds break at a time."

2 Vacancies and the exponential

Some atomic sites are simply empty, and the number of them climbs exponentially with temperature. That exponential drives every slow, hot process in the subject.

\[n_v=N\exp\!\left(\frac{-Q_v}{kT}\right)\]

\(N\) is the total number of atomic sites, \(Q_v\) is the energy needed to make one vacancy, \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant and \(T\) is the absolute temperature in kelvin.

Atoms can only move through a solid by hopping into neighbouring empty sites, so the vacancy count sets the speed of diffusion. That in turn sets how fast creep happens, how quickly a heat treatment homogenises, and how well powders sinter.

The exam point is almost always the shape of this relationship rather than a number. Temperature sits inside an exponential, so a modest heating gives a dramatic increase. Say "exponentially", never "a bit".

Feeds: creep in Week 5, which is diffusion-driven and therefore inherits this temperature sensitivity.
Say it cold: "Vacancies let atoms move, and their number rises exponentially with temperature, so hot processes speed up dramatically."

3 Grain size and Hall–Petch

A metal is a mosaic of small crystals. Dislocations pile up at the boundaries between them, so making the grains smaller makes the metal stronger.

\[\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_y\,d^{-1/2}\]

\(\sigma_y\) is yield strength, \(d\) is the average grain diameter, and \(\sigma_0\) and \(k_y\) are constants for that material.

Each grain has its atomic planes pointing a different way, so a dislocation running through one grain hits a boundary and cannot simply continue — the slip directions do not line up. More boundaries means more stopping, and more boundaries is exactly what small grains give you.

The \(d^{-1/2}\) is worth internalising as a shape. Making grains four times smaller multiplies the grain-size contribution by \(\sqrt{4}=2\). Letting them grow four times larger halves it.

This is the one strengthening route that improves toughness at the same time, which is why grain refinement is so widely used.

Feeds: the strengthening comparison below, and any heat-treatment question where grain growth is the hidden downside.
Say it cold: "Finer grains mean more boundaries to stop dislocations, and strength scales with one over the square root of grain size."

4 The four strengthening routes

Four methods with four names, all doing the same job: putting obstacles in front of dislocations. And all four charge the same price.

RouteThe obstacle it createsThe cost
Grain refinementMore grain boundariesLittle — this one also improves toughness
Solid solutionForeign atoms distorting the latticeSome ductility, and often conductivity
Strain hardening (cold work)Dislocations tangling with each otherDuctility, noticeably
PrecipitationHard second-phase particlesDuctility, and it can be undone by heat

The shared price is the important half. Strength up almost always means ductility down, because you obstructed the very mechanism that let the metal deform gracefully. A question asking for a strengthening method usually wants the trade-off named too.

Annealing is the reverse gear. Heat a cold-worked metal and it passes through recovery, then recrystallisation — where new strain-free grains replace the deformed ones and most of the softening happens — and then grain growth, which is where you start losing the strength you wanted to keep.

Feeds: Week 4, where these methods raise the curve but do nothing for a part that already has a crack in it.
Say it cold: "Grain refinement, solid solution, strain hardening and precipitation all block dislocations, and all trade ductility for strength."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — grain growth during heat treatment

Given: Grains grow from 10 µm to 40 µm during a heat treatment.
  1. The grain-size term is \(k_y d^{-1/2}\), so only \(d^{-1/2}\) changes.
  2. Grains are four times larger, so \(d^{-1/2}\) scales by \(1/\sqrt{4}=0.5\).
  3. The grain-boundary contribution to yield strength halves.
  4. Note the wording carefully: the contribution halves, not the yield strength itself. The \(\sigma_0\) term is untouched.
What it means: This is why over-long or over-hot treatments backfire. You may have dissolved the precipitates you wanted, but you coarsened the grains and gave back strength doing it.

Worked example 2 — comparing two alloys

Given: Alloy B has grains four times the diameter of alloy A. Same material otherwise.
  1. \(d_B=4d_A\), so \(d_B^{-1/2}=(4d_A)^{-1/2}=0.5\,d_A^{-1/2}\).
  2. B gets half the grain-size strengthening that A gets.
  3. A is the stronger alloy on this mechanism.
What it means: Ratio questions like this never need the constants. Recognising that everything except \(d\) cancels is the entire skill being tested.

Worked example 3 — reading the temperature trend

Why does raising the temperature accelerate creep and sintering so sharply?

  1. Vacancies follow \(n_v=N\exp(-Q_v/kT)\).
  2. Raising \(T\) makes \(Q_v/kT\) smaller, so the negative exponent moves toward zero and the exponential term grows.
  3. More vacancies means atoms have more empty sites to hop into, so diffusion speeds up.
  4. Creep, sintering and homogenisation are all diffusion-controlled, so all three accelerate together.
What it means: Say "exponentially". An answer that says temperature "increases diffusion" describes the direction but misses the sensitivity, and the sensitivity is the examinable part.

Where marks get lost

  • Using °C in the vacancy equation. \(T\) must be in kelvin. This is the most common single error in Week 3.
  • Saying temperature "increases" diffusion. Say exponentially — the whole point of that equation is the sensitivity.
  • Getting Hall–Petch backwards. Smaller grains give higher strength. The negative exponent trips people up; sanity-check with a number.
  • Claiming the yield strength halves when grains coarsen fourfold. Only the \(k_yd^{-1/2}\) contribution halves; \(\sigma_0\) does not move.
  • Naming a strengthening method with no trade-off. Except for grain refinement, they all cost ductility, and the examiner is looking for that sentence.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open. An answer you can only recognise is not an answer you can write in an exam.

1. Grains grow from 10 µm to 40 µm. What happens to Hall–Petch strengthening?
The grains are four times larger, so \(d^{-1/2}\) scales by \(1/\sqrt{4}=0.5\) and the grain-boundary contribution to yield strength halves. The \(\sigma_0\) term is unaffected, so total yield strength falls by less than half.
2. Name the four strengthening routes and the single thing they have in common.
Grain refinement, solid solution strengthening, strain hardening and precipitation hardening. All four obstruct dislocation movement, and with the partial exception of grain refinement, all four trade ductility for yield strength.
3. Why can high-temperature processing both help and hurt final strength?
It helps by dissolving unwanted phases, evening out composition and removing casting defects. It hurts because vacancy count and therefore diffusion rise exponentially with temperature, so grains grow and any cold-work strengthening is wiped out by recovery and recrystallisation. The skill is choosing a temperature and time that gets the first without much of the second.
4. One mechanism that raises strength, and one that restores ductility after cold work?
Cold work itself raises strength by tangling dislocations. Annealing restores ductility: heating drives recovery and then recrystallisation, growing new strain-free grains that replace the deformed ones. Hold it too long or too hot and grain growth follows, which costs strength.
5. Why is a real metal so much weaker than a perfect crystal of the same material?
Because it contains dislocations. A perfect crystal would need every bond on a plane broken simultaneously to slip. A dislocation lets the slip happen progressively, a few bonds at a time as the line travels, so yielding occurs at a small fraction of the theoretical stress.
