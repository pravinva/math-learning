Imperfections and Dislocations

Real crystals are full of mistakes, and that turns out to be the point. A perfect crystal would be far stronger than any metal you have ever handled, and also impossible to bend into a useful shape. This week is about the flaws that make metals workable, and how controlling them controls strength.

What this week is really asking
Why is a real metal so much weaker than a perfect crystal, and how do we use that?

Everything here is one idea seen from four angles: dislocations let metals bend, and anything that blocks dislocations makes them stronger. Once you have that sentence, the four strengthening methods stop being a list to memorise.

Where this sits. This is card 4 of the core map, and it feeds more directions than any other card — strengthening, fracture and eventually ceramics. It explains the curve you measured in Week 2, and Week 4 pushes it into failure. Textbook sections: Callister Ch4 §4.1–4.3 and §4.5–4.7, Ch7 §7.1–7.7 and §7.10–7.13.

Working through the week

Read this section first if the topic is new. It builds the ideas in order. Everything below — the glossary, cards, worked examples and exam practice — stays as your summary and drill.

Why perfect crystals are not the whole story

Calculate the strength of a perfect crystal from bond stiffness and you get enormous numbers — far above any real metal. Real metals are weak by comparison because their crystals contain defects. The most important for strength and ductility is the dislocation: an extra half-plane of atoms wedged into the lattice. Under stress, dislocations move. That motion is plastic deformation.

Without movable dislocations a metal would be brittle like a ceramic. With too many dislocations pinned in place it becomes hard but less ductile. Materials engineering is largely about managing defects, not eliminating them.

Vacancies and diffusion

A vacancy is a missing atom site. At any temperature above absolute zero some sites are empty, and the concentration rises with temperature. Atoms can jump into vacant sites, so vacancies enable diffusion — atoms migrating through the solid. That matters for carburising, homogenising alloys and creep at high temperature.

Do not treat vacancies as a separate topic from dislocations. Both are departures from perfection, and both change properties in predictable ways.

Strengthening by blocking motion

If dislocation motion is what makes metals yield, stopping or slowing that motion raises strength. Solid-solution strengthening puts oversized or undersized atoms in the lattice so dislocations cannot glide easily. Grain boundaries block dislocations because the crystal orientation changes across the boundary. Cold working creates so many dislocations that they tangle and jam each other.

Each method trades ductility for strength. That trade-off is the central pattern of Week 4 as well.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 Dislocations — why metals bend at all

A dislocation is a line where the atomic pattern is interrupted. Metals bend because these lines shuffle through the crystal, letting a plane slip one row at a time instead of all at once.

The comparison worth remembering is moving a heavy rug. Dragging the whole rug at once is nearly impossible. Pushing a ripple along it and letting the ripple travel the length of the rug moves it easily. The dislocation is the ripple.

This is why real metals yield at stresses maybe a hundred times below what a perfect crystal would need. You are never breaking a whole plane of bonds at once; you are breaking a handful at the moving front of the ripple.

The consequence that runs the rest of the week
  • Easy dislocation movement means a soft, ductile metal.
  • Obstructed dislocation movement means a strong, less ductile metal.
  • Every strengthening method is a way to put obstacles in the path.
Feeds: all four strengthening routes below, and the reason ceramics are brittle in Week 12 — they have no easy dislocation movement, so stress has nowhere to go but into a crack.
Say it cold: "Metals bend because dislocations shuffle along like a ripple in a rug, so only a few bonds break at a time."

2 Vacancies and the exponential

Some atomic sites are simply empty, and the number of them climbs exponentially with temperature. That exponential drives every slow, hot process in the subject.

\[n_v=N\exp\!\left(\frac{-Q_v}{kT}\right)\]

\(N\) is the total number of atomic sites, \(Q_v\) is the energy needed to make one vacancy, \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant and \(T\) is the absolute temperature in kelvin.

Atoms can only move through a solid by hopping into neighbouring empty sites, so the vacancy count sets the speed of diffusion. That in turn sets how fast creep happens, how quickly a heat treatment homogenises, and how well powders sinter.

The exam point is almost always the shape of this relationship rather than a number. Temperature sits inside an exponential, so a modest heating gives a dramatic increase. Say "exponentially", never "a bit".

Feeds: creep in Week 5, which is diffusion-driven and therefore inherits this temperature sensitivity.
Say it cold: "Vacancies let atoms move, and their number rises exponentially with temperature, so hot processes speed up dramatically."

3 Grain size and Hall–Petch

A metal is a mosaic of small crystals. Dislocations pile up at the boundaries between them, so making the grains smaller makes the metal stronger.

\[\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_y\,d^{-1/2}\]

\(\sigma_y\) is yield strength, \(d\) is the average grain diameter, and \(\sigma_0\) and \(k_y\) are constants for that material.

Each grain has its atomic planes pointing a different way, so a dislocation running through one grain hits a boundary and cannot simply continue — the slip directions do not line up. More boundaries means more stopping, and more boundaries is exactly what small grains give you.

The \(d^{-1/2}\) is worth internalising as a shape. Making grains four times smaller multiplies the grain-size contribution by \(\sqrt{4}=2\). Letting them grow four times larger halves it.

This is the one strengthening route that improves toughness at the same time, which is why grain refinement is so widely used.

Feeds: the strengthening comparison below, and any heat-treatment question where grain growth is the hidden downside.
Say it cold: "Finer grains mean more boundaries to stop dislocations, and strength scales with one over the square root of grain size."

4 The four strengthening routes

Four methods with four names, all doing the same job: putting obstacles in front of dislocations. And all four charge the same price.

RouteThe obstacle it createsThe cost
Grain refinementMore grain boundariesLittle — this one also improves toughness
Solid solutionForeign atoms distorting the latticeSome ductility, and often conductivity
Strain hardening (cold work)Dislocations tangling with each otherDuctility, noticeably
PrecipitationHard second-phase particlesDuctility, and it can be undone by heat

The shared price is the important half. Strength up almost always means ductility down, because you obstructed the very mechanism that let the metal deform gracefully. A question asking for a strengthening method usually wants the trade-off named too.

Annealing is the reverse gear. Heat a cold-worked metal and it passes through recovery, then recrystallisation — where new strain-free grains replace the deformed ones and most of the softening happens — and then grain growth, which is where you start losing the strength you wanted to keep.

Feeds: Week 4, where these methods raise the curve but do nothing for a part that already has a crack in it.
Say it cold: "Grain refinement, solid solution, strain hardening and precipitation all block dislocations, and all trade ductility for strength."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — grain growth during heat treatment

Given: Grains grow from 10 µm to 40 µm during a heat treatment.
  1. The grain-size term is \(k_y d^{-1/2}\), so only \(d^{-1/2}\) changes.
  2. Grains are four times larger, so \(d^{-1/2}\) scales by \(1/\sqrt{4}=0.5\).
  3. The grain-boundary contribution to yield strength halves.
  4. Note the wording carefully: the contribution halves, not the yield strength itself. The \(\sigma_0\) term is untouched.
What it means: This is why over-long or over-hot treatments backfire. You may have dissolved the precipitates you wanted, but you coarsened the grains and gave back strength doing it.

Worked example 2 — comparing two alloys

Given: Alloy B has grains four times the diameter of alloy A. Same material otherwise.
  1. \(d_B=4d_A\), so \(d_B^{-1/2}=(4d_A)^{-1/2}=0.5\,d_A^{-1/2}\).
  2. B gets half the grain-size strengthening that A gets.
  3. A is the stronger alloy on this mechanism.
What it means: Ratio questions like this never need the constants. Recognising that everything except \(d\) cancels is the entire skill being tested.

Worked example 3 — reading the temperature trend

Why does raising the temperature accelerate creep and sintering so sharply?

  1. Vacancies follow \(n_v=N\exp(-Q_v/kT)\).
  2. Raising \(T\) makes \(Q_v/kT\) smaller, so the negative exponent moves toward zero and the exponential term grows.
  3. More vacancies means atoms have more empty sites to hop into, so diffusion speeds up.
  4. Creep, sintering and homogenisation are all diffusion-controlled, so all three accelerate together.
What it means: Say "exponentially". An answer that says temperature "increases diffusion" describes the direction but misses the sensitivity, and the sensitivity is the examinable part.

Where marks get lost

  • Using °C in the vacancy equation. \(T\) must be in kelvin. This is the most common single error in Week 3.
  • Saying temperature "increases" diffusion. Say exponentially — the whole point of that equation is the sensitivity.
  • Getting Hall–Petch backwards. Smaller grains give higher strength. The negative exponent trips people up; sanity-check with a number.
  • Claiming the yield strength halves when grains coarsen fourfold. Only the \(k_yd^{-1/2}\) contribution halves; \(\sigma_0\) does not move.
  • Naming a strengthening method with no trade-off. Except for grain refinement, they all cost ductility, and the examiner is looking for that sentence.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

Carbon dissolved in iron sits in the gaps between the iron atoms rather than replacing them. What kind of defect is that?

  1. A vacancy
  2. A substitutional point defect
  3. An interstitial point defect
  4. A grain boundary
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. An interstitial defect is a small foreign atom wedged into a gap — an interstice — between the host atoms. Carbon in iron is the standard example and the reason steel exists. A substitutional defect (option B) is a foreign atom of similar size sitting in place of a host atom, and a vacancy (option A) is an empty site where an atom should be. A grain boundary is not a point defect at all; it is the mismatched join between two crystals. Design meaning: either kind of foreign atom distorts the surrounding lattice, and that distortion obstructs dislocation movement — which is solid solution strengthening.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

Which statement about a dislocation is correct?

  1. It is a line defect whose movement lets one plane of atoms slip progressively
  2. It is a small crack that grows each time the metal is loaded
  3. It is an empty atomic site whose population rises with temperature
  4. It is the boundary between two grains of different orientation
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. A dislocation is a line-shaped interruption in the atomic pattern, and plastic deformation is these lines travelling through the crystal. The rug analogy is the one to hold onto: dragging a whole heavy rug at once is nearly impossible, but pushing a ripple along it and letting the ripple travel is easy. Option B is the single most costly confusion in this week — a dislocation is not a crack, it does not separate the material, and it is a normal feature of every metal you have handled. Option C describes a vacancy. Design meaning: because only a handful of bonds break at the moving front, real metals yield at stresses perhaps a hundred times below what a perfect crystal would need.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A metal follows \(\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_yd^{-1/2}\) with \(\sigma_0=60\) MPa and \(k_y=0.5\) MPa·m\(^{1/2}\). What is the yield strength at an average grain diameter of \(d=25\) µm?

  1. 60 MPa
  2. 110 MPa
  3. 160 MPa
  4. 260 MPa
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Convert the grain size to metres so it matches the units of \(k_y\): \(d=25\) µm \(=25\times10^{-6}\) m. Then \(\sqrt d=\sqrt{2.5\times10^{-5}}=5.0\times10^{-3}\) m\(^{1/2}\), so \(d^{-1/2}=1/(5.0\times10^{-3})=200\) m\(^{-1/2}\). The grain-size contribution is \(k_yd^{-1/2}=0.5\times200=100\) MPa, and \(\sigma_y=60+100=160\) MPa. Sanity check: the answer must be larger than \(\sigma_0=60\) MPa, because grain boundaries can only add strength — so option A is impossible by inspection. Design meaning: here the grain structure supplies more of the yield strength than the base lattice does, which is why grain size is worth controlling so carefully.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

A cold-drawn copper wire is annealed, but the furnace is left both hotter and longer than the process sheet specifies. What is the most likely outcome?

  1. The wire keeps its cold-work strength and simply becomes more ductile
  2. Recrystallisation removes the cold-work strengthening, and continued grain growth then costs further strength
  3. The wire becomes stronger, because heating adds vacancies and vacancies obstruct dislocations
  4. Nothing changes, because annealing only affects surface finish
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Annealing passes through three stages in order: recovery, then recrystallisation — where new strain-free grains grow and replace the deformed ones, and where most of the softening happens — and then grain growth. The intended stop point is once ductility is restored. Overshooting on both temperature and time carries the wire into grain growth, and coarser grains reduce the Hall–Petch term \(k_yd^{-1/2}\), so strength falls further than planned. Why the trap works: option A is what people want annealing to do, but strength and ductility move in opposite directions here as everywhere else in this week — you cannot restore ductility by removing tangled dislocations and keep the strength those tangles provided. Option C misreads the role of vacancies: they let atoms diffuse, which is what enables recrystallisation and grain growth in the first place.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: in \(n_v=N\exp(-Q_v/kT)\), the number of vacancies rises exponentially with absolute temperature, so a modest increase in temperature can multiply the vacancy count many times over.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Temperature sits inside the exponential, in the denominator of the exponent. Raising \(T\) makes \(Q_v/kT\) smaller, so the negative exponent moves toward zero and the whole term grows sharply. Here \(n_v\) is the number of vacancies, \(N\) the total number of atomic sites, \(Q_v\) the energy needed to create one vacancy, and \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant (\(1.38\times10^{-23}\) J/K, or \(8.62\times10^{-5}\) eV/K — match its units to \(Q_v\)). Design meaning: atoms can only move through a solid by hopping into empty sites, so the vacancy count sets the diffusion rate, which in turn sets how fast creep, sintering and homogenisation proceed. The examinable habit is to say "exponentially" rather than "a bit", because the sensitivity is the point.
Q6 · True/falseTrap

True or false: halving the average grain diameter of a metal doubles its yield strength.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False — on two separate counts. First, the grain-size term varies as \(d^{-1/2}\), not as \(d^{-1}\). Halving \(d\) multiplies that term by \(\sqrt2\approx1.41\), not by 2. Second, only the \(k_yd^{-1/2}\) contribution responds at all — the \(\sigma_0\) term is a property of the lattice and does not move — so the total yield strength rises by even less than 41%. Worked check with the Q3 numbers: going from 25 µm to 12.5 µm takes the contribution from 100 MPa to \(100\times1.41=141\) MPa, so \(\sigma_y\) goes from 160 MPa to 201 MPa. That is a 26% rise in yield strength, nowhere near double. Why the trap works: the direction is right — finer grains really are stronger — so the statement feels correct until you check the exponent and remember the constant term.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

A metal has a vacancy formation energy \(Q_v=0.90\) eV. Using \(k=8.62\times10^{-5}\) eV/K, find the equilibrium vacancy fraction \(n_v/N\) at 500 K and at 1000 K, and state the factor between them.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: about \(8.5\times10^{-10}\) at 500 K and \(2.9\times10^{-5}\) at 1000 K — roughly 34,000 times more vacancies.
1. Rearrange to the fraction: \(n_v/N=\exp(-Q_v/kT)\). Both \(Q_v\) and \(k\) are in electronvolt units here, so the exponent is dimensionless, as it must be.
2. At \(T=500\) K: \(kT=(8.62\times10^{-5}\text{ eV/K})(500\text{ K})=0.0431\) eV, so the exponent is \(-0.90/0.0431=-20.9\) and \(n_v/N=e^{-20.9}=8.5\times10^{-10}\).
3. At \(T=1000\) K: \(kT=0.0862\) eV, so the exponent is \(-0.90/0.0862=-10.4\) and \(n_v/N=e^{-10.4}=2.9\times10^{-5}\).
4. Ratio: \(2.9\times10^{-5}/8.5\times10^{-10}\approx3.4\times10^{4}\). Equivalently, doubling \(T\) halves the exponent, so the ratio is just \(e^{10.4}\).
Sanity checks: both fractions must be far below 1, since only a tiny minority of sites are ever empty — a value above 1 would be nonsense. Both temperatures must be in kelvin; entering 227 °C and 727 °C would change the exponent completely and is the single most common error of this week. And the direction must be an increase with temperature.
Design meaning: doubling the absolute temperature multiplied the vacancy population by about 34,000, and diffusion tracks that population. This is why creep, sintering and homogenisation all accelerate so violently with heat, and why a service temperature limit is not a soft recommendation.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A steel obeys Hall–Petch with \(\sigma_0=70\) MPa and \(k_y=0.60\) MPa·m\(^{1/2}\). What average grain diameter, in micrometres, is needed to reach a yield strength of 250 MPa?

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: about 11 µm.
1. Start from \(\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_yd^{-1/2}\) and isolate the grain-size term: \(k_yd^{-1/2}=\sigma_y-\sigma_0=250-70=180\) MPa.
2. Divide out the constant: \(d^{-1/2}=180/0.60=300\) m\(^{-1/2}\).
3. Invert to get the square root of the diameter: \(\sqrt d=1/300=3.33\times10^{-3}\) m\(^{1/2}\).
4. Square: \(d=(3.33\times10^{-3})^2=1.11\times10^{-5}\) m \(=11.1\) µm.
Sanity checks: the required strength is well above \(\sigma_0\), so the answer must be a fine grain size — and typical engineering grain sizes are single-figure to tens of micrometres, so 11 µm is credible. Cross-check by substituting back: \(d^{-1/2}=1/\sqrt{1.11\times10^{-5}}=300\), and \(70+0.60(300)=250\) MPa. If your answer came out in metres and looked like 11, a unit conversion was dropped.
Design meaning: this converts a strength requirement into a processing requirement. Hitting 11 µm is a job for controlled rolling and cooling rate, and once achieved it must be protected — any later heat treatment hot enough to coarsen the grains gives the strength straight back. Grain refinement is also the one strengthening route that improves toughness at the same time, so it is the first one to reach for.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

A graph plots two curves for a cold-worked brass against annealing temperature on the horizontal axis: tensile strength falling and ductility rising. Both curves are flat at low temperature, then change steeply over a middle band, then flatten again — except that the strength curve continues to creep downward at the highest temperatures. Name the three process stages the horizontal axis passes through, say which one the middle band is, and state where you would stop the furnace.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: recovery, recrystallisation and grain growth; the steep middle band is recrystallisation; stop just past the end of that band.
The flat low-temperature region — recovery. Internal strain energy is relieved and some dislocations rearrange, but the grains are still the deformed ones. This is why strength has barely moved and ductility has barely recovered.
The steep middle band — recrystallisation. Brand-new strain-free grains nucleate and grow, consuming the deformed structure. The tangled dislocation network that supplied the cold-work strengthening is swept away, so strength drops sharply and ductility climbs sharply. Both curves change steeply at the same temperatures because they are two views of the same event.
The continued downward creep at high temperature — grain growth. Recrystallisation is finished, so there is no more cold work to remove, yet strength keeps sliding. The cause has changed: the new grains are now coarsening, and a larger \(d\) shrinks the Hall–Petch term \(k_yd^{-1/2}\). Ductility has already plateaued, so this stage is loss with no gain.
Where to stop: just past the top of the recrystallisation band, where ductility has plateaued but grain growth has not yet taken hold. That gives the formability the annealing was for while keeping the finest possible grain size.
Reading habit worth carrying: when two curves move steeply together and then one keeps drifting alone, the drift is a different mechanism. Naming the mechanism behind each part of the shape is what these questions reward.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

An overhead conductor must be made stronger so it can span further between poles, but its electrical conductivity must not drop measurably. Two proposals are on the table: alloy the copper with 2% of a dissolved element, or refine the grain size through controlled processing. Recommend one, and justify it through the mechanism.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: refine the grain size.
Step 1 — what the two proposals have in common. Both are strengthening routes and both work the same way: they put obstacles in the path of moving dislocations. Solid solution strengthening uses foreign atoms that distort the lattice; grain refinement uses grain boundaries, where the slip directions of adjacent grains do not line up, so a dislocation arriving at one cannot simply continue.
Step 2 — what separates them is the price each charges. Solid solution strengthening costs some ductility and, critically here, conductivity. The dissolved atoms are exactly the lattice irregularities that scatter conduction electrons, so the mechanism that buys the strength is the mechanism that spoils the conduction. There is no way to have one without the other. Grain refinement charges very little: grain boundaries are a much smaller obstacle to electrons than randomly dispersed foreign atoms, and this is the one route that improves toughness rather than trading it away.
Step 3 — the recommendation, with its own condition attached. Refine the grain size, and add the processing constraint: the fine grain structure must survive everything that happens afterwards. Any subsequent heat exposure hot enough to drive grain growth will take back the strength, because the gain lives entirely in the \(k_yd^{-1/2}\) term and that term shrinks as \(d\) rises.
What a full-mark answer includes: the trade-off named explicitly for both options, not just the recommendation. A strengthening answer with no price attached is incomplete, because in this week every route except grain refinement costs ductility, and this particular route was chosen precisely because of what it does not cost.
