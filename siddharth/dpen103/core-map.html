How the atoms are arranged decides what the material does. How you make and treat it decides the arrangement. How it behaves in service decides whether you chose right. Almost every exam question is one arrow of that chain, and design questions run the whole chain backwards: performance needed → property targets → structure → material.

The words and symbols, in plain English

This subject shortens a lot of things to initials, and lecturers tend to start using them without stopping to say what they stand for. Here is the full list up front, so nothing below arrives as a surprise. Come back to this table any time a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
Words you will hear constantly
Stress, \(\sigma\)force per unit areaHow hard the material is being squeezed or pulled at a point. Force spread over area, so a thin bar is under more stress than a thick one carrying the same load.

Ductility—How far it stretches before breaking. High ductility means it bends a lot first and gives you warning.
Brittle—The opposite: it breaks with almost no stretching, and with no warning.
Toughness—How much energy it soaks up before breaking. Strong and tough are not the same thing.
Necking—When a bar being pulled starts thinning at one spot instead of stretching evenly. It is the beginning of the end.

Grain—One small crystal within the metal. A metal is a mosaic of thousands of them, all facing different ways.

Phases and processing
Phase—A region with one consistent structure and composition. Ice and water are two phases of one substance.
Microstructure—The arrangement of phases and grains you would see under a microscope. Processing controls it; it controls properties.
Liquidus—On a phase diagram, the line above which everything is liquid.
Solidus—The line below which everything is solid. Between the two, you have a mush of both.
Solvus—A line showing how much of one solid will dissolve in another as temperature changes.

Cold work—Deliberately deforming a metal at room temperature to make it stronger and harder.

Thermoset—A plastic whose chains are chemically tied together, so it can never be re-melted.

TIER 1 The two roots Week 1 · learn before anything else

Nothing in this subject is above these. Every later explanation eventually says "because of how the atoms are bonded" or "because of how they are stacked". Get these two cheap and everything downstream costs less.

1 Bonding type

How atoms are held together decides the personality of the material. Four kinds, and you only need the behaviour each one predicts.

Know these four
  • Metallic — electrons roam free. Conducts, bends rather than snaps, shiny.
  • Ionic — plus and minus locked together. Hard, brittle, insulating.
  • Covalent — electrons shared in fixed directions. Very stiff, very brittle, high melting point.
  • Secondary (van der Waals, hydrogen) — weak stickiness between chains. Why polymers are soft and melt low.
Feeds: the stress–strain shape (card 3), all of card 8 (electrical and magnetic), and why polymers behave as they do (card 9a).
Say it cold: "Metals conduct and bend because the electrons are shared loosely; ceramics insulate and snap because the bonds are locked in place."

2 Crystal packing

Metal atoms stack in a repeating pattern, like oranges in a crate. Which pattern you get changes how easily the layers can slide over each other, and that is what makes one metal easy to bend and another stiff and stubborn.

The three names, spelled out
  • FCC — face-centred cubic. A cube with an atom at each corner and one in the middle of every face.
  • BCC — body-centred cubic. A cube with an atom at each corner and one right in the centre.
  • HCP — hexagonal close-packed. Layers of hexagons stacked in an ABAB pattern.
PatternAtoms in the boxNeighbours each atom touchesFraction of space filled
FCC4120.74
BCC280.68
HCP6120.74

That last column has a formal name you will see in lectures: the atomic packing factor, often shortened to APF. It just means the fraction of the box that is actually atom rather than empty gap. 0.74 is the tightest that equal spheres can ever be packed, so FCC and HCP are as dense as it gets.

\[\rho=\frac{nA}{V_c N_A}\]

This works out density from the box. \(n\) is atoms in the box, \(A\) is the atomic mass, \(V_c\) is the volume of the box, and \(N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\) is Avogadro's number.

Feeds: how ductile a metal is — FCC has more easy sliding planes, so FCC metals like aluminium and copper are generally the most formable. Also feeds card 4, because a defect is only meaningful once there is a pattern for it to break.
Say it cold: "FCC fills 74% of the space and each atom touches 12 others, and it usually deforms most easily. BCC is looser at 68% with 8 neighbours."
TIER 2 The two workhorses Weeks 2–3 · used in nearly every question

If you only had time for two cards in the whole subject, these are the two. The curve is where the numbers come from. Defects are where the explanations come from.

3 The stress–strain curve

A map of what happens when you pull a bar until it breaks. Learn the landmarks in order and most mechanical questions are just "point at the right spot".

\[\sigma=\frac{F}{A_0}\qquad \varepsilon=\frac{\Delta L}{L_0}\qquad E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\]
The five landmarks
  • The straight part — this is the elastic region, where the bar springs back to its original length if you let go. Its slope is \(E\), the stiffness, properly called Young's modulus.
  • Yield, written \(\sigma_y\) — the point where the bar stops springing back and starts staying bent. Because the bend in the curve is gradual, there is a standard way of pinning it down called the 0.2% offset method, which is drawn out on the pictures page.
  • The highest point — the most stress the material ever carries. Its full name is the ultimate tensile strength, and it is almost always shortened to UTS. Just after this point the bar starts thinning at one spot instead of stretching evenly, which is called necking.
  • Where the curve ends — how far the bar stretched before breaking. That is its ductility.
  • The area underneath — the total energy the bar soaked up before breaking. That is toughness.
\[U_r=\frac{\sigma_y^{\,2}}{2E}\]

\(U_r\) is resilience: the energy stored in the springy part only, the bit you get back. Toughness is the whole area including the permanent damage; resilience is just the triangle at the start.

Builds on: cards 1–2.  Feeds: everything with a number in it — fracture, fatigue, composites, thermal stress.
Say it cold: Sketch the curve from blank paper and label elastic, yield, UTS, fracture, and shade toughness. Under 60 seconds.

4 Defects and flaws

Real crystals are imperfect, and the imperfections are the whole story of strength. A perfect crystal would be far stronger than any real metal.

Four to name
  • Vacancy — a missing atom. Lets atoms diffuse.
  • Dislocation — a line defect. It is how metals bend: layers slip by moving it along.
  • Grain boundary — where two crystal orientations meet. Blocks dislocations.
  • Crack / pore — a big flaw. Owns card 7.
\[n_v=N\exp\!\left(\frac{-Q_v}{kT}\right)\]

How many vacancies you get. \(N\) is the number of atom sites available, \(Q_v\) is the energy it costs to make one vacancy, \(k\) is Boltzmann's constant, and \(T\) is temperature in kelvin. The shape to remember is that heating creates vacancies very fast.

\[\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_y\,d^{-1/2}\]

This one is named after the two people who found it, Hall and Petch. \(d\) is the grain size, \(\sigma_0\) is the strength you would have with no grain boundaries at all, and \(k_y\) is a constant for that material. Because \(d\) is on the bottom, smaller grains give a stronger metal.

Builds on: card 2.  Feeds: cards 5, 7, and creep in card 10.
Say it cold: "Metals bend because dislocations glide. Anything that gets in a dislocation's way makes the metal stronger and less ductile."

Tier 2 says what a material is. Tier 3 says how you change it and how it lets you down. These three cards carry most of the numerical marks.

5 Strengthening (four routes, one idea)

Every strengthening method does the same thing: put obstacles in front of dislocations. And every one costs ductility.

The four
  • Grain refinement — more boundaries. Hall–Petch.
  • Solid solution — foreign atoms distort the lattice.
  • Strain hardening (cold work) — dislocations tangle each other.
  • Precipitation — hard particles block the path.

Going the other way: annealing (recovery, recrystallisation, grain growth) softens and gives ductility back.

Builds on: card 4.  Related to: card 6 — heat treatment is how you get the microstructure in the first place.
Say it cold: Name all four routes and the one sentence they share, plus the tradeoff. No notes.

6 Phase diagrams and the lever rule

A map of what you have at a given mix and temperature. Pick your composition and temperature, and the diagram tells you which phases exist and how much of each.

The routine, every time
  • Mark overall composition \(C_0\) and temperature \(T\).
  • One phase region? Done — it is all that phase.
  • Two-phase region? Draw the horizontal tie-line at that \(T\), read \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_\beta\) at its ends.
  • Apply the lever rule.
\[W_\alpha=\frac{C_\beta-C_0}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}\qquad W_\beta=\frac{C_0-C_\alpha}{C_\beta-C_\alpha}\]

It is just mass balance: \(W_\alpha+W_\beta=1\) and \(C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta\). Each weight uses the far end of the tie-line.

Feeds: card 5 (microstructure decides strength) and card 9 (which class you end up with).
Say it cold: "The fraction of a phase uses the distance to the opposite end of the tie-line, divided by the whole line."

7 Fracture — flaws decide strength

A material with a crack in it is not as strong as the same material without one. The crack concentrates stress at its tip, and there is a threshold past which it runs.

\[K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\qquad\text{it fails when } K_I\ge K_{IC}\]

Two symbols worth naming properly, because they look alike and mean opposite things. \(K_I\) is the stress intensity: a single number that combines how hard you are pulling with how big the crack is. \(K_{IC}\) is the fracture toughness: a property of the material, the largest \(K_I\) it can survive. So one is the demand and the other is the limit. \(Y\) is a shape factor close to 1 that accounts for where the crack sits, and \(a\) is the crack size.

\[\sigma_{\text{allow}}=\frac{K_{IC}}{Y\sqrt{\pi a}}\]

The square root is the point. Make the crack four times bigger and the stress you are allowed to apply halves — it does not drop to a quarter. So a small amount of crack growth forces a big cut in working load.

Builds on: card 4.  Feeds: fatigue (card 10) and ceramics (card 9b) — both are this equation wearing a different hat.
Say it cold: Write \(K_I=Y\sigma\sqrt{\pi a}\), then say what happens to allowable stress when \(a\) quadruples.
TIER 4 Service, classes, and function Weeks 7 & 9–12 · branches, not new foundations

These look like five new topics. They are five applications of tiers 1–3. Each card below opens by naming which earlier card it is standing on — hold onto that and the volume shrinks.

10 Service conditions: fatigue, creep, thermal

Three ways a part dies without the load ever being big enough to break it on day one.

Fatigue — the same load, over and over

A crack starts at the surface, usually at a sharp corner, and grows a tiny amount every time the load cycles. Eventually it is big enough to break the part, at a load it carried happily for years. The standard graph plots the stress against the number of cycles the part survives, and because those are labelled S and N, everyone calls it an S–N curve. The fixes are to lower the stress, smooth the surface finish, round off sharp corners, or squeeze the surface into compression so cracks cannot open.

Creep — hot and slow

A part under a steady load slowly stretches over months or years, without the load ever changing. It only matters when the material is hot — roughly above \(0.4\,T_m\), where \(T_m\) is the melting temperature measured in kelvin. That is why lead creeps in your hand and steel does not. There are three stages, and the steady middle stage is the one design calculations use.

Thermal
\[\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\qquad \sigma_{\text{th}}\sim E\alpha\Delta T \text{ (if constrained)}\]
\[q_x=-kA\frac{dT}{dx}\]

Free to expand → strain, little stress. Bolted down → stress.

Builds on: card 7 (fatigue is crack growth), card 4 (creep needs vacancies to diffuse).
Say it cold: "Fatigue is repeated small loads growing a crack. Creep is hot and slow. Thermal stress only appears when expansion is blocked."

9a Polymers

Plastics: very long chain molecules tangled together. One question governs almost everything about how they behave, and it is whether they are being used above or below their glass transition temperature.

The glass transition temperature, written \(T_g\), is the temperature at which a polymer changes from hard and glassy to soft and rubbery. It is not melting — the material is still solid on both sides of it. \(T_m\) is the separate, higher melting temperature where it actually flows.

  • Used below \(T_g\) — glassy: stiff and brittle, snaps rather than bends.
  • Used above \(T_g\) — rubbery: soft, stretchy, absorbs impact.
  • Longer chains — more tangling, so tougher, but also thicker when melted and therefore harder to mould.
  • More crosslinks (chemical bridges tying chains together) — stiffer, and it can never be melted down again.
  • Time matters — polymers slowly stretch under a steady load even at room temperature.
\[DP_n=\frac{M_n}{m}\]

This gives the degree of polymerisation — simply how many repeat units are joined together in an average chain. \(M_n\) is the average weight of a whole chain and \(m\) is the weight of one repeat unit, so dividing gives you the count.

Builds on: card 1. The chains themselves are held together by strong covalent bonds, but the chains are only stuck to each other by weak secondary bonds, and that mismatch explains nearly all polymer behaviour.
Say it cold: "Never say anything about a polymer without first saying whether it is being used above or below its glass transition temperature."

9b Ceramics

Card 7 with the volume turned up. Strong bonds, no dislocation glide to relieve stress, so whatever flaw is present decides the strength.

\[\sigma_f\approx\frac{K_{IC}}{Y\sqrt{\pi a}}\]

Same equation as card 7, just rearranged and renamed. \(\sigma_f\) is the stress at which this particular piece will fracture, set by \(a\), the biggest flaw that happens to be in it.

  • Strength is not one number — it scatters with the flaw population.
  • Much stronger in compression than tension, because compression does not open cracks.
  • Surface finish is a mechanical property lever: chipping from machining = bigger flaws = weaker part.
Builds on: cards 1 and 7.  Same equation as fracture, applied where there is no plasticity to save you.
Say it cold: "Two ceramic batches with identical chemistry can test differently, because strength follows the biggest flaw."

9c Composites

Stiff fibres carrying load inside a soft matrix that holds them in place and transfers load into them. Direction is everything.

\[E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m \qquad \frac{1}{E_T}=\frac{V_f}{E_f}+\frac{V_m}{E_m}\]

The subscript \(f\) means fibre and \(m\) means matrix, so \(E_f\) is the stiffness of the fibre and \(V_f\) is the fraction of the volume that is fibre. \(E_L\) is the stiffness measured along the fibres and \(E_T\) is the stiffness measured across them.

  • Along the fibres — a simple weighted average, and since the fibres are far stiffer, you get most of their stiffness.
  • Across the fibres — much weaker, because the load has to pass through the soft matrix and the softest part always dominates.
\[l_c=\frac{\sigma_f^{*}d}{2\tau_c}\]

This is the critical fibre length: the shortest a fibre can be and still take up its full share of the load. \(\sigma_f^{*}\) is the fibre's own strength, \(d\) is the fibre diameter, and \(\tau_c\) is how well the fibre is glued to the matrix. Fibres shorter than \(l_c\) slide out instead of breaking, so their strength is wasted.

Builds on: card 3. You cannot blend two stiffnesses until you know what stiffness means, which is the slope of the stress–strain curve.
Say it cold: "Along the fibres it is a weighted average. Across them it is far lower. And fibres shorter than the critical length do not pull their weight."

8a Electrical properties

Straight out of card 1. Free electrons conduct; locked electrons do not.

\[\sigma=nq\mu\qquad \rho=\frac{1}{\sigma}\qquad R=\frac{\rho L}{A}\]

A "carrier" is anything that carries charge through the material, usually an electron. \(n\) is how many carriers there are, \(q\) is the charge on each one, and \(\mu\) (mobility) is how freely they can move. \(\sigma\) is conductivity, how well it conducts. \(\rho\) is resistivity, the exact opposite, and \(R\) is the resistance of one particular part with length \(L\) and cross-sectional area \(A\).

  • Conductivity is a product of how many carriers and how fast they move. Adding impurities to raise \(n\) also makes the carriers bump into things more, which lowers \(\mu\), so conductivity can barely change.
  • \(\rho\) describes the material. \(R\) describes an actual object. Never use one where the other is wanted.
\[R_H\approx-\frac{1}{nq}\]

\(R_H\) is the Hall coefficient. Put a current through a material in a magnetic field and the carriers get pushed sideways, producing a small voltage across the sample. Measuring it tells you how many carriers there are and whether they are negative or positive.

Builds on: card 1 and nothing else. This branch does not need the mechanical chain at all, which is why it can be learned on its own.
Say it cold: "Conductivity is how many carriers times how freely they move. Resistance puts the size and shape of the part back in."

8b Magnetic properties

Drive a magnetic field back and forth through a material and plot what you get out against what you put in. The shape of the loop you trace answers almost every question, and a fat loop wastes energy while a thin one does not.

\[M=\chi H\qquad B=\mu_0(H+M)\]

\(H\) is the magnetic field you apply. \(M\) is how strongly the material magnetises in response. \(B\) is the total field you end up with inside it. \(\chi\) (the Greek letter chi) is susceptibility, meaning how eagerly the material responds, and \(\mu_0\) is a fixed constant of nature.

  • Soft magnetic — thin loop. It magnetises and demagnetises easily, so it wastes very little energy each time you flip it. Used for transformer cores, motors, and anything running on alternating current.
  • Hard magnetic — fat loop. It is difficult to demagnetise, so it holds onto its field. Used for permanent magnets.
  • The area inside the loop is the energy lost as heat every time you go round it once. At mains frequency that is fifty times a second, so it adds up fast. This is the number that drives the design choice.

Two words you will meet on this diagram: remanence is how much magnetism is left when you switch the field off, and coercivity is how hard you have to push in the opposite direction to wipe it out.

Pairs with: card 8a. Both of these are about what a material can do, rather than whether it will break.
Say it cold: "Thin loop means soft, and soft is for alternating current. Fat loop means hard, and hard is for permanent magnets. The area is the energy wasted."
TIER 5 The thing it is all for Weeks 8 & 13 · the design question

11 Materials selection — the answer structure

Design questions are not "name the best material". They are constrained choices, and the marks are in the reasoning, including why you rejected things.

Six steps, in this order
  • Function — what must the part actually do?
  • Hard constraints — turn words into property targets (must not yield, must not deflect, must not corrode, must be light).
  • Failure modes — yield, fracture, fatigue, creep, corrosion. Name them before comparing anything.
  • Shortlist by material class and the properties that matter.
  • Screen by manufacturing route and cost / lifecycle.
  • Justify — say why the winner won and why each loser lost.
And the five-line structure for any calculation question
  • Classify: is this mechanical, thermal, electrical, magnetic, phase, or composite?
  • State assumptions and units.
  • One governing equation.
  • Check the trend makes physical sense.
  • Finish with one sentence of design meaning.
Say it cold: "If I cannot say why a candidate was rejected, my selection answer is incomplete."

Five patterns that keep coming back

These shapes repeat across unrelated topics. Learning the shape once means five topics get cheaper.

1 · The square-root pattern — cracks

\(\sigma \propto 1/\sqrt{a}\). Quadruple the flaw, halve the allowable stress. Appears in fracture, in ceramics, and behind fatigue life.

2 · The inverse-square-root pattern — grain size

\(\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_y d^{-1/2}\). Grains four times bigger → the strengthening term halves. Same maths as pattern 1, opposite direction of benefit.

3 · The exponential pattern — temperature

\(n_v=N\exp(-Q_v/kT)\). Anything diffusion-driven — vacancies, creep, sintering, grain growth — accelerates sharply with temperature. In an answer, say "exponentially", not "a bit".

4 · The weighted-average pattern — mixtures

The lever rule and the composite rule of mixtures are the same algebra: a total is split between two contributors in proportion to how much of each you have. \(C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta\) and \(E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m\).

5 · The obstacle pattern — strength versus ductility

Block dislocations and yield goes up while ductility goes down. Grain refinement, alloying, cold work, precipitates — four names, one mechanism, one price. Annealing runs it backwards.

Not for marks — for catching an answer that is off by a thousand.

Traps that cost marks

  • Using the current area instead of \(A_0\). Engineering stress always divides by the original area.
  • Lever rule with the near end. \(W_\alpha\) uses the distance to \(C_\beta\) — the far end. Sanity check: if \(C_0\) sits close to \(C_\alpha\), then \(W_\alpha\) should be large.
  • Reading the tie-line at the wrong temperature. The tie-line is horizontal, at the temperature you were given.
  • Treating crack growth as linear. It is a square root. Four times the crack, half the stress.
  • Hall–Petch upside down. \(d^{-1/2}\) means smaller grains are stronger.
  • Mixing \(\rho\) and \(R\). Resistivity is the material. Resistance needs \(L\) and \(A\).
  • Discussing a polymer without a service temperature. Stiff or floppy depends entirely on \(T\) versus \(T_g\).
  • Quoting one strength for a ceramic. It scatters with flaw size, and compression is a different story from tension.
  • Composite stiffness in the wrong direction. \(E_L\) is the impressive number; \(E_T\) is what fails in service.
  • Saying temperature "increases" diffusion. Say exponentially.
  • Forgetting the design sentence. Most short-answer questions want one line of "so the part should…" at the end.

How to practise it — the three-rung ladder

Same content, three depths. Do not move a card up a rung until the rung below is automatic. This is what stops revision turning into re-reading.

Rung 1 · Name

Can you say it?

Cover the card. Say the green line out loud. Write the formula and the units of every symbol. Ten cards in ten minutes.

Twelve recall prompts

Answer out loud first, then open. If you can do all twelve without notes, the core is in place.

1. Why does a metal bend where a ceramic snaps?
Metals have delocalised electrons and planes that can slip via dislocation movement, so they deform plastically. Ceramic bonds are locked and directional with no easy dislocation glide, so stress builds at a flaw until it fractures.
2. Point at yield, UTS and toughness on a stress–strain curve.
Yield is where the line stops being straight (read by 0.2% offset). UTS is the highest point. Toughness is the whole area under the curve up to fracture. Resilience is only the triangle under the elastic part.
3. Grains grow from 10 µm to 40 µm. What happens to Hall–Petch strengthening?
Four times bigger, so \(d^{-1/2}\) scales by \(1/\sqrt{4}=0.5\). The grain-boundary contribution to yield halves.
4. Name the four strengthening routes and the one thing they share.
Grain refinement, solid solution, strain hardening, precipitation. All four obstruct dislocation movement, and all four trade ductility for yield strength.
5. A crack grows from 2 mm to 8 mm. What happens to allowable stress?
\(\sigma_{\text{allow}}=K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), so it scales by \(1/\sqrt{4}=1/2\). The allowable stress halves.
6. \(C_\alpha=10\), \(C_\beta=70\), \(C_0=40\) wt%. Find both phase fractions.
\(W_\alpha=(70-40)/(70-10)=30/60=0.5\) and \(W_\beta=(40-10)/60=0.5\). Half and half, which makes sense since \(C_0\) sits exactly midway.
7. Why does a bar heated 100 °C sometimes carry no stress and sometimes a lot?
Free to expand, the temperature change produces strain \(\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\) with little stress. Constrained, the expansion cannot happen, so stress of order \(E\alpha\Delta T\) appears instead.
8. Two polymers, same chemistry, one is stiff and one is floppy at room temperature. Why?
Their glass transition temperatures differ. The one with \(T_g\) above room temperature is glassy and stiff; the one with \(T_g\) below is rubbery. Crosslink density can also differ.
9. Doping doubles carrier density but conductivity barely moves. Explain.
\(\sigma=nq\mu\). The added dopant atoms scatter carriers, so mobility \(\mu\) falls roughly as much as \(n\) rises and the product stays similar.
10. \(E_f=230\) GPa, \(E_m=3\) GPa, \(V_f=0.55\). Longitudinal modulus?
\(E_L=230(0.55)+3(0.45)=126.5+1.35=127.85\) GPa. Note the matrix contributes almost nothing along the fibres.
11. Transformer core: soft or hard magnetic, and why?
Soft. It reverses magnetisation every cycle, and loop area is energy lost as heat per cycle, so you want a thin loop with low coercivity. Hard magnets would waste enormous energy.
12. Two ceramic batches, identical composition, different measured strength. Why?
Ceramic strength is flaw-controlled via \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\). Different surface finish or processing gives a different largest-flaw size, so measured strength scatters even with identical chemistry.
