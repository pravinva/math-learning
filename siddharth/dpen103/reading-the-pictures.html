DPEN103 · Materials in Design

Reading the Pictures

Most marks in this subject come from a diagram with a question attached, not from a blank-page derivation. That is a separate skill from knowing the formulas, and it is trainable: every diagram type has a fixed order you read it in. Twenty-two diagrams below, each with the reading order and worked questions. Cover the answers and say them out loud first.

The universal routine — use it on any diagram you have never seen.
  • Axes first. What is on x, what is on y, and which direction is "more"? Half of all diagram errors are answered here.
  • Find the landmarks. Peaks, kinks, plateaus, crossings, and where lines meet. Those are always where the marks are.
  • Name the regions. A diagram divided into areas wants you to say what each area is.
  • Then compare. Higher, steeper, wider, sooner — the question is usually a comparison in disguise.
  • Finish with meaning. One sentence: so what does this mean for the part?

Every technical word is spelled out where it first appears below. If one still catches you, the plain-English glossary on the core map lists all of them in one table.

A · Structure

Only one diagram type here, but it turns up in week 1 and never fully leaves.

P1

Unit cells — counting atoms

Core map card 2 · Week 1

A box drawn around the repeating pattern. Atoms sitting on a corner or a face are shared with neighbouring boxes, so they only count as a fraction.

Read it in this order
  1. Count corner atoms — each contributes ⅛.
  2. Count face atoms — each contributes ½. Edge atoms would be ¼.
  3. Count fully-inside atoms — each contributes 1.
  4. Add up. That total \(n\) is what goes into \(\rho=nA/(V_cN_A)\).
Q1 · The picture shows atoms only at the eight corners. How many atoms per cell?
\(8\times\tfrac18=1\). That arrangement is called simple cubic, and it is rare in real metals because it wastes so much space — only 52% of the box is filled.
Q2 · Which of these two deforms more easily, and what in the picture tells you?
FCC. The higher packing fraction (0.74) means close-packed planes exist that can slide over each other easily, so FCC metals like aluminium and copper are generally more formable. BCC at 0.68 has no true close-packed plane.
Q3 · Someone counts the FCC face atoms as whole atoms and gets 14. What went wrong?
Each face atom is split between two neighbouring cells, so it counts ½, not 1. \(8(\tfrac18)+6(\tfrac12)=1+3=4\).

B · The stress–strain curve and its relatives

This is the highest-value section on the page. Eight diagrams, all variations on one curve. If you can read these fluently you have covered a large share of the exam.

P2

The full curve — every landmark

Card 3 · Week 2

One bar, pulled until it breaks. Everything mechanical is somewhere on this picture. Two labels need spelling out before you start: UTS is short for ultimate tensile strength, which is simply the highest stress the material ever reaches — the peak of the curve. And necking is what happens just after that peak, when the bar stops stretching evenly and starts thinning at one spot.

Read it in this order
  1. Straight part → slope is \(E\). Steeper means stiffer.
  2. Where it stops being straight → yield \(\sigma_y\). Read across to the stress axis.
  3. Highest point → the ultimate tensile strength, UTS. Everything to the right of it is the bar thinning down at one spot.
  4. Where it ends → ductility, read down to the strain axis.
  5. Area under it → toughness. Just the elastic triangle → resilience.
Trap: yield and UTS are read across to the stress axis; ductility is read down to the strain axis. Mixing the two directions is the single most common diagram error in this subject.
Q1 · Why does the curve go down after UTS if the material is not getting weaker?
Because engineering stress divides by the original area \(A_0\). After UTS the bar necks, so the real area carrying load shrinks fast. The load falls, so \(F/A_0\) falls, even though the true stress in the neck is still climbing.
Q2 · Shade the energy a spring can store and give it back.
Only the green triangle under the elastic part — that is resilience, \(U_r=\sigma_y^2/2E\). Anything past yield is not returned; it went into permanent deformation.
Q3 · A part must not permanently deform in service. Which landmark is your design limit?
Yield strength \(\sigma_y\), with a safety factor. UTS is irrelevant — by the time you reach it the part is long since ruined.
Q4 · The straight part reaches stress 250 MPa at strain 0.00125. Find \(E\).
\(E=\sigma/\varepsilon = 250/0.00125 = 200{,}000\) MPa \(=200\) GPa. That is steel — a good sanity check that you read the graph correctly.
P3

Two curves compared — the classic exam question

Card 3 · Week 2

Two materials on one set of axes. The question is never "describe them"; it is "which one, and why".

Read it in this order
  1. Stiffer? Steeper initial slope. Here B.
  2. Stronger? Higher peak. Here B.
  3. More ductile? Reaches further right. Here A, by a long way.
  4. Tougher? Bigger area underneath. Here A, despite being weaker.
Q1 · B is stronger. Is B the better choice for a part that might get hit?
No. Impact needs toughness — energy absorbed — which is area under the curve, and A has far more of it. B is strong but brittle: it will reach a high stress and then shatter with no warning. A will bend visibly first, which is also a safety feature.
Q2 · Which one is more likely a ceramic, and which a ductile metal?
B is the ceramic: steep (high \(E\)), high strength, almost no plastic strain, ends abruptly. A is the ductile metal: clear yield, long plastic region, necking.
Q3 · Can a material be strong and tough?
Yes, but there is a tension. Strengthening works by blocking dislocations (card 5 on the core map) and that always costs ductility, which shrinks the area. Good engineering alloys are a compromise, not a maximum of both.
P4

The 0.2% offset construction

Card 3 · Week 2

Real curves bend gradually, so "where it stops being straight" is a judgement call. The offset method makes it a rule.

Read it in this order
  1. Find the straight elastic part and note its slope.
  2. Step along the strain axis to 0.002 (that is 0.2%).
  3. From there, draw a line parallel to the elastic slope.
  4. Where it cuts the curve is the yield point. Read across for \(\sigma_y\).
Q1 · Why 0.2% and not zero?
Because the transition is gradual, so "zero permanent strain" cannot be located on a real plot. 0.2% is a convention: the stress that leaves 0.002 permanent strain behind. It is small enough to be harmless in most designs and large enough to measure reliably.
Q2 · The offset line must be parallel to what, exactly?
The elastic portion — slope \(E\). That is the whole point: it represents unloading from the yield point back to zero stress, which happens along the elastic slope. The strain left over is the 0.002.
P5

Load, then unload — elastic recovery and permanent set

Card 3 · Week 2

Pull past yield to point P, then release. The bar springs back a bit but does not return to its original length.

Read it in this order
  1. Find P — the highest point reached before release.
  2. Draw the unload line down from P, parallel to the elastic slope.
  3. Where it hits the strain axis splits the total strain into two parts.
  4. Left of that point = permanent set. Right of it = elastic recovery.
Q1 · This diagram explains springback in sheet metal forming. How?
When you bend a panel in a press you load it well past yield. On releasing, it recovers the elastic part of the strain and opens up slightly. So you must over-bend by the elastic recovery amount to land on the shape you want.
Q2 · If you now reload the same bar, where is the new yield point?
At P, higher than the original \(\sigma_y\). The bar has been strain hardened by the excursion into the plastic region — this is exactly the cold-work strengthening mechanism from card 5.
P6

Engineering versus true stress–strain

Card 3 · Week 2

Two ways of dividing by area. They agree until necking starts, then they part company completely.

Read it in this order
  1. Note where the two curves separate — that is the onset of necking, at UTS.
  2. Engineering uses \(A_0\), so it drops once the load falls.
  3. True uses the actual shrinking area, so it keeps rising: the material really is still hardening.
Q1 · Which curve do you use for design, and why?
Engineering. Design works with the original dimensions you can measure and specify. True stress matters for metal forming and for the physics of what is happening in the neck.
Q2 · Does the drop in the engineering curve mean the material has been damaged?
No. It is a bookkeeping effect from dividing by an area that no longer exists. The true curve shows the material getting stronger right up to fracture.
P7

Cold work — strength up, ductility down

Card 5 · Weeks 3–4

The tradeoff of card 5, drawn. Every percent of cold work buys strength and spends ductility.

Read it in this order
  1. Two curves go up, one goes down. Name them before anything else.
  2. Notice yield rises faster than tensile strength — the two converge.
  3. Ductility falls steeply. That is the price, and it is not optional.
  4. Pick a percentage on the x axis and read all three off together.
Q1 · Why do yield and tensile strength converge as cold work increases?
Cold work fills the metal with tangled dislocations, so it is already near its hardening limit before you even start the tensile test. There is little plastic capacity left between first yield and fracture, so the two numbers close up. That is the same reason ductility collapses.
Q2 · A wire is drawn to 50% cold work and now cracks when bent. What is the fix, and what does it cost?
Anneal it — see P8. That recrystallises the grains and restores ductility. The cost is that you give the strength back, so you may need to re-draw a smaller amount, or anneal partway and finish with a light final draw.
Q3 · Which of the four strengthening mechanisms is this diagram showing?
Strain hardening. Dislocations pile up and tangle, obstructing each other. Same underlying idea as the other three — obstruct dislocation motion — same price in ductility.
P8

Annealing — the three regions

Card 5 · Week 3

Heat a cold-worked metal and it undoes itself in three stages. This diagram is almost always asked as "name the regions".

Read it in this order
  1. Three vertical bands. Name them left to right: recovery, recrystallisation, grain growth.
  2. Find the region where strength drops sharply — that is recrystallisation, where new grains replace the deformed ones.
  3. Grain size stays flat until the new grains have formed, then climbs in grain growth.
  4. Link grain size back to Hall–Petch: bigger grains, weaker metal. That is why strength keeps sagging on the right.
Q1 · You want to relieve internal stresses without losing strength. Which region do you anneal in?
Recovery — the lowest temperature band. Dislocations rearrange and residual stress drops, but the grain structure is untouched so strength is largely retained.
Q2 · Why does strength keep falling in the grain growth region even though recrystallisation is finished?
Hall–Petch, \(\sigma_y=\sigma_0+k_yd^{-1/2}\). Growing grains means fewer grain boundaries per unit volume, fewer obstacles to dislocations, lower yield strength. Over-annealing is a real defect, not just wasted energy.
Q3 · Which region gives the biggest ductility gain per degree of temperature?
Recrystallisation — the steep middle band. That is where the deformed, dislocation-packed grains are replaced by fresh strain-free ones.
P9

The Hall–Petch plot

Card 4 · Week 3

Yield strength against \(d^{-1/2}\). Plotted this way it is a straight line, which is the whole reason the axis looks odd.

Read it in this order
  1. Check the x axis carefully — it is \(d^{-1/2}\), not \(d\). Moving right means smaller grains.
  2. The intercept is \(\sigma_0\): the strength you would have with essentially no grain boundaries.
  3. The slope is \(k_y\), a material constant.
  4. Line goes up to the right, so smaller grains give higher yield.
Trap: reading this as "strength increases with grain size". It does the opposite. The reversed axis is deliberate and it catches people every year.
Q1 · Grain size drops from 100 µm to 25 µm. What happens to the Hall–Petch term?
\(d\) is four times smaller, so \(d^{-1/2}\) doubles. The grain-boundary contribution \(k_yd^{-1/2}\) doubles. Note that total yield does not double — \(\sigma_0\) is unchanged.
Q2 · Two alloys give parallel Hall–Petch lines at different heights. What differs?
Parallel means the same \(k_y\), so grain boundaries are equally effective in both. The different heights mean different \(\sigma_0\) — a different base lattice strength, probably from alloying or solid solution content.

C · Failure diagrams

Three pictures for the three ways parts die: a flaw runs, a load repeats, or the thing sits hot for years.

P10

A crack in a plate, and what it does to allowable stress

Card 7 · Week 4

Left, the physical picture: the lines of stress have to squeeze around the crack, so they crowd together at its tip and the stress there is far higher than the average. Right, the consequence: the stress you are allowed to apply falls off as a square root of the crack size, not in a straight line. The symbol \(K_{IC}\) in the equation is fracture toughness — the material's own limit on how much crowded-up stress a crack tip can take before it runs.

Read it in this order
  1. Identify \(a\). For a centre crack the full length is \(2a\), so \(a\) is the half-length. For an edge crack \(a\) is the full depth.
  2. Note the curve is steepest at small \(a\) — the first bit of crack growth hurts most.
  3. Apply the square-root rule: multiply \(a\) by \(n\) and allowable stress divides by \(\sqrt{n}\).
  4. Say the design consequence: inspection interval, or lower the working load.
Trap: using the full visible crack length as \(a\) for a centre crack. The picture labels \(2a\) for a reason. Half it.
Q1 · \(K_{IC}=40\) MPa√m, \(Y=1.1\), crack \(a=2\) mm. Find the allowable stress.
\(\sigma=\dfrac{40}{1.1\sqrt{\pi(0.002)}}=\dfrac{40}{1.1(0.0793)}\approx 459\) MPa. Always convert mm to m before the square root.
Q2 · That crack grows to 8 mm in service. New allowable stress, without recalculating?
Four times the crack, so divide by \(\sqrt4=2\): about 230 MPa. If the part is working above that, it is now unsafe even though nothing about the material changed.
Q3 · Why does the curve flatten at large \(a\)?
Because \(1/\sqrt a\) changes slowly once \(a\) is already big. Practically, the damage is mostly done early: going from a 0.1 mm to a 0.4 mm flaw costs you the same factor of two as going from 2 mm to 8 mm.
P11

The S–N curve — with and without an endurance limit

Card 10 · Week 4

Stress on the vertical axis against the number of load cycles the part survives on the horizontal. Those two quantities are labelled S and N, which is the entire reason it is called an S–N curve. The single most important thing to look at is whether the line eventually flattens out.

Read it in this order
  1. Check the x axis is logarithmic. Each gridline is ten times the last, not one more.
  2. Does the curve flatten? If yes, that plateau is the endurance limit \(S_e\) — typical of steels.
  3. If it never flattens (aluminium, most non-ferrous), there is no safe stress; you quote fatigue strength at a stated life such as \(10^7\) cycles.
  4. To answer "how long will it last", go up from the stress, across to the curve, down to N.
Q1 · A steel part runs below \(S_e\). How many cycles will it survive?
Effectively unlimited, as far as fatigue is concerned. That is what an endurance limit means and it is why steel is attractive for parts that cycle billions of times. Other failure modes such as corrosion still apply.
Q2 · Why can you not design an aluminium component "below the fatigue limit"?
Because aluminium does not have one. The curve keeps descending, so every stress level has a finite life. You design for a required number of cycles and then retire or inspect the part.
Q3 · Two identical steel parts, same peak stress, but one has a sharp internal corner. Which fails first?
The notched one, by a wide margin. Fatigue cracks start at surface stress concentrations. Its effective S–N curve is shifted down, so at the same applied stress it reaches failure far sooner. Rounding the corner is usually the cheapest fix available.
Q4 · Name three fixes that move a part to the safe side of this curve.
Lower the stress amplitude (thicker section), improve surface finish (polishing removes initiation sites), and induce compressive residual surface stress (shot peening) so cracks struggle to open. Rounding notches and changing to a material with a higher \(S_e\) also count.
P12

The creep curve — three stages

Card 10 · Week 5

How much a part has stretched, plotted against time, under a load that never changes, at high temperature. This is not a stress–strain curve even though it looks a bit like one, so check the horizontal axis before you say anything about it.

Read it in this order
  1. Confirm the x axis is time, not strain. This is the diagram people misidentify most often.
  2. Find the straight middle section. Its slope is the steady-state creep rate.
  3. Name the three stages: primary slows down, secondary is constant, tertiary runs away to rupture.
  4. Compare curves: raising temperature or stress steepens the middle and brings rupture forward.
Q1 · Why does design use the secondary stage rather than the other two?
Because it occupies most of the service life and it is linear, so it is predictable. Total creep strain over the design life is roughly the steady-state rate multiplied by time. Tertiary means the part is already failing.
Q2 · Two turbine blades, same material, one runs 50 °C hotter. Read the difference off the diagram.
The hotter one has a steeper secondary slope and reaches rupture much sooner — that is the dashed curve. Creep is diffusion-driven, so the temperature effect is exponential, not proportional.
Q3 · At what temperature should you start worrying about creep?
Roughly above \(0.4\,T_m\) in kelvin. That is why lead creeps at room temperature and steel does not, and why creep dominates turbine and boiler design but is irrelevant for a bicycle frame.

D · Thermal

One picture that decides whether a temperature change is harmless or dangerous.

P13

Free versus constrained expansion

Card 10 · Week 5

Same bar, same temperature rise, completely different outcome. The only difference is whether the ends can move.

Read it in this order
  1. Look for supports. Are both ends fixed, or can one slide?
  2. Free → compute strain with \(\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\) and stop. There is no significant stress.
  3. Constrained → the strain cannot happen, so it becomes stress of order \(E\alpha\Delta T\).
  4. Heating a constrained bar puts it in compression; cooling puts it in tension.
Q1 · \(L_0=2\) m, \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\)/°C, \(\Delta T=100\) °C, free ends. Find \(\Delta L\).
\(\Delta L=(12\times10^{-6})(2)(100)=2.4\times10^{-3}\) m \(=2.4\) mm. Small, but enough to buckle a rail or crack a tiled floor if there is nowhere to put it.
Q2 · Same bar, now fully constrained, \(E=200\) GPa. Roughly what stress?
\(\sigma\approx E\alpha\Delta T=(200\times10^{3})(12\times10^{-6})(100)=240\) MPa in compression. That is a serious fraction of the yield strength of structural steel, from a 100 °C change alone. Note the length cancelled out — a short bar is just as bad as a long one.
Q3 · Two bonded materials with different \(\alpha\) are heated. What happens?
They want to expand by different amounts but are joined, so each constrains the other. Stress builds at the interface and the assembly bows. This thermal mismatch is why bimetallic strips work and why solder joints and coatings fail in thermal cycling.

E · Phase diagrams

The two you must be able to read. These carry heavy calculation marks and the reading procedure is completely mechanical, which makes them a reliable place to score.

P14

The simplest phase diagram — tie-line and lever rule

Card 6 · Week 6

Two metals that dissolve into each other completely, in any proportion. The technical name for this type is isomorphous, which just means "one form throughout". Three words to know before reading it: the liquidus is the upper line, above which everything is liquid; the solidus is the lower line, below which everything is solid; and the lens-shaped gap between them is where you have a mush of both at once.

Read it in this order
  1. Mark \(C_0\) on the x axis and \(T\) on the y axis. Find the point where they meet.
  2. Which region is the point in? Above liquidus = all liquid. Below solidus = all solid. In between = two phases, keep going.
  3. Draw the tie-line: horizontal, at that temperature, from one boundary to the other.
  4. Read the compositions at the two ends. Those are \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_L\), not \(C_0\).
  5. Apply the lever rule, using the distance to the opposite end for each phase.
Trap: \(W_\alpha\) uses \(C_L-C_0\), the far side. Sanity check every time: if \(C_0\) sits close to \(C_\alpha\), you should be getting mostly \(\alpha\).
Q1 · Read off the fraction of solid at \(T_1\) from the diagram.
\(W_\alpha=\dfrac{C_L-C_0}{C_L-C_\alpha}=\dfrac{64-52}{64-40}=\dfrac{12}{24}=0.50\). Half solid, half liquid — which matches the picture, since \(C_0\) sits exactly midway along the tie-line.
Q2 · \(C_\alpha=10\), \(C_L=70\), \(C_0=40\) wt%. Both fractions?
\(W_\alpha=(70-40)/60=0.5\) and \(W_L=(40-10)/60=0.5\). They must sum to 1 — always check that as your last step.
Q3 · The point sits above the liquidus. What is the answer?
100% liquid of composition \(C_0\). No tie-line, no lever rule. Recognising a single-phase region and stopping is worth marks; grinding through the lever rule there loses them.
Q4 · You cool an alloy slowly from all-liquid to all-solid. What happens to the composition of the first solid formed?
The first solid forms at the solidus and is richer in the higher-melting component than \(C_0\). As cooling continues, both the solid and liquid compositions slide along their boundary curves until, at the solidus, the solid finally reaches \(C_0\) — assuming equilibrium and slow cooling.
P15

Eutectic diagram — naming the regions

Card 6 · Week 6

Two metals that only partly dissolve in each other, so at low temperature you end up with a mixture of two different solids rather than one. That gives more regions on the diagram and one special point, but the tie-line and lever rule work exactly as before inside any two-phase region. A eutectic is the particular mixture that melts at the lowest temperature of any mixture in the system.

Read it in this order
  1. Find the lowest point of the liquid region — that is the eutectic point, at \(C_E\) and \(T_E\).
  2. The horizontal line through it is the eutectic isotherm. Everything below it is fully solid.
  3. Name the six regions: L, α, β, α+L, β+L, α+β.
  4. The curved lines near the left and right edges are called solvus lines. They show how much of one metal will stay dissolved in the other, and they curve inwards because a cold solid can hold less in solution than a hot one.
  5. Inside any two-phase region, the tie-line and lever rule work exactly as in P14.
Q1 · What is special about an alloy at exactly \(C_E\)?
It melts and freezes at a single temperature \(T_E\), like a pure metal, with no mushy two-phase range. On cooling it transforms straight from liquid to a fine mixture of α and β. It also has the lowest melting point of any composition in the system — which is exactly why solders sit near eutectic.
Q2 · Why do the solvus lines curve inwards as temperature falls?
Solubility decreases with temperature. A hot α phase can dissolve more B than a cold one. As the alloy cools past the solvus, the excess B comes out as β precipitates — which is the basis of precipitation strengthening from card 5.
Q3 · A point sits in the α+β region. How do you get the phase fractions?
Identically to P14: horizontal tie-line at that temperature, read \(C_\alpha\) off the solvus on the left and \(C_\beta\) off the solvus on the right, then lever rule. The extra regions do not change the method at all.

F · Material classes

Five diagrams, one for each way a non-metal misbehaves.

P16

Polymer modulus against temperature

Card 9a · Week 7

The most important polymer diagram. Stiffness runs up the side on a log scale, temperature across the bottom. It explains why the same plastic clip is rigid outdoors in winter and floppy in a hot car. Two temperatures are marked: \(T_g\) is the glass transition temperature, where the plastic changes from hard and glassy to soft and rubbery while still staying solid, and \(T_m\) is the higher melting temperature where it finally flows.

Read it in this order
  1. Find \(T_g\) — the big cliff. Stiffness falls by around three orders of magnitude across it.
  2. Mark the service temperature on the axis. Left of \(T_g\) = glassy and brittle. Right = rubbery.
  3. Find \(T_m\) — where a thermoplastic finally flows and can be moulded.
  4. Check for crosslinking: a crosslinked polymer keeps a rubbery plateau instead of flowing, so it cannot be re-melted.
Q1 · Polymer A has \(T_g=80\) °C, polymer B has \(T_g=-20\) °C. Both used at 25 °C. Compare them.
A is below its \(T_g\) so it is glassy: stiff and prone to brittle fracture on impact. B is above its \(T_g\) so it is rubbery: low modulus, large recoverable strain, good impact tolerance. Same service temperature, opposite behaviour, and the diagram is the whole explanation.
Q2 · Why does a heavily crosslinked polymer not have a flow region?
Its chains are chemically tied to each other, so they cannot slide past one another no matter how hot it gets. It stays on the rubbery plateau and eventually degrades rather than melting. That is a thermoset, and it is why you cannot recycle it by melting.
Q3 · A plastic clip works fine indoors but snaps in a cold warehouse. Explain with this diagram.
The warehouse temperature has dropped below \(T_g\). The clip has moved from the rubbery region into the glassy region, so modulus jumped and ductility collapsed. Nothing about the material changed — only where it sits on this axis.
P17

Three polymer stress–strain shapes

Card 9a · Week 7

Polymers do not give one curve shape. Recognising which of the three you are looking at names the material class straight away.

Read it in this order
  1. How far right does it go? A few percent = brittle. Hundreds of percent = elastomer.
  2. Is there a flat plateau after yield? That is cold drawing, where chains align along the pull direction.
  3. Is there a sharp upturn at the end? That is an elastomer running out of chain slack.
  4. Cross-check with P16 — the shape follows directly from where \(T\) sits relative to \(T_g\).
Q1 · Why does the ductile curve dip and then run flat instead of climbing?
A neck forms, and then instead of breaking there, the neck spreads along the specimen at roughly constant load. Chains are aligning with the pull direction as the neck travels. Once drawing is complete the aligned chains carry load directly and the stress climbs again.
Q2 · The elastomer curve turns up steeply at the end. Why?
The coiled chains have straightened out. Up to that point you are uncoiling, which is easy. Past it you are stretching covalent bonds themselves, which is very hard, so the modulus rockets.
Q3 · One polymer is tested at 20 °C and 80 °C and gives two different shapes from this set. Explain.
The test has crossed \(T_g\). Below it the polymer gives the brittle curve; above it, the ductile or elastomeric one. This is why a polymer datasheet without a test temperature is nearly meaningless.
P18

Ceramic strength scatter

Card 9b · Week 12

Test twenty identical metal specimens and you get twenty similar numbers. Do it with a ceramic and you get a spread. That spread is the diagram.

Read it in this order
  1. Compare the widths of the two clusters, not their positions.
  2. Ask what varies. For the ceramic it is the largest flaw in each specimen, via \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\).
  3. Note where the design value must sit: near the bottom of the ceramic spread, not the average.
Q1 · Two batches of identical composition test differently. Has someone made an error?
No — this is expected. Ceramic strength is flaw-controlled, so it is set by the largest defect in each specimen rather than by the chemistry. Different surface finish, grinding damage or porosity gives a different flaw population and therefore a different measured strength.
Q2 · Give two practical ways to move this whole distribution right and make it narrower.
Improve surface finish so machining does not leave chips and scratches, and put the surface into compression (for example by tempering or a compressive coating) so cracks cannot open. Better powder processing to reduce porosity, and generous fillet radii to avoid stress concentrations, also help.
Q3 · Why is the same ceramic far stronger in compression?
Compression closes cracks rather than opening them, so the flaw population is largely neutralised. This is why ceramics and concrete are used as columns and arches but need reinforcement whenever tension appears.
P19

Composite stiffness against fibre fraction

Card 9c · Week 11

Two lines running between the same two endpoints. The gap between them is the whole reason fibre direction matters. \(V_f\) on the horizontal axis is the fibre volume fraction — what proportion of the material is fibre rather than the softer matrix holding it together.

Read it in this order
  1. Both lines start at the matrix stiffness and end at the fibre stiffness, which makes sense: a composite with no fibres is just matrix, and one with no matrix is just fibre.
  2. The straight upper line is the stiffness along the fibres. Pulled that way, the fibres and the matrix stretch by the same amount, so the result is a plain weighted average and the stiff fibres dominate it.
  3. The curved lower line is the stiffness across the fibres. Pulled that way the load has to pass through the matrix, and whenever things are lined up one behind the other the softest one governs. So this line stays low until there is very little matrix left.
  4. The vertical distance between the two lines tells you how differently the material behaves in the two directions. A big gap means the direction you load it in matters enormously.
Q1 · \(E_f=230\) GPa, \(E_m=3\) GPa, \(V_f=0.55\). Compute both.
\(E_L=230(0.55)+3(0.45)=127.85\) GPa. \(1/E_T=0.55/230+0.45/3=0.00239+0.15=0.1524\), so \(E_T\approx6.6\) GPa. Nineteen times stiffer along the fibres than across them — that is the number that surprises people.
Q2 · Why does the matrix barely matter for \(E_L\) but dominate \(E_T\)?
Loaded along the fibres, both phases stretch by the same amount and the stiff fibres carry almost all the load, so the matrix contribution is negligible. Loaded across, the load passes through the matrix in series, and a series arrangement is always dominated by the most compliant member.
Q3 · A part is designed using \(E_L\) but fails in service. What is the likely mistake?
Real loads are rarely purely along one axis. Any off-axis or transverse component sees \(E_T\), which may be twenty times lower, so deflections and stresses are far worse than predicted. The fix is a layup with fibres in several orientations rather than all in one.
P20

Fibre stress along the length — critical length

Card 9c · Week 11

Stress builds up along a fibre from its ends inwards, dragged in through the glue-like grip of the surrounding matrix. If the fibre is too short, it runs out of length before it ever reaches full load. The dashed line \(\sigma_f^*\) is the fibre's own strength, and \(l_c\) is the critical length — the shortest a fibre can be and still do its job properly.

Read it in this order
  1. The dashed line is \(\sigma_f^*\), the fibre's own strength. Does the curve reach it?
  2. Stress is always zero at the two ends of the fibre and highest in the middle, because the load is dragged in gradually through the matrix's grip along the sides.
  3. Compare the peak with the dashed line to decide whether the fibre is doing its job.
  4. Compute \(l_c=\dfrac{\sigma_f^*d}{2\tau_c}\) and compare with the actual fibre length.
Q1 · \(\sigma_f^*=2.5\) GPa, \(d=12\) µm, \(\tau_c=35\) MPa. Find \(l_c\).
\(l_c=\dfrac{(2.5\times10^9)(12\times10^{-6})}{2(35\times10^6)}=\dfrac{30000}{70\times10^6}=4.29\times10^{-4}\) m \(=0.43\) mm. Fibres much shorter than that are not being used properly.
Q2 · Chopped fibres in a moulding are 0.1 mm long. Comment.
Well below the 0.43 mm critical length, so they sit in the left-hand panel. They will pull out of the matrix rather than break, the composite gets only a fraction of the possible reinforcement, and toughness comes mainly from pull-out friction rather than fibre strength.
Q3 · How does a stronger fibre–matrix bond change \(l_c\)?
A better bond means higher \(\tau_c\), which sits in the denominator, so \(l_c\) drops. Shorter fibres then become effective. This is why fibre surface treatments and sizing agents matter as much as the fibre itself.

P21

Resistivity against temperature — metal versus semiconductor

Card 8a · Week 9

Two materials, opposite slopes. The slope alone tells you which class you are dealing with.

Read it in this order
  1. Which way does the line go? Up with temperature = metal. Down = semiconductor.
  2. Explain via \(\sigma=nq\mu\): in a metal \(n\) is fixed and \(\mu\) falls, so \(\rho\) rises. In a semiconductor \(n\) rises sharply, which overwhelms any loss in \(\mu\).
  3. Convert to a component with \(R=\rho L/A\) if the question asks for ohms.
Q1 · Doping doubles \(n\) but conductivity barely changes. Explain.
The dopant atoms are also scattering sites, so mobility \(\mu\) falls roughly as much as \(n\) rises. Since \(\sigma=nq\mu\) is a product, the two effects cancel. More carriers is not automatically more conductivity.
Q2 · \(\rho=2\times10^{-6}\) Ω·m, \(L=0.4\) m, \(A=2\times10^{-6}\) m². Find \(R\).
\(R=\rho L/A=(2\times10^{-6})(0.4)/(2\times10^{-6})=0.4\) Ω. Note that \(\rho\) alone tells you nothing about the part until you supply the geometry.
Q3 · Resistivity drops from \(5\times10^{-6}\) to \(4\times10^{-6}\) Ω·m. By what fraction does conductivity change?
\(\sigma=1/\rho\), so it goes from \(2.0\times10^5\) to \(2.5\times10^5\) S/m — a 25% increase. A 20% fall in resistivity is not a 20% rise in conductivity; the reciprocal matters.
P22

The B–H hysteresis loop — soft versus hard

Card 8b · Week 10

What happens as you drive a magnetic field back and forth through a material. \(H\) across the bottom is the field you apply, and \(B\) up the side is the magnetism you get out. You read this loop by its shape, and the shape names the application immediately. Two words appear on it: remanence (\(B_r\)) is the magnetism left behind when you switch the field off, and coercivity (\(H_c\)) is how hard you have to push the other way to wipe that out.

Read it in this order
  1. Axes: \(H\) is the field you apply, \(B\) is the response you get.
  2. Where the loop crosses the vertical axis is \(B_r\), the remanence — what is left when you switch the field off.
  3. Where it crosses the horizontal axis is \(H_c\), the coercivity — how hard you must push backwards to wipe it out.
  4. The enclosed area is energy lost as heat per cycle, per unit volume.
  5. Thin loop = soft = AC duty. Fat loop = hard = permanent magnet. They are not interchangeable.
Q1 · Why is loop area a thermal design concern, not just an efficiency one?
Every cycle dumps that energy into the core as heat. At mains frequency that is 50 cycles a second; at high switching frequencies it is thousands. The heat must be removed or the core temperature rises, which degrades the magnetic properties further and can damage the insulation.
Q2 · A material has high \(B_r\) but low \(H_c\). Is it a good permanent magnet?
No. It holds a strong field only until something disturbs it. Low coercivity means a modest opposing field — or heat, or a knock — demagnetises it. A permanent magnet needs both high remanence and high coercivity, which is what makes the loop fat rather than merely tall.
Q3 · Two soft materials have the same saturation but one has a 35% smaller loop area. Which for a transformer, and why?
The smaller-area one. Same flux capability, 35% less hysteresis loss per cycle, so less heat, better efficiency and a smaller cooling requirement. At high frequency the advantage grows because losses scale with the number of cycles.
Q4 · Why do AC devices avoid hard magnetic materials entirely?
Because AC reverses the field continuously, so you traverse the whole loop many times a second. A fat loop means enormous energy loss every traverse — the core would overheat almost immediately and the device would be hopelessly inefficient.

Mixed practice — name the diagram

In the exam the diagram arrives without a label. Classifying it correctly is the first mark and it unlocks all the others. Read each description and name the picture before opening.

1 · Axes are strain against time. Three regions, the middle one straight.
Creep curve (P12). The straight middle is secondary creep and its slope is the design number. Give the answer in terms of temperature and \(0.4\,T_m\).
2 · A curve that rises, peaks, then falls, with the fall labelled.
Engineering stress–strain (P2). The fall is necking, and it happens because stress is computed on the original area.
3 · Log x axis running from 10³ to 10⁷, curve descending then flat.
S–N fatigue curve for a ferrous alloy (P11). The flat part is the endurance limit.
4 · A closed loop, symmetric about the origin, with area shaded.
B–H hysteresis loop (P22). Area is loss per cycle; thin is soft, fat is hard.
5 · Two curves between the same two endpoints, one straight and one sagging below it.
Composite modulus against fibre fraction (P19). Straight is longitudinal, sagging is transverse.
6 · Temperature on y, composition on x, a horizontal line with a point at its lowest.
Eutectic phase diagram (P15). The point is the eutectic; the horizontal line is the eutectic isotherm.
7 · Three shaded vertical bands, one curve falling and one rising across them.
Annealing (P8): recovery, recrystallisation, grain growth. Strength falls, ductility rises.
8 · A curve with a plateau, a cliff, another plateau, then a final drop. Y axis is logarithmic.
Polymer modulus against temperature (P16). The cliff is \(T_g\), the final drop is \(T_m\).
9 · A straight line whose x axis is written as \(d^{-1/2}\).
Hall–Petch (P9). Intercept \(\sigma_0\), slope \(k_y\), and remember that moving right means smaller grains.
10 · A decaying curve where quadrupling x halves y.
Allowable stress against crack size (P10). The signature of the \(1/\sqrt a\) relation, and the same shape governs ceramics.
