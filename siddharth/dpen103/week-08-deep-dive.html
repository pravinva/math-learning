What this week is really asking
Which material, and why not the alternatives?

Materials selection is not about maximising one property. It is about satisfying the non-negotiable constraints first, then optimising the trade-offs that remain. An answer that names a good material but cannot say why the others were rejected is an incomplete answer.

Working through the week

This section develops the week's ideas in order — from first principles through to how they are used in engineering practice.

The materials selection process

Engineering design specifies what a component must do before naming a material. The selection process moves from function to measurable requirements, then to candidate materials, then to a justified choice.

First state the function and all constraints — maximum mass, maximum cost, operating temperature, environment, manufacturing route. Next identify failure modes: yielding, fracture, fatigue, creep, corrosion, excessive deflection, wear. Each failure mode maps to a property target with a numerical value or limit.

Materials that fail any constraint are eliminated. Those that pass all constraints form a shortlist, ranked on an objective — usually minimising mass or cost. The final answer names the chosen material, states which alternatives were considered, identifies the constraint each rejected candidate failed, acknowledges the trade-off accepted, and offers a backup if the main risk materialises.

Performance indices and property charts

Many design problems reduce to maximising a ratio of properties. A tie rod limited by stiffness and weight is optimised on specific stiffness \(E/\rho\). A beam in bending benefits from \(E^{1/2}/\rho\) or \(E^{1/3}/\rho\) depending on whether the section can be redesigned. A strength-limited tie uses \(\sigma_y/\rho\).

Ashby charts plot properties on logarithmic axes so entire material families appear as clusters. A light, stiff panel seeks the upper-left region of an \(E\) versus \(\rho\) chart. Steel and aluminium have similar specific stiffness even though steel's absolute modulus is three times higher, because steel is also three times denser.

Charts screen families efficiently; calculations on shortlisted candidates then confirm that all constraints are met. A chart shows what is possible in principle; manufacturing, joining, environment and cost determine what is possible in practice.

Lifecycle cost and environmental screening

Purchase price is often a small fraction of lifecycle cost. A coated carbon steel component may cost little to buy but require repeated recoating in a corrosive environment, with each maintenance event carrying labour and downtime costs. Stainless steel with a higher initial price may be cheaper over twenty years if maintenance is eliminated.

Environmental screening asks whether a candidate survives the service medium — marine salt, acids, UV exposure, elevated temperature. Galvanic compatibility between joined materials matters: dissimilar metals in electrical contact in an electrolyte can accelerate corrosion of the more anodic member.

Selection is complete only when function, properties, process, cost and environment have all been addressed in the justification.

1 The six-step method

Follow it in order every time. The order is what stops you falling in love with a material before knowing what the part needs.

The sequence
  • 1 — Function and constraints. What must it do, and what is non-negotiable. Numbers wherever possible.
  • 2 — Failure modes. List every way it could fail: yield, fatigue, fracture, creep, corrosion, wear, deflection.
  • 3 — Property targets. Turn each constraint into a property with a value attached.
  • 4 — Shortlist. Screen material classes against those targets. Three or four candidates is plenty.
  • 5 — Process and cost. Can it actually be made in this shape, at this volume, for this money?
  • 6 — Decide and justify. Name the choice, and give the reason each rejected candidate was rejected.

Step 2 is the one most often skipped and the one that most often decides the answer. A part that will never yield can still fail by fatigue, and if fatigue was never on your list you will confidently recommend the wrong material.

Say it cold: "Function, failure modes, property targets, shortlist, process and cost, then decide and justify the rejects."

2 Specific properties — why aluminium competes with steel

When weight matters, the raw property is the wrong number. What counts is the property per unit weight, and that reorders the whole ranking.

Material\(E\) (GPa)\(\rho\) (g/cm³)\(E/\rho\)
Steel2107.8~27
Aluminium702.7~26
CFRP (along fibres)1501.6~94

Steel is three times stiffer than aluminium in absolute terms, yet on a per-weight basis they are almost identical. This is why aircraft are not simply made of the stiffest available metal, and why "steel is stiffer" is a true statement that answers the wrong question.

Which index applies depends on how the part is loaded. A beam in bending rewards \(E^{1/2}/\rho\) rather than \(E/\rho\), because you can also make the section deeper. The general skill is deriving the index from the governing equation instead of memorising a list.

Say it cold: "Steel and aluminium have almost the same stiffness per unit weight, so the choice between them is decided by other constraints."

4 Justifying the rejections

The single highest-value habit in this subject. Naming a good material shows recognition; explaining the rejections shows you can actually design.

For each candidate you discard, give the specific constraint it failed. Not "steel is heavy" but "steel meets the stiffness target but exceeds the mass budget, and would need coating for the salt-air requirement, adding a maintenance obligation".

What a complete answer contains
  • The choice, named specifically — an alloy family, not just "metal".
  • The constraint each rejected candidate failed.
  • The trade-off you accepted, since every choice has one.
  • A backup option if the primary risk materialises.

A useful test before you stop writing: if you cannot say why a candidate was rejected, you have not finished selecting — you have merely picked.

Feeds: the highest-mark parts of the exam, and it costs nothing but structure.
Say it cold: "Name the choice, give the constraint each rejected candidate failed, state the trade-off accepted, and offer a backup."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — a lightweight support member

Given: Needs low mass, adequate stiffness, corrosion tolerance and moderate cost. Medium production volume.
  1. Function and constraints: carry the design load without permanent set; deflection within limits; survive a damp environment; keep mass down; moderate unit cost at medium volume.
  2. Failure modes: yielding, excessive deflection, corrosion, and fatigue if the load cycles.
  3. Property targets: \(\sigma_y\) with a safety margin above working stress; high \(E/\rho\); a stable surface or a coatable one.
  4. Shortlist: carbon steel, aluminium alloy, polymer-matrix composite.
  5. Evaluate: steel gives high \(E\) but high density and needs coating in damp air. Aluminium has one third the stiffness but one third the density, so specific stiffness is comparable, and it resists corrosion naturally. Composite offers the best specific stiffness by far but brings joining complexity, higher unit cost and tooling that only pays back at volume.
  6. Decide: aluminium alloy. It meets stiffness through section design, needs no coating, and suits medium volume.
  7. Reject with reasons: steel is rejected on mass plus the coating maintenance obligation, not on strength. Composite is rejected on cost and joining complexity at this volume, not on performance — and it would be the answer if volume rose or mass became critical.
What it means: Notice that the rejections carry as much content as the choice. Both rejected candidates were technically capable; they failed on constraints other than raw strength.

Worked example 2 — when the process decides

A thin-walled part with complex internal geometry, needed in high volume at low cost.

  1. The geometry rules out anything that cannot be formed in one shot — machining it would be uneconomic at volume.
  2. That points at injection moulding or die casting, which immediately restricts the material list to mouldable polymers and castable alloys.
  3. A material with excellent properties that cannot be economically shaped this way is eliminated regardless of how well it scores on the property targets.
  4. Only then do you compare the survivors on properties.
What it means: Sometimes the manufacturing route selects the material rather than the other way round. Recognising when process dominates is itself an examinable judgement.

Worked example 3 — why the dearer material can be cheaper

A stainless component costs three times the coated carbon steel equivalent. Justify choosing it.

  1. Purchase price is only part of the cost. Add installation, maintenance, downtime during replacement, and the number of replacements over the design life.
  2. The coated steel needs periodic recoating and will eventually be replaced, and each intervention may take the plant offline.
  3. If the stainless part lasts the full design life with no intervention, its higher purchase price can be repaid several times over.
  4. The argument turns on access cost and downtime value — for an easily reached, non-critical bracket it may well not hold.
What it means: Lifecycle cost, not purchase price. The exam version of this question always hinges on maintenance access or the cost of downtime.

Where marks get lost

  • Naming a material before stating property targets. Without targets there is nothing to justify the choice against.
  • Skipping failure modes. The mode determines the property. Miss fatigue and you will confidently screen on the wrong number.
  • Optimising one property. Selection satisfies constraints first, then optimises. Maximising strength alone is not selection.
  • Comparing absolute properties when weight matters. Use \(E/\rho\) or \(\sigma_y/\rho\). Steel looks unbeatable on \(E\) and merely average on \(E/\rho\).
  • Leaving out the rejections. The reasoning about what you did not choose is where the marks concentrate.

Exam practice — 20 questions

Eight multiple choice, four true/false, and eight short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

A design brief lists four requirements for a bracket. Which one is an objective rather than a constraint?

  1. It must fit within a 40 mm envelope.
  2. It must not corrode in salt-laden air.
  3. Its mass should be as low as possible.
  4. It must carry the design load without permanent set.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. A constraint is something that must be satisfied or the design fails outright — it is non-negotiable and answers yes or no. An objective is something to minimise or maximise once the constraints are met, and usually it is mass or cost. A, B and D each set a pass/fail threshold, so they are constraints. "As low as possible" has no threshold to fail, so it is an objective. Design meaning: the order matters. Screen candidates against the constraints first to get a shortlist, then rank the survivors on the objective. Optimising the objective first tends to produce a light material that fails a requirement you had not yet checked.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

A beam must be as light as possible while meeting a stiffness requirement in bending. Which performance index should be maximised?

  1. \(E\) alone
  2. \(E/\rho\)
  3. \(E^{1/2}/\rho\)
  4. \(\sigma_y/\rho\)
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. A performance index is the combination of properties that captures what actually matters for a particular loading case. For a beam in bending you are free to make the section deeper as well as choosing the material, and that extra freedom changes the index from \(E/\rho\) — which applies to a tie loaded in simple tension — to \(E^{1/2}/\rho\). A is wrong because \(E\) alone ignores the mass objective entirely, which is what makes steel look unbeatable when it is not. D is a strength index, appropriate when the limiting failure mode is yielding rather than deflection. Design meaning: the transferable skill is deriving the index from the governing design equation for the actual loading, rather than memorising a list — the same material can win on one index and lose on another.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A tie must carry a given load with minimum mass, so the relevant measure is specific strength \(\sigma_y/\rho\). Which candidate has the highest value?

  1. Steel: \(\sigma_y=350\) MPa, \(\rho=7.8\) g/cm³
  2. Aluminium alloy: \(\sigma_y=280\) MPa, \(\rho=2.7\) g/cm³
  3. Titanium alloy: \(\sigma_y=830\) MPa, \(\rho=4.5\) g/cm³
  4. Magnesium alloy: \(\sigma_y=200\) MPa, \(\rho=1.8\) g/cm³
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Divide strength by density in each case, keeping the units consistent as MPa per (g/cm³): steel \(350/7.8=44.9\); aluminium \(280/2.7=103.7\); titanium \(830/4.5=184.4\); magnesium \(200/1.8=111.1\). Titanium wins by a clear margin, and steel — the strongest-sounding option to many people and in fact the second weakest here — comes last once weight is accounted for. Sanity check: the ranking on specific strength is completely different from the ranking on \(\sigma_y\) alone, which is the whole point of the index. Design meaning: this is only the screening step. Titanium's cost, its difficulty to machine and weld, and its availability may still eliminate it, so the index identifies the technical front-runner rather than the final answer.
Q4 · Multiple choiceOne step

A pressure vessel wall operates well below yield but inspection finds a small pre-existing flaw. Which property most directly governs whether that flaw causes sudden fracture?

  1. Yield strength, \(\sigma_y\)
  2. Young's modulus, \(E\)
  3. Plane-strain fracture toughness, \(K_{IC}\)
  4. Coefficient of thermal expansion, \(\alpha\)
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Naming the failure mode first tells you which property to screen on. Here the mode is fracture from an existing flaw, and the governing property is \(K_{IC}\), the plane-strain fracture toughness — the material's resistance to a crack propagating. \(\sigma_y\) governs yielding, which the question explicitly rules out by stating the wall operates well below yield; \(E\) governs deflection; \(\alpha\) governs thermal expansion. Screening this vessel on yield strength alone could select a high-strength alloy with low toughness, which is the worst combination for a flawed component. Design meaning: in this failure mode the flaw size matters as much as the material, so a toughness requirement and an inspection interval are two halves of the same answer.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: In materials selection, all constraints must be satisfied before the objective is optimised.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Selection is not the maximisation of one property. Constraints are pass/fail requirements, so a candidate that fails any one of them is out regardless of how well it scores elsewhere — the lightest material in the world is useless if it cannot carry the load or survive the environment. Only once the shortlist contains candidates that all satisfy every constraint does ranking on the objective, usually mass or cost, mean anything. Design meaning: this ordering is what stops you settling on a material before knowing what the part needs. The practical version is the six-step sequence: function and constraints, failure modes, property targets, shortlist, process and cost, then decide and justify.
Q6 · True/falseTrap

True or false: Because steel and aluminium have almost the same specific stiffness \(E/\rho\), an aluminium part can replace a steel one with no change to the section geometry and still meet the same stiffness requirement.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. Two different claims are being run together. Specific stiffness being similar — roughly 27 for steel (\(E=210\) GPa, \(\rho=7.8\) g/cm³) against roughly 26 for aluminium (\(E=70\) GPa, \(\rho=2.7\) g/cm³) — says that the two can reach the same stiffness at a similar mass. It does not say they reach it at the same geometry. Aluminium's absolute modulus is about one third of steel's, so an identical section would deflect about three times as much. To hit the same stiffness the aluminium section has to be made thicker or deeper, and it is that larger, lower-density section which brings the mass back to roughly the same figure. Design meaning: a material substitution is a redesign, not a swap. It has knock-on effects on the envelope, the joints and the tooling, and if the envelope is fixed the substitution may not be available at all.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

A tie of fixed length must meet a stiffness requirement in simple tension, for which the mass required is proportional to \(\rho/E\). Compare steel (\(E=210\) GPa, \(\rho=7.8\) g/cm³), aluminium (\(E=70\) GPa, \(\rho=2.7\) g/cm³) and carbon-fibre-reinforced polymer (CFRP) along the fibres (\(E=150\) GPa, \(\rho=1.6\) g/cm³). State which is lightest, by how much, and what would still have to be checked.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: CFRP is about 3.5 times lighter than steel; aluminium is fractionally heavier than steel, not lighter.
Step 1 — evaluate \(\rho/E\) for each, in (g/cm³)/GPa. Steel: \(7.8/210=0.0371\). Aluminium: \(2.7/70=0.0386\). CFRP: \(1.6/150=0.0107\). Lower is better, because mass is proportional to this quantity.
Step 2 — take ratios against steel. Aluminium: \(0.0386/0.0371=1.04\), so about 4% heavier. CFRP: \(0.0107/0.0371=0.29\), so about 71% lighter, a factor of roughly 3.5.
Sanity check. This is the reciprocal of the specific stiffness \(E/\rho\) quoted for these materials — about 27 for steel, 26 for aluminium and 94 for CFRP — and the rankings agree, with steel and aluminium essentially tied and CFRP far ahead. Two routes giving the same ordering confirms the index was not inverted. Note that a lower \(\rho/E\) must correspond to a higher \(E/\rho\); if it did not, the ratio was taken upside down.
What still has to be checked. The index only addresses stiffness-limited mass. Strength has to be confirmed separately, and for CFRP so does behaviour off the fibre axis, since these properties are quoted along the fibres and are much lower across them. Then come joining — CFRP cannot be welded and bolted holes cut fibres — tooling and unit cost, which usually only repay at volume, plus environmental durability and inspectability. Aluminium may still win over steel for reasons this calculation never touches, such as natural corrosion resistance.
Design meaning: for a stiffness-limited tie, substituting aluminium for steel saves essentially no mass — a result that contradicts the common assumption that aluminium is simply the light option.
Self-mark: one point for all three values of \(\rho/E\) with units; one for the ratios and the correct conclusion that aluminium is not lighter; one for a check against \(E/\rho\); one for naming at least two non-property factors that could still decide the outcome.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A bracket has a 20-year design life. Coated carbon steel costs $400 supplied and installed and must be recoated every 4 years. Stainless steel costs $1,300 and needs no intervention. In its intended location a recoat costs $600 in labour plus $1,500 in lost production while the line is stopped. (a) Compare the 20-year costs. (b) Show what happens if the bracket is instead easily accessible, so a recoat costs $150 with no shutdown. (c) State what the comparison depends on.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: in the original location the coated steel costs $8,800 against $1,300 for stainless; if access is easy, the coated steel costs $1,000 and becomes the cheaper option.
(a) Count the interventions. Recoating every 4 years over a 20-year life falls at years 4, 8, 12 and 16 — four recoats. The 20-year mark is the end of life, so no recoat is needed then. Cost per recoat \(=\$600+\$1{,}500=\$2{,}100\). Total for coated steel \(=\$400+4\times\$2{,}100=\$400+\$8{,}400=\$8{,}800\). Stainless \(=\$1{,}300\) with no further spend. The stainless option is cheaper by \(\$8{,}800-\$1{,}300=\$7{,}500\), about 6.8 times cheaper overall, despite costing 3.25 times as much to buy.
(b) Easy access. Cost per recoat becomes \$150 with no downtime, so the coated steel total is \(\$400+4\times\$150=\$400+\$600=\$1{,}000\), which is now \$300 less than the stainless option. The ranking reverses.
(c) What it depends on. Entirely on the cost of each intervention — that is, how hard the part is to reach and what an hour of stopped production is worth — multiplied by how many interventions the design life demands. Purchase price barely features in the first case, where downtime accounts for \(4\times\$1{,}500=\$6{,}000\) of the \$8,800.
Sanity check and caveat. Both totals should exceed the purchase price, and they do. The arithmetic here adds future dollars directly to present ones; a real appraisal would discount future costs, which reduces the advantage of avoiding later maintenance but does not reverse a 6.8-fold gap. Getting the intervention count wrong — five instead of four — is the usual slip, so count the years explicitly.
Design meaning: select on lifecycle cost, not purchase price, and record the assumption that drives the answer. The same two materials give opposite recommendations for the same bracket in two different locations.
Self-mark: one point for four interventions and the \$8,800 total; one for the comparison with \$1,300; one for the reversed conclusion at \$1,000 when access is easy; one for identifying access cost and downtime as the deciding variables, with the discounting caveat noted.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Picture an Ashby chart with Young's modulus \(E\) on the vertical axis and density \(\rho\) on the horizontal axis, both on logarithmic scales, with whole material families drawn as blobs — metals to the upper right, polymers to the lower left, ceramics high up, and foams in the bottom-left corner. For a light, stiff panel: (a) which region of the chart is desirable and why; (b) the steel blob sits well above the aluminium blob — explain why that does not settle the choice; (c) what you would check next.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: the upper-left region is desirable; vertical position alone compares \(E\), whereas the choice depends on \(E\) relative to \(\rho\).
(a) Where to look. You want high stiffness and low density, so the desirable direction is up (higher \(E\)) and to the left (lower \(\rho\)) — the upper-left region of the chart. Ceramics sit high but are brittle; foams sit far left but are compliant; the useful compromises for a light stiff panel are the engineering composites and the light alloys. The value of the chart is that it shows whole families at once, so you can see which class is even worth considering before looking up individual grades.
(b) Why steel being higher does not decide it. Vertical position on its own answers "which has the greater absolute stiffness", and steel does, at about \(E=210\) GPa against \(70\) GPa for aluminium. But the blobs also differ horizontally: steel sits at about 7.8 g/cm³ and aluminium at about 2.7 g/cm³. Both ratios come out close to the same value — around 27 against 26 — so on stiffness per unit weight the two families are effectively tied. Reading only the vertical axis answers a question the brief did not ask. Moving diagonally up and to the right, as steel does relative to aluminium, buys stiffness and pays for it in weight at almost exactly the same rate.
(c) What to check next. Confirm which index the loading actually calls for, since a panel in bending rewards \(E^{1/2}/\rho\) rather than \(E/\rho\) and that changes the ranking. Then apply the constraints the chart does not show at all: strength and the relevant failure modes, corrosion resistance in the service environment, whether the shape can be manufactured at the required volume, joining method, and cost. A chart screens families; it does not select a material.
Design meaning: "steel is stiffer than aluminium" is a true statement that answers the wrong question — which is precisely why aircraft are not built from the stiffest available metal.
Self-mark: one point for identifying the upper-left region with a reason; one for explaining that the vertical axis alone ignores density and quoting the near-equal ratios; one for noting that the bending case changes the index; one for at least two constraints the chart cannot show.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A designer notes that a component will never see more than 30% of its yield strength, screens all candidates on yield strength alone, and recommends the strongest available alloy. The component is a welded bracket on a vibrating machine mounted outdoors near the coast. Identify at least two failure modes this screening misses, name the property each requires, and state how the recommendation should change.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: fatigue and corrosion are both missed, and fracture from a weld flaw is a third; screening on yield strength can actively select the wrong material.
Missed mode 1 — fatigue. A vibrating machine applies a cyclic load, and a crack can initiate at a surface feature and grow a little each cycle until final fracture, all while the nominal stress stays far below yield. The relevant properties are the endurance limit and the surface condition, and weld toes are classic initiation sites. Being at 30% of yield offers no protection here, because fatigue is governed by the stress range and the number of cycles rather than by the margin to yield.
Missed mode 2 — corrosion. Coastal outdoor service means salt-laden, humid air. The property needed is environmental resistance in that specific environment, either inherent or through a coating that has to be maintained. Corrosion also feeds back into fatigue, since pits act as stress concentrations and shorten fatigue life.
A third — fracture from a flaw. Welding introduces potential defects and a heat-affected zone, so the plane-strain fracture toughness \(K_{IC}\) matters. This is where the original recommendation is actively dangerous rather than merely incomplete: the highest-strength alloys generally have the lowest toughness, so screening on yield strength alone tends to select the material least able to tolerate a weld flaw.
How the recommendation should change. List the failure modes first, then set a property target for each: an endurance limit with a margin at the expected stress range, adequate toughness, weldability without a large loss of properties in the heat-affected zone, and corrosion resistance for coastal air. Then shortlist against all of them together. A moderate-strength, weldable, tough alloy — or a stainless grade if the coating maintenance is unwelcome — will beat the strongest available alloy here. Complete the answer by stating why each rejected candidate was rejected, the trade-off accepted, and a backup if the main risk appears.
Design meaning: the failure mode determines the property, so skipping the failure-mode step does not merely leave the answer incomplete — it can point confidently at the worst available choice.
Self-mark: one point for fatigue with endurance limit and surface condition; one for corrosion with environmental resistance, ideally linking pitting to fatigue; one for the weld/toughness point and the strength-versus-toughness conflict; one for a revised recommendation that screens on all modes and justifies the rejections.

Q13 · Multiple choiceOne step

Beam bending, stiffness-limited, minimum mass: compare \(E^{1/2}/\(\rho\) for aluminium (\(E=70\) GPa, \(\rho=2.7\)) and titanium (\(E=110\) GPa, \(\rho=4.5\)).

  1. Aluminium wins
  2. Titanium wins
  3. Equal
  4. Neither — use \(\sigma_y/\(\rho\) instead
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. Al: \(\sqrt{70}/2.7=3.10\); Ti: \(\sqrt{110}/4.5=2.33\) (GPa\(^{1/2}\)/(g/cm³)). Sanity check: lighter metal with decent \(E\) often wins bending screens. Design meaning: titanium wins tension strength screens but not always bending stiffness.
Q14 · Multiple choiceTrap

The cheapest material by purchase price is selected for a coastal outdoor bracket. Which failure mode is most likely overlooked?

  1. Elastic deflection
  2. Corrosion and pit-assisted fatigue
  3. Colour mismatch
  4. Magnetic saturation
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Coastal service needs environmental resistance; pits initiate fatigue. Design meaning: lifecycle cost and failure-mode screening beat catalogue price.
Q15 · True/falseQuick

True or false: performance indices are derived from the governing design equation for a stated loading case and objective.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Tie in tension → \(\sigma_y/\(\rho\); beam in bending → \(E^{1/2}/\(\rho\). Design meaning: memorising one index without the loading case inverts rankings.
Q16 · True/falseTrap

True or false: because CFRP has very high \(E/\rho\), it can always replace steel in any shape without redesign.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. CFRP is anisotropic, notch-sensitive, and not weldable; geometry and load direction must be redesigned. Design meaning: substitution is a redesign project, not a drop-in swap.
Q17 · Short calculationWritten

Compare \(\sigma_y/\(\rho\) (MPa·cm³/g) for stainless (\(\sigma_y=520\) MPa, \(\rho=8.0\)) and high-strength Al (\(\sigma_y=450\) MPa, \(\rho=2.8\)). Which wins for a mass-minimised tie?

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: Al wins: 161 vs 65.
Steel: \(520/8.0=65\); Al: \(450/2.8=161\).
Sanity check: Al is ~2.5× better on this index though steel has higher \(\sigma_y\). Design meaning: still check corrosion, joining and fatigue before selecting Al outdoors.
Q18 · Short calculationWritten

Component life 15 years. Painted steel: $200 install + repaint every 3 years ($80 paint + $400 downtime). Stainless: $900 once. Compare 15-year cost.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: painted steel $2,120; stainless $900 over 15 years.
1. Repaints at years 3, 6, 9 and 12 → four events.
2. Cost per repaint \(=\$80+\$400=\$480\).
3. Steel total \(=\$200+4(\$480)=\$2{,}120\). Stainless \(=\$900\).
Sanity check: both totals exceed purchase price; stainless wins by \$1,220. Design meaning: downtime cost dominates — not sticker price.
Q19 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Ashby chart: \(E\) vs \(\rho\) log-log. Metals upper-right, foams lower-left, CFRP upper-left diagonal. For minimum-mass stiffness in tension, which direction on the chart is desirable and why is CFRP attractive?

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: desirable = up-left (high \(E\), low \(\rho\)) → high \(E/\rho\). CFRP sits on a favourable diagonal with very high specific stiffness. Design meaning: chart screens families before grade-level data entry.
Q20 · Short explanationWritten

A brief says 'pick the lightest metal.' Rewrite as constraints + objective, name two constraints a light magnesium casting might fail, and one index to screen on.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: objective: minimise mass. Constraints: yield/strength, stiffness, corrosion, temperature, manufacturability. Mg may fail corrosion in marine service and creep near 150 °C. Screen \(\sigma_y/\(\rho\) or \(E/\rho\) depending on whether strength or stiffness limits. Design meaning: 'lightest' without constraints selects foam — constraints make the question engineering.
