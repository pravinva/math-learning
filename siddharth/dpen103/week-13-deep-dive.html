How to revise
Classification—Working out which topic a question belongs to before touching an equation. The step most often skipped and most often fatal.
Transfer—Using an idea in a context you have not seen before. This is what the exam tests; recall alone is not enough.
Spaced repetition—Re-testing at growing intervals — 2 days, 7 days, 21 days — instead of re-reading. Far more effective per hour.
Active recall—Answering from memory before checking. Recognising an answer is not the same as being able to produce one.
Interleaving—Practising mixed topics in random order rather than in blocks. It feels harder and works better, precisely because it forces classification.

2 The five-line answer structure

One structure that fits almost every short-answer calculation in this subject. Using it consistently stops you dropping the easy marks around a correct number.

  • 1 — Classify. One line naming what kind of problem this is.
  • 2 — State assumptions and units. Uniaxial, room temperature, elastic — whatever you are taking for granted.
  • 3 — Compute. One governing relation, with units carried on every line.
  • 4 — Check the trend. Is the answer the right size and the right direction? Say so explicitly.
  • 5 — State the design implication. One sentence of "so the part should…".

Lines 4 and 5 are where the easy marks are, and they are the ones people run out of time for because they did not do line 1. A correct number with no interpretation is rarely full marks in this subject.

It also helps when you are stuck. If the calculation will not come, writing lines 1, 2 and 5 still earns real credit, whereas a blank page earns none.

Feeds: exam technique directly, and it costs nothing to adopt.
Say it cold: "Classify, state assumptions and units, compute, check the trend, then give the design implication."

3 The five recurring patterns

Across thirteen weeks the same five mathematical shapes keep reappearing. Recognising the shape often gets you to the answer before you have chosen the equation.

  • Square root — cracks. \(\sigma\propto1/\sqrt a\). Four times the flaw halves the strength. Weeks 4 and 12.
  • Inverse square root — grains. \(\sigma_y\propto d^{-1/2}\). Four times smaller grains double the contribution. Week 3.
  • Exponential — temperature. \(n_v\propto e^{-Q/kT}\). Never say "increases"; say exponentially. Weeks 3 and 5.
  • Weighted average — mixtures. Lever rule and rule of mixtures are the same algebra. Weeks 6 and 11.
  • Obstacle trade-off — strength versus ductility. Every strengthening method blocks dislocations and costs ductility. Weeks 3 and 4.

A useful drill: take any past question and name which of the five it uses before doing anything else. Most use exactly one.

Feeds: speed under time pressure, which is usually the binding constraint in the exam.
Say it cold: "Square root for cracks, inverse square root for grains, exponential for temperature, weighted average for mixtures, and a ductility cost for every strengthening route."

4 How to spend the remaining time

Revision that feels comfortable is usually revision that is not working. The discomfort of trying to recall something is the part that builds the memory.

  • Do not re-read. It creates a strong feeling of familiarity and almost no ability to produce the answer unprompted.
  • Test cold. Cover the card, say the green line, write the formula and the units. Ten cards in ten minutes.
  • Keep a collapse list. Only the cards that fail go on it. Everything else is done — stop revisiting it.
  • Space it. Re-test at 2 days, 7 days, then 21 days. A card that survives 21 days is finished.
  • Interleave. Shuffle topics deliberately. Solving ten fracture problems in a row never practises recognising a fracture problem.
  • Write the root cause of every error. Not "silly mistake" — "I used \(E_L\) when the load was transverse". Then re-test that specific trap.

For anyone who finds open-ended revision hard to start, the collapse list is the single most useful habit here. It converts a vague and infinite task into a short, shrinking, concrete one.

Feeds: the final weeks before the exam, where how you revise matters more than how long.
Say it cold: "Test cold rather than re-read, keep only the cards that collapse, space them at 2, 7 and 21 days, and shuffle the topics."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — classifying an unlabelled question

"A steel bracket in a vibrating machine has been in service two years. It fails suddenly with no visible deformation. Explain and recommend."

  1. Classify: vibrating means cyclic load, two years means many cycles, and no visible deformation rules out ordinary overload. This is fatigue — Week 4.
  2. Assumptions: nominal stress was below yield, since it survived two years and did not deform.
  3. Mechanism: a crack initiated at a surface feature — a machining mark or a sharp corner — grew a little each cycle, then reached the size where \(K_I\) hit \(K_{IC}\) and fractured instantly.
  4. Trend check: sudden fracture with no deformation is consistent with fatigue, since the final break is fast even in a ductile steel.
  5. Recommend: improve surface finish, shot peen for compressive residual stress, enlarge fillet radii, and reduce stress amplitude if possible. Note that a stronger alloy is likely to be counterproductive because of greater notch sensitivity.
What it means: The classification came from three words — vibrating, two years, no deformation. Once the topic is named, the rest is Week 4 content you already have.

Worked example 2 — a question spanning two weeks

"A transformer core material must have low loss and be formable into thin laminations. Justify a choice."

  1. Classify: two topics at once — magnetic properties from Week 10 and formability from Weeks 3 and 8.
  2. Magnetic requirement: the core reverses 50 times a second, so loop area is energy lost per cycle. This demands a soft magnetic material with low coercivity and a thin loop.
  3. Second loss mechanism: eddy currents, which is exactly why laminations are wanted. Thin insulated sheets break the circulating current paths.
  4. Formability requirement: rolling to thin sheet needs ductility, which points to a material that has not been heavily strengthened — recall from Week 3 that strengthening costs ductility.
  5. Resolve the conflict: silicon-iron alloy. Silicon raises resistivity, which reduces eddy currents further, while keeping the material soft magnetically and workable into sheet.
  6. Design implication: specify both the magnetic grade and the lamination thickness, because the material alone does not fix eddy losses.
What it means: Combined questions are the norm in Week 13. The method is to satisfy each requirement separately, then name where they conflict and how the choice resolves it.

Worked example 3 — the four checks on a stress–strain curve

"You are given a stress–strain curve and asked whether the material suits an application." What do you check?

  1. Yield margin: is \(\sigma_y\) comfortably above the working stress, with a defensible safety factor?
  2. Stiffness: is \(E\) enough for the deflection limit? Remember this is independent of strength.
  3. Energy: toughness if impact is possible, resilience if it must spring back.
  4. Actual failure mode: the curve is a single monotonic pull, so it says nothing about fatigue, creep or corrosion. If the service is cyclic, hot or wet, the curve alone cannot answer the question.
What it means: That fourth check is what separates a strong answer. A stress–strain curve is one test under one condition, and saying what it cannot tell you is itself worth marks.

The one habit worth carrying into the exam

Finish every answer with a sentence starting "so the part should…" or "so the material must…".

Most questions in this subject are ultimately design questions wearing a calculation as a disguise. The number is the middle of the answer, not the end of it, and the marks for interpretation are usually the cheapest on the paper.

If you are short of time, an answer with the classification, the governing equation and the design implication — but an incomplete calculation — will normally score better than a bare correct number.

Where marks get lost

  • Choosing an equation before classifying. The most expensive mistake available, because it wastes the time you would need to recover.
  • Re-reading notes and calling it revision. Familiarity is not recall. Test yourself cold instead.
  • Practising in blocks. Ten fracture questions in a row never trains you to recognise a fracture question. Shuffle them.
  • Stopping at the number. Add the trend check and the design sentence; they are quick marks.
  • Recording errors as "silly mistakes". Name the actual trap so you can re-test it, or you will make it again.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open. An answer you can only recognise is not an answer you can write in an exam.

1. You need low hysteresis loss and good formability. Which direction is likely best, and why?
A soft magnetic metallic alloy, typically a silicon-iron. Low coercivity and a thin loop minimise energy lost per cycle, silicon raises resistivity to cut eddy currents, and the material stays ductile enough to roll into thin laminations — which are themselves needed to break eddy-current paths. Then confirm cost, environment and manufacturability.
2. Two designs have equal static stress, but one has sharp notches under cyclic loading. Which fails first?
The notched one, by fatigue. The notch concentrates stress locally well above nominal, so a crack initiates there much sooner and then grows a little each cycle until \(K_I\) reaches \(K_{IC}\) and it fractures. Equal static stress is irrelevant because fatigue is driven by local conditions and cycle count, not by the nominal static value.
3. A lightweight requirement conflicts with high-temperature creep resistance. How do you structure the trade-off?
Quantify both first: maximum mass, maximum service temperature, required life. Note that creep depends on temperature relative to melting point, so light low-melting alloys such as aluminium are poor candidates if the service temperature is a large fraction of their melting point. Shortlist on specific stiffness or strength, screen against creep data at the actual temperature and duration, then check process route and lifecycle cost, and justify the final choice with its risks and a backup.
4. Given a stress–strain curve and asked about design suitability, what four checks do you run?
Yield margin against working stress with a safety factor; stiffness \(E\) against the deflection limit; energy absorption — toughness for impact, resilience for spring-back; and the likely real failure mode in service. The fourth matters most: a monotonic tensile curve says nothing about fatigue, creep or corrosion, so if the service is cyclic, hot or wet the curve alone cannot decide the question.
5. What is the single most useful thing to do in the first thirty seconds of an unfamiliar question?
Classify it. Read for the signal words — crack, cycles, grain size, \(T_g\), fibres, volume fractions, a loop, two compositions and a temperature — and name which week it belongs to before touching an equation. Nearly all serious lost marks come from applying the wrong framework, and thirty seconds spent here saves far more than it costs.
