How to revise
Classification—Working out which topic a question belongs to before touching an equation. The step most often skipped and most often fatal.
Transfer—Using an idea in a context you have not seen before. This is what the exam tests; recall alone is not enough.
Spaced repetition—Re-testing at growing intervals — 2 days, 7 days, 21 days — instead of re-reading. Far more effective per hour.
Active recall—Answering from memory before checking. Recognising an answer is not the same as being able to produce one.
Interleaving—Practising mixed topics in random order rather than in blocks. It feels harder and works better, precisely because it forces classification.

2 The five-line answer structure

One structure that fits almost every short-answer calculation in this subject. Using it consistently stops you dropping the easy marks around a correct number.

  • 1 — Classify. One line naming what kind of problem this is.
  • 2 — State assumptions and units. Uniaxial, room temperature, elastic — whatever you are taking for granted.
  • 3 — Compute. One governing relation, with units carried on every line.
  • 4 — Check the trend. Is the answer the right size and the right direction? Say so explicitly.
  • 5 — State the design implication. One sentence of "so the part should…".

Lines 4 and 5 are where the easy marks are, and they are the ones people run out of time for because they did not do line 1. A correct number with no interpretation is rarely full marks in this subject.

It also helps when you are stuck. If the calculation will not come, writing lines 1, 2 and 5 still earns real credit, whereas a blank page earns none.

Feeds: exam technique directly, and it costs nothing to adopt.
Say it cold: "Classify, state assumptions and units, compute, check the trend, then give the design implication."

3 The five recurring patterns

Across thirteen weeks the same five mathematical shapes keep reappearing. Recognising the shape often gets you to the answer before you have chosen the equation.

  • Square root — cracks. \(\sigma\propto1/\sqrt a\). Four times the flaw halves the strength. Weeks 4 and 12.
  • Inverse square root — grains. \(\sigma_y\propto d^{-1/2}\). Four times smaller grains double the contribution. Week 3.
  • Exponential — temperature. \(n_v\propto e^{-Q/kT}\). Never say "increases"; say exponentially. Weeks 3 and 5.
  • Weighted average — mixtures. Lever rule and rule of mixtures are the same algebra. Weeks 6 and 11.
  • Obstacle trade-off — strength versus ductility. Every strengthening method blocks dislocations and costs ductility. Weeks 3 and 4.

A useful drill: take any past question and name which of the five it uses before doing anything else. Most use exactly one.

Feeds: speed under time pressure, which is usually the binding constraint in the exam.
Say it cold: "Square root for cracks, inverse square root for grains, exponential for temperature, weighted average for mixtures, and a ductility cost for every strengthening route."

4 How to spend the remaining time

Revision that feels comfortable is usually revision that is not working. The discomfort of trying to recall something is the part that builds the memory.

  • Do not re-read. It creates a strong feeling of familiarity and almost no ability to produce the answer unprompted.
  • Test cold. Cover the card, say the green line, write the formula and the units. Ten cards in ten minutes.
  • Keep a collapse list. Only the cards that fail go on it. Everything else is done — stop revisiting it.
  • Space it. Re-test at 2 days, 7 days, then 21 days. A card that survives 21 days is finished.
  • Interleave. Shuffle topics deliberately. Solving ten fracture problems in a row never practises recognising a fracture problem.
  • Write the root cause of every error. Not "silly mistake" — "I used \(E_L\) when the load was transverse". Then re-test that specific trap.

For anyone who finds open-ended revision hard to start, the collapse list is the single most useful habit here. It converts a vague and infinite task into a short, shrinking, concrete one.

Feeds: the final weeks before the exam, where how you revise matters more than how long.
Say it cold: "Test cold rather than re-read, keep only the cards that collapse, space them at 2, 7 and 21 days, and shuffle the topics."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — classifying an unlabelled question

"A steel bracket in a vibrating machine has been in service two years. It fails suddenly with no visible deformation. Explain and recommend."

  1. Classify: vibrating means cyclic load, two years means many cycles, and no visible deformation rules out ordinary overload. This is fatigue — Week 4.
  2. Assumptions: nominal stress was below yield, since it survived two years and did not deform.
  3. Mechanism: a crack initiated at a surface feature — a machining mark or a sharp corner — grew a little each cycle, then reached the size where \(K_I\) hit \(K_{IC}\) and fractured instantly.
  4. Trend check: sudden fracture with no deformation is consistent with fatigue, since the final break is fast even in a ductile steel.
  5. Recommend: improve surface finish, shot peen for compressive residual stress, enlarge fillet radii, and reduce stress amplitude if possible. Note that a stronger alloy is likely to be counterproductive because of greater notch sensitivity.
What it means: The classification came from three words — vibrating, two years, no deformation. Once the topic is named, the rest is Week 4 content you already have.

Worked example 2 — a question spanning two weeks

"A transformer core material must have low loss and be formable into thin laminations. Justify a choice."

  1. Classify: two topics at once — magnetic properties from Week 10 and formability from Weeks 3 and 8.
  2. Magnetic requirement: the core reverses 50 times a second, so loop area is energy lost per cycle. This demands a soft magnetic material with low coercivity and a thin loop.
  3. Second loss mechanism: eddy currents, which is exactly why laminations are wanted. Thin insulated sheets break the circulating current paths.
  4. Formability requirement: rolling to thin sheet needs ductility, which points to a material that has not been heavily strengthened — recall from Week 3 that strengthening costs ductility.
  5. Resolve the conflict: silicon-iron alloy. Silicon raises resistivity, which reduces eddy currents further, while keeping the material soft magnetically and workable into sheet.
  6. Design implication: specify both the magnetic grade and the lamination thickness, because the material alone does not fix eddy losses.
What it means: Combined questions are the norm in Week 13. The method is to satisfy each requirement separately, then name where they conflict and how the choice resolves it.

Worked example 3 — the four checks on a stress–strain curve

"You are given a stress–strain curve and asked whether the material suits an application." What do you check?

  1. Yield margin: is \(\sigma_y\) comfortably above the working stress, with a defensible safety factor?
  2. Stiffness: is \(E\) enough for the deflection limit? Remember this is independent of strength.
  3. Energy: toughness if impact is possible, resilience if it must spring back.
  4. Actual failure mode: the curve is a single monotonic pull, so it says nothing about fatigue, creep or corrosion. If the service is cyclic, hot or wet, the curve alone cannot answer the question.
What it means: That fourth check is what separates a strong answer. A stress–strain curve is one test under one condition, and saying what it cannot tell you is itself worth marks.

The one habit worth carrying into the exam

Finish every answer with a sentence starting "so the part should…" or "so the material must…".

Most questions in this subject are ultimately design questions wearing a calculation as a disguise. The number is the middle of the answer, not the end of it, and the marks for interpretation are usually the cheapest on the paper.

If you are short of time, an answer with the classification, the governing equation and the design implication — but an incomplete calculation — will normally score better than a bare correct number.

Where marks get lost

  • Choosing an equation before classifying. The most expensive mistake available, because it wastes the time you would need to recover.
  • Re-reading notes and calling it revision. Familiarity is not recall. Test yourself cold instead.
  • Practising in blocks. Ten fracture questions in a row never trains you to recognise a fracture question. Shuffle them.
  • Stopping at the number. Add the trend check and the design sentence; they are quick marks.
  • Recording errors as "silly mistakes". Name the actual trap so you can re-test it, or you will make it again.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

Nominally identical pump housings burst at pressures differing by 40%, every failure arriving suddenly with no visible deformation. Which framework does this question belong to?

  1. Fatigue and the S–N curve
  2. Flaw-controlled brittle fracture in a ceramic, where strength is set by the largest defect present
  3. Creep at a large fraction of the melting temperature
  4. The lever rule applied to a two-phase microstructure
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Three signals point the same way: wide scatter between identical parts, no plastic deformation, and sudden failure. Ceramic strength follows \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), where \(a\) is the worst flaw in that specimen, so strength behaves as a property of the part rather than of the material and scatters accordingly. Fatigue is ruled out because nothing suggests cyclic loading or a cycle count. Creep needs sustained load at high homologous temperature and would show deformation before failure. The lever rule answers "how much of each phase", which nothing here asks. Design meaning: once classified, the response follows — design to a low percentile of the strength distribution rather than the mean, control the surface finish that sets \(a\), and proof test.
Q2 · Multiple choiceOne step

"An alloy of overall composition 35 wt% B is held at 700 °C, where the microstructure is two-phase. What proportion of the alloy is \(\beta\)?" Which route answers this?

  1. Hall–Petch, using the grain size
  2. Draw the tie-line at 700 °C to read the phase compositions, then apply the lever rule
  3. The rule of mixtures, weighting each phase modulus by its volume fraction
  4. Compare the applied stress intensity with the fracture toughness
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. The signal is unmistakable once you look for it: a composition and a temperature, with a question about how much of each phase. That is always the tie-line followed by the lever rule. The horizontal tie-line at the stated temperature gives the phase compositions \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_\beta\) where it meets the boundaries; the lever rule then converts those, with the overall composition \(C_0\), into mass fractions. Option A applies to yield strength versus grain size, not phase amounts. Option C is the composites relation — genuinely the same weighted-average algebra, which is why it is a plausible distractor, but there the fractions are given and the property is unknown, whereas here the property is given and the fractions are unknown. Option D belongs to a cracked component. Design meaning: naming the route before calculating is the whole skill being tested; the arithmetic afterwards is easy.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A tie-line gives \(C_\alpha=15\) wt% B and \(C_\beta=75\) wt% B, and the overall composition is \(C_0=35\) wt% B. What is the mass fraction of \(\alpha\)?

  1. 0.25
  2. 0.33
  3. 0.50
  4. 0.67
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: D. \(W_\alpha=(C_\beta-C_0)/(C_\beta-C_\alpha)=(75-35)/(75-15)=40/60=0.67\), and \(W_\beta=(35-15)/60=0.33\). Sanity checks, both worth writing down: the two fractions sum to 1.00, and \(C_0=35\) sits nearer \(C_\alpha=15\) than \(C_\beta=75\), so the alloy must be mostly \(\alpha\) — which 0.67 confirms. Option B is the answer you get by using the adjacent segment instead of the opposite one, which is the single most common lever-rule error, and the sanity check catches it every time. Design meaning: phase fractions drive properties, so getting the arm the wrong way round does not just cost the mark, it reverses the prediction about how the alloy will behave.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

An electrical contact was changed from pure copper to a stronger copper alloy of identical dimensions. Carrying the same current, it now runs measurably hotter. What is the explanation?

  1. Solute atoms scatter conduction electrons, lowering mobility and conductivity, so resistance and \(I^2R\) heating rise
  2. The alloy is creeping under the contact preload, generating heat
  3. Fatigue cracks have raised the electrical resistance of the contact
  4. The alloy has a higher carrier concentration, which increases heating
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. This one hides an electrical-properties question inside a thermal symptom. Conductivity is \(\sigma=nq\mu\); in a metal the carrier concentration \(n\) is fixed by the bonding, so the only term alloying can move is the mobility \(\mu\), and solute atoms disrupt the regular lattice and scatter conduction electrons. Lower \(\sigma\) means higher resistivity, and with the geometry unchanged \(R=\rho L/A\) rises, so \(P=I^2R\) rises with it. Note the mechanism is the same obstruction that provides the extra strength — it blocks dislocations and electrons alike, so the strength cannot be had without the conductivity penalty. Option D is wrong on the physics as well as the direction. Design meaning: to keep the alloy, increase the cross-section to restore the original resistance; better still, ask whether the strength could have come from geometry or extra support instead of from degrading the conductor.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: In this subject, a correct final number with no trend check and no design implication is usually a complete answer.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. Most questions here are design questions wearing a calculation as a disguise, so the number is the middle of the answer rather than the end of it. The five-line structure covers what a complete answer contains: classify the problem, state assumptions and units, compute with units carried on every line, check that the answer is the right size and direction, and finish with one sentence of design implication. Why this is practical rather than pedantic: lines 4 and 5 are the cheapest marks on the paper and take seconds, while they are also the ones people run out of time for — usually because they skipped line 1 and started with the wrong framework. It also gives you something to write when stuck: a classification, a governing relation and a design implication with an unfinished calculation will normally score better than a bare number, and far better than a blank page.
Q6 · True/falseQuick

True or false: Practising mixed topics in random order is harder than working through one topic at a time, and better preparation for the exam.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. This is interleaving, and the reason it works is precise: solving ten fracture problems in a row never practises recognising a fracture problem, because you already know what the next question is about. The exam presents questions shuffled and unlabelled, so classification is the step it actually tests, and blocked practice is the one arrangement that removes it. The trap to be aware of: interleaving feels worse. Accuracy during practice drops and progress feels slower, which is exactly why people abandon it — but that difficulty is the part that builds retrievable memory, whereas the comfort of re-reading produces familiarity with no ability to produce an answer unprompted. How to use it: test cold rather than re-read, keep a collapse list of only the cards that fail, re-test at 2, 7 and 21 days, and record the actual root cause of each error — "I used \(E_L\) when the load was transverse", not "silly mistake" — so the specific trap can be re-tested.
Q7 · Short explanationWritten

Classify each of these three unlabelled stems, name the governing idea or relation, and give the one-line reason. Spend no more than a minute in total.

(i) A moulded plastic housing is fine indoors but becomes noticeably brittle in a cold store at −20 °C. (ii) A transformer rewound to run at 400 Hz instead of 50 Hz now overheats, with no change of core material. (iii) A bracket moulded from chopped glass fibre is far weaker than a laminate made from exactly the same fibre and resin.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: (i) polymers and the glass transition; (ii) magnetic core losses and their frequency dependence; (iii) composites and the critical fibre length.
(i) Polymers — glass transition temperature \(T_g\). The signals are a plastic part and a temperature-dependent change in behaviour with no load change. Above \(T_g\) the chains retain segmental mobility and the material is relatively tough; below it that motion is frozen and the polymer is glassy and comparatively brittle. The service range has crossed \(T_g\). Note \(T_g\) is not melting — the material is solid on both sides of it. Design meaning: specify a polymer whose \(T_g\) sits below the coldest service temperature, and test impact at the cold end rather than at room temperature.
(ii) Magnetic properties — loss versus frequency. The signals are a core and a frequency change with the material held constant. Hysteresis loss is a fixed energy per cycle, set by the enclosed area of the B–H loop, so it scales roughly with frequency: eight times more cycles per second, eight times the heat. Eddy-current loss — from circulating currents induced in the conducting core — scales roughly with the square of frequency, so it rises about 64-fold and takes over as the dominant term. Design meaning: thinner laminations or a higher-resistivity material, because the new dominant loss is the one lamination addresses.
(iii) Composites — critical fibre length \(l_c=\sigma_f^{*}d/(2\tau_c)\). The signal is identical ingredients giving very different performance, which rules out any explanation based on the material itself. Load enters a fibre through interfacial shear along its length, so fibres shorter than \(l_c\) cannot build up to their tensile strength and pull out of the matrix instead of breaking. The reinforcement is largely wasted and failure becomes interface-controlled. Design meaning: thinner fibres or better interfacial bonding to lower \(l_c\), gentler processing to preserve fibre length, or accept the lower allowable and design to it.
What the drill is training. In all three cases the classification came from two or three words, and none of the calculations is difficult once the framework is named. That is the sequence to rehearse: read for the signal, name the week, then choose the relation — never the other way round.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A weight-critical stiffness-limited panel has three candidates. X: \(E=70\) GPa, density 2.7 g/cm³. Y: \(E=210\) GPa, density 7.8 g/cm³. Z: \(E=150\) GPa, density 1.6 g/cm³. Rank them on the appropriate measure and state what must still be checked before choosing.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: on specific stiffness, Z ≈ 94, Y ≈ 27, X ≈ 26 GPa per g/cm³. Z leads by roughly 3.5 times, while X and Y are effectively tied.
1. Classify and choose the measure. Weight-critical and stiffness-limited, so the relevant quantity is specific stiffness \(E/\rho\), not modulus alone and not density alone.
2. Candidate X. \(70/2.7=25.9\) GPa/(g/cm³).
3. Candidate Y. \(210/7.8=26.9\) GPa/(g/cm³).
4. Candidate Z. \(150/1.6=93.8\) GPa/(g/cm³).
5. The result that surprises people. Y is three times stiffer than X in absolute terms and also about three times denser, so by weight they are essentially equivalent. Ranking on \(E\) alone would have chosen Y and achieved nothing for mass. Meanwhile Z has a lower modulus than Y yet wins decisively, because its density is lower by far more than its stiffness is.
6. Sanity check. The three sets of figures correspond to aluminium, steel and a carbon-fibre composite, and the near-tie between aluminium and steel on specific stiffness is a well-known result — so the arithmetic is landing where it should.
7. What the number does not settle. Z's 150 GPa is a longitudinal value, available only along the fibres; across them the reciprocal relation lets the soft matrix dominate and the modulus can be lower by a factor of twenty, so every load path must be known at design time. Then there is joining, since a composite cannot be welded and bolting cuts fibres; delamination after impact, which may be invisible externally and needs a real inspection method; and tooling and layup cost, which suits high value or high volume rather than a one-off panel.
8. Design meaning. Shortlist on specific stiffness, then screen the shortlist against the requirements the index cannot see — joining route, inspection, temperature, environment, cost and volume. If Z's practical objections cannot be answered, X is the sensible choice over Y, because it matches Y by weight while being easier to form and join. A defensible selection answer names the winner, the measure it won on, its risks, and a backup.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

An exam figure shows three unlabelled panels. (i) A closed loop, axes marked \(H\) horizontally and \(B\) vertically. (ii) A field bounded by two curves, with a horizontal dashed line drawn across it ending on both curves and a marked point part-way along. (iii) A curve rising steeply and straight, stopping abruptly with no bend or plateau. For each panel, name what it is, state what can be read directly from it, and name the week.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: (i) a B–H hysteresis loop, Week 10; (ii) a tie-line across a two-phase field of a binary phase diagram, Week 6; (iii) a stress–strain curve for a brittle material, Week 12.
(i) Hysteresis loop. The axis labels alone identify it: applied field strength \(H\) as the cause, flux density \(B\) as the effect. Read four things directly — saturation, the flat top where every domain is aligned; remanence \(B_r\), the vertical-axis crossing, meaning what is left at zero applied field; coercivity \(H_c\), the horizontal-axis crossing, meaning the reverse field needed to clear it; and the enclosed area, which is energy lost as heat per cycle per unit volume. The shape then answers the design question by itself: thin and tall means soft magnetic and suits a core that must reverse constantly, wide and full means hard magnetic and suits a permanent magnet.
(ii) Tie-line on a phase diagram. A horizontal line across a two-phase field is always a tie-line at a fixed temperature. Its two endpoints give the compositions of the two phases, \(C_\alpha\) and \(C_\beta\); the marked point is the overall composition \(C_0\). What you cannot read directly is the phase amounts — those come from the lever rule using the segment lengths, with the fraction of a phase given by the opposite arm divided by the whole. Always check the fractions sum to one.
(iii) Brittle stress–strain curve. A straight rise stopping abruptly with no yield point and no plateau means no plastic deformation, which points to a ceramic or other brittle material. Read the modulus \(E\) from the slope and the fracture stress from the endpoint, and note that the area under the curve is tiny, so energy absorption is negligible and failure gives no warning. Expect substantial scatter between nominally identical specimens, because \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\) makes strength depend on the worst flaw in that particular specimen.
The transferable skill. Identify a figure from its axes and its shape before reading any value off it, because the axes name the topic and the shape names the sub-case. Then be explicit about what the figure cannot tell you: a hysteresis loop says nothing about mechanical strength, a phase diagram assumes equilibrium and says nothing about cooling rate, and a single monotonic tensile curve says nothing about fatigue, creep or corrosion. Stating that limit is itself worth marks.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

An inductor core must operate at 100 kHz with low loss, and it will be clamped into an assembly with a bolted bracket. A ferrite is proposed. Justify the magnetic choice, then identify the requirement that the magnetic argument alone will miss.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: the ferrite is right magnetically, because at 100 kHz eddy currents dominate and a ferrite is magnetically soft but electrically insulating. What the magnetic argument misses is that a ferrite is a ceramic, so it is brittle and flaw-controlled — and a bolted clamp is a very effective way to put a ceramic into tension.
1. Classify. Two topics at once: magnetic core losses, and brittle-material design. Combined questions are the norm here, and the method is to satisfy each requirement separately, then name where they conflict.
2. The magnetic requirement. The core reverses 100,000 times a second, so the enclosed B–H loop area, which is energy lost as heat per cycle per unit volume, must be small — that means a soft magnetic material with low coercivity.
3. Why frequency changes the answer. Relative to mains at 50 Hz the frequency has risen by a factor of 2000. Hysteresis loss scales roughly with frequency, so it rises about 2000-fold. Eddy-current loss scales roughly with frequency squared, so it rises by a factor of order a million and completely dominates. Laminated steel cannot be made thin enough to cope, so the fix has to be material rather than geometric.
4. Why a ferrite resolves it. A ferrite is magnetically soft, giving a thin loop and low hysteresis loss, while being electrically insulating, which suppresses eddy currents almost entirely rather than merely subdividing their paths. Both loss mechanisms are addressed at once.
5. Trend check. The choice should look worse at low frequency and better at high, and it does — ferrite has lower saturation flux density than silicon-iron, so at 50 Hz laminated steel remains the better answer. The recommendation is frequency-dependent, which is the sign that the reasoning is sound rather than a memorised preference.
6. The requirement the magnetic argument misses. Ferrites are ceramics. They have no easy dislocation motion, cannot yield, and fail from the largest flaw present according to \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), with a tensile strength perhaps a tenth of their compressive strength. Bolt preload, a bracket that deflects, or a hard point contact all introduce local bending and therefore tension. Add thermal gradients from the core's own losses and the tension can arrive without any mechanical mishandling at all.
7. Design responses. Clamp through a compliant pad so load is distributed and never applied as a point contact; keep the bolt path outside the core rather than through it; specify controlled torque; avoid sharp corners and demand generous radii; control surface finish and handling, since these set the flaw size that sets the strength; and design to a low percentile of the strength distribution rather than the mean, with a proof test if the application warrants it.
8. Design meaning. Specify the magnetic grade and the mounting arrangement, because either one alone leaves a failure mode uncovered. This is the shape of most Week 13 questions: the first requirement chooses the material, and the second requirement decides whether that material can actually survive being installed.
