Introduction, Bonding and Crystals

Why one material bends and another shatters comes down to two things: what holds the atoms together, and how those atoms are stacked. Everything else in this subject is a consequence of these two, so this week is worth more time than its lecture length suggests.

Working through the week

This section develops the week's ideas in order — from first principles through to how they are used in engineering practice.

The four ways atoms bond

Every solid is held together by forces between its atoms. Engineers classify these forces into four bond types, and the type largely determines what the material will do in service.

In a metallic bond, the outer electrons are shared across the whole solid rather than belonging to individual atoms. Those delocalised electrons carry electric current, reflect light (which is why metals look shiny), and allow atomic planes to slide past one another. That sliding is what we call plastic deformation — a metal bar bends because its layers can move.

In an ionic bond, one atom gives up an electron and another takes it. The resulting positive and negative ions lock into a rigid three-dimensional pattern. With no free electrons, ionic solids are electrical insulators. The locked geometry also resists sliding, so ionic ceramics tend to be hard and brittle.

In a covalent bond, electrons are shared between specific neighbouring atoms in fixed directions. Diamond is the extreme case: every carbon atom is tied to four neighbours in a stiff network, giving enormous hardness and a very high melting point. Most engineering ceramics contain strong covalent character.

Secondary bonds — van der Waals forces and hydrogen bonding — are much weaker. They hold polymer chains to one another. Because the forces are weak, polymers soften and melt at temperatures far below those of metals.

Knowing the bond type lets you predict conductivity, stiffness, ductility and melting behaviour before opening a data book.

How metal atoms pack in crystals

Most engineering metals are crystalline: their atoms sit in a repeating three-dimensional pattern. The smallest repeating box is the unit cell. Copy that box in all directions and the whole crystal is built.

Three packing patterns dominate metals. In face-centred cubic (FCC), atoms sit on each corner of a cube and at the centre of every face. Aluminium, copper and nickel use this structure. In body-centred cubic (BCC), atoms occupy the eight corners plus one at the cube centre — iron at room temperature and chromium are examples. In hexagonal close-packed (HCP), atoms form hexagonal layers stacked in an ABAB sequence; magnesium, zinc and titanium crystallise this way.

Each structure has a characteristic number of atoms per unit cell, a coordination number (how many neighbours each atom touches), and an atomic packing factor (APF) — the fraction of cell volume actually filled by atoms. FCC and HCP both reach APF 0.74, the maximum for equal spheres; BCC reaches 0.68.

Packing geometry also influences how easily a metal deforms. FCC metals offer many slip systems — directions along which atomic planes can glide — and are therefore relatively easy to press, draw and forge. HCP metals have fewer slip systems at room temperature and can be harder to form, even though their packing fraction matches FCC.

Density from the unit cell

Theoretical density follows directly from what sits inside the unit cell and how large that cell is:

\[\rho=\frac{nA}{V_c N_A}\]

Here \(n\) is the number of atoms belonging to one cell (4 for FCC, 2 for BCC, 6 for HCP), \(A\) is the atomic mass in g/mol, \(V_c\) is the cell volume, and \(N_A = 6.022\times10^{23}\) mol\(^{-1}\) converts between atom scale and gram scale.

For a cubic cell, \(V_c = a^3\), where \(a\) is the lattice parameter. Because volume depends on the cube of length, the lattice parameter must be converted to centimetres before cubing — a value quoted in nanometres must be multiplied by \(10^{-7}\) to obtain centimetres.

A worked calculation proceeds line by line: identify \(n\) from the crystal structure, convert \(a\), compute \(V_c\), find the mass of atoms in the cell, then divide. Sanity checks matter: aluminium is near 2.7 g/cm³, iron near 7.9 g/cm³, and lead near 11 g/cm³. An answer orders of magnitude away usually means a unit conversion or a wrong value of \(n\).

When two alloys share the same structure and similar chemistry, density scales inversely with \(a^3\). A lattice parameter only 2% larger lowers density by roughly 6%.

Translating design requirements into properties

Materials selection begins with what the part must do, not with a material name. Each function maps to a measurable property.

A bracket that must return to shape after loading is controlled by yield strength — the stress at which permanent deformation begins. A beam that must limit sag is controlled by Young's modulus \(E\), which governs elastic deflection. A component where mass matters is controlled by density \(\rho\), once strength and stiffness targets are met. A part exposed to salt spray needs corrosion resistance. A grounding strap needs electrical conductivity.

Each target should be written with a number or an inequality: yield strength above 250 MPa, deflection below 2 mm, density below 3 g/cm³. With targets stated, candidate materials can be screened systematically and alternatives rejected on specific grounds.

1 The four bond types

How the atoms are held together decides the personality of the material. There are four kinds, and you only need the behaviour each one predicts — not the quantum mechanics behind it.

The four, and what each one predicts
  • Metallic — the outer electrons roam free between positive cores. Conducts heat and electricity, bends rather than snaps, and is shiny because free electrons reflect light.
  • Ionic — one atom hands an electron to another, and the resulting plus and minus lock together. Hard, brittle and insulating, because there are no free electrons and no easy way for planes to slide.
  • Covalent — electrons shared in fixed directions. Very stiff, very brittle, very high melting point. Diamond is the extreme case.
  • Secondary (van der Waals and hydrogen bonding) — weak stickiness between whole molecules rather than a proper bond. This is why polymers are soft and melt at low temperatures.

The useful habit is to run the prediction in one direction: bond type first, then behaviour. If you are told a material is ionic, you should already expect brittle and insulating before you are told anything else.

Feeds: the shape of the stress–strain curve (Week 2), electrical and magnetic behaviour (Weeks 9 and 10), and why polymers act the way they do (Week 7).
Say it cold: "Metals conduct and bend because the electrons are loose; ceramics insulate and snap because the bonds are locked in place."

2 How the atoms stack

Metal atoms pack in a repeating pattern, like oranges in a crate. Which pattern you get changes how easily layers slide over each other, and sliding layers is exactly what bending is.

The three patterns, spelled out
  • FCC — face-centred cubic. An atom on each cube corner plus one in the centre of every face. Aluminium, copper, nickel.
  • BCC — body-centred cubic. An atom on each corner plus one dead centre. Iron at room temperature, chromium.
  • HCP — hexagonal close-packed. Hexagonal layers stacked ABAB. Magnesium, zinc, titanium.

That last column is the atomic packing factor (APF) — the fraction of the box that is atom rather than gap. FCC and HCP are as tightly packed as equal spheres can get.

The number that matters most for behaviour is not in this table: it is how many easy sliding planes each pattern has. FCC has the most, which is why FCC metals are usually the easiest to bend and press into shape.

Feeds: ductility, and therefore how a metal can be manufactured. It also feeds Week 3, because a defect only means something once there is a pattern for it to interrupt.
Say it cold: "FCC fills 74% of space with 12 neighbours and deforms most easily. BCC is looser at 68% with 8 neighbours."

3 Density from the unit cell

If you know what is inside the repeating box and how big the box is, you can calculate the density of the material from first principles. This is the standard calculation of Week 1.

\[\rho=\frac{nA}{V_c N_A}\]

\(\rho\) is density, \(n\) is the number of atoms in one unit cell, \(A\) is the atomic mass in grams per mole, \(V_c\) is the volume of the unit cell, and \(N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\) is Avogadro's number.

The logic is simply mass divided by volume. The top of the fraction is the mass of the atoms in one box; the bottom is the volume of that box. Everything else is unit conversion.

The part that costs marks
  • Cubic cells have \(V_c=a^3\), so a lattice parameter in nanometres must be converted to centimetres before cubing.
  • Get \(n\) from the structure, not from the periodic table: 4 for FCC, 2 for BCC.
  • Check your answer is believable. Steel is around 7.9, aluminium around 2.7. An answer of 0.5 or 400 means a conversion slipped.
Feeds: every weight-sensitive design decision later in the subject, and the specific-property thinking of Week 8.
Say it cold: "Density is the mass of the atoms in one box divided by the volume of that box."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — density of a BCC iron-like metal

Given: \(n=2\) (BCC), atomic mass \(A=55.85\) g/mol, lattice parameter \(a=0.286\) nm.
  1. Convert the lattice parameter to centimetres: \(0.286\) nm \(=0.286\times10^{-7}\) cm.
  2. Volume of the box: \(V_c=a^3=(0.286\times10^{-7})^3=2.34\times10^{-23}\) cm³.
  3. Mass of the atoms in the box: \(nA/N_A=2(55.85)/(6.022\times10^{23})=1.85\times10^{-22}\) g.
  4. Divide: \(\rho=1.85\times10^{-22}/2.34\times10^{-23}=7.9\) g/cm³.
What it means: That is right in the iron and steel range, which is the check that the conversions held. If you had left the lattice parameter in nanometres you would be out by a factor of \(10^{21}\), and the wrongness would be obvious.

Worked example 2 — density of an FCC metal

Given: \(n=4\) (FCC), \(A=63.5\) g/mol, \(a=0.361\) nm.
  1. \(a=3.61\times10^{-8}\) cm, so \(V_c=4.70\times10^{-23}\) cm³.
  2. Mass in the box: \(4(63.5)/(6.022\times10^{23})=4.22\times10^{-22}\) g.
  3. \(\rho=4.22\times10^{-22}/4.70\times10^{-23}=8.98\) g/cm³.
What it means: Copper, and the value matches the handbook. Note that FCC put four atoms in the box rather than two, which is where most of the extra density came from.

Worked example 3 — reasoning without a calculator

Two BCC alloys have the same \(n\) and the same atomic mass. Alloy B has a lattice parameter 2% larger. Which is denser?

  1. With \(n\) and \(A\) fixed, the top of the density fraction is identical for both.
  2. Only \(V_c=a^3\) differs, so \(\rho\propto1/a^3\).
  3. B has the larger box, so B has the lower density. Alloy A is denser.
  4. Roughly how much? A 2% larger edge gives about 6% more volume, so about 6% less density.
What it means: Questions worded as "which is denser and why" want this reasoning, not a full calculation. Spotting that only one term varies is the whole answer.

Where marks get lost

  • Leaving the lattice parameter in nanometres. The single most common lost mark in Week 1. Convert to centimetres before cubing.
  • Using the atomic number instead of the atomic mass. \(A\) in the density formula is grams per mole.
  • Guessing atoms per cell. FCC is 4 and BCC is 2. These come from the structure and are worth memorising outright.
  • Calling BCC "less dense" as a rule. BCC has a lower packing fraction, but a BCC metal of heavy atoms can easily out-weigh an FCC metal of light ones. Packing fraction and density are different things.
  • Naming a material before stating property targets. Selection answers that skip the targets cannot justify their rejections, and the rejections carry the marks.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

Which row correctly describes body-centred cubic (BCC) packing?

  1. 4 atoms per unit cell, 12 nearest neighbours, packing factor 0.74
  2. 2 atoms per unit cell, 8 nearest neighbours, packing factor 0.68
  3. 6 atoms per unit cell, 12 nearest neighbours, packing factor 0.74
  4. 2 atoms per unit cell, 12 nearest neighbours, packing factor 0.68
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. BCC — body-centred cubic — is a cube with an atom on each of the eight corners plus one atom sitting at the centre of the cube. The eight corner atoms are each shared between eight neighbouring cells, giving \(8\times\tfrac18=1\) atom, and the central atom belongs entirely to this cell, so \(n=2\). Each atom touches 8 others, and the APF — atomic packing factor, the fraction of the cell volume that is atom rather than gap — is 0.68. Option A is FCC (face-centred cubic) and option C is HCP (hexagonal close-packed). Why it matters: \(n=2\) is what you feed into the density formula, so getting this row wrong makes every later number wrong by a factor of two.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

A supplier tells you only that a new solid is ionically bonded. Before you see any test data, which set of behaviours should you expect?

  1. Shiny, conducts electricity well, bends before breaking
  2. Hard, brittle, and an electrical insulator
  3. Soft, low melting point, easily scratched with a fingernail
  4. High electrical conductivity together with a very low stiffness
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. In an ionic bond one atom hands an electron to another, and the resulting positive and negative ions lock together. There are no delocalised (free-roaming) electrons, so it cannot conduct — it insulates. The charge pattern also means planes of atoms cannot slide without bringing like charges together, so there is no easy plastic deformation and the solid is hard and brittle. Option A describes metallic bonding, option C describes secondary bonding such as van der Waals stickiness between polymer molecules. Design meaning: the prediction runs bond type first, then behaviour, so you can rule out an ionic ceramic for a part that must be bent into shape before you test anything.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A BCC metal has atomic mass \(A=52.00\) g/mol and lattice parameter \(a=0.289\) nm. Using \(N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\) mol\(^{-1}\), which value is closest to its density?

  1. 3.6 g/cm³
  2. 7.2 g/cm³
  3. 14.3 g/cm³
  4. \(7.2\times10^{21}\) g/cm³
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Use \(\rho=nA/(V_cN_A)\) with \(n=2\) for BCC. Convert first: \(a=0.289\) nm \(=2.89\times10^{-8}\) cm, so \(V_c=a^3=2.41\times10^{-23}\) cm³. Mass of the atoms in one cell is \(2(52.00)/(6.022\times10^{23})=1.73\times10^{-22}\) g. Dividing, \(\rho=1.73\times10^{-22}/2.41\times10^{-23}=7.2\) g/cm³, which is chromium. Sanity check: engineering metals sit roughly between 2 g/cm³ (aluminium 2.7) and 20 g/cm³, so 7.2 is believable. Option A is what you get from using \(n=1\), option C from \(n=4\), and option D is the classic result of cubing the lattice parameter while it is still in nanometres.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

Metal X is BCC with an APF of 0.68. Metal Y is FCC with an APF of 0.74. Which conclusion is safe on this information alone?

  1. Y must be denser, because it packs more atom into the same box
  2. X must be denser, because looser packing leaves room for heavier atoms
  3. Neither density can be ranked without the atomic masses and lattice parameters
  4. Their densities must be equal, because packing factor and density measure the same thing
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. APF is a purely geometric fraction — how much of the box is atom rather than empty gap. Density is mass per unit volume, so it also depends on how heavy each atom is (\(A\)) and how big the box is (\(a\)). Tungsten is BCC at about 19.3 g/cm³ and aluminium is FCC at about 2.7 g/cm³, which settles the argument: the looser structure is over seven times denser here. Why the trap works: "more tightly packed" sounds like "heavier", but the two statements are about different quantities. Only the full calculation \(\rho=nA/(V_cN_A)\) can rank them.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: window glass is amorphous, so no unit cell repeats through the solid.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Amorphous means the atoms are frozen in a jumble with no long-range repeating order, which is exactly the definition of having no unit cell. A unit cell is the smallest repeating box of a pattern, and there is no pattern here to have a smallest box of. Crystalline solids — most metals — do repeat, which is why FCC, BCC and HCP can be named for them. Design meaning: because there are no crystal planes and no grain boundaries, glass has no dislocation-driven plastic deformation, and that is one reason it fails suddenly from a surface flaw.
Q6 · True/falseTrap

True or false: because FCC and HCP both fill 74% of space with 12 nearest neighbours, the two structures are equally easy to press into shape.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. The two numbers in the statement are correct — both are close-packed at APF 0.74 with a coordination number of 12 — but neither number governs formability. What governs it is how many easy sliding planes the structure offers. FCC has the most, which is why aluminium, copper and nickel press and draw so readily, while HCP metals such as magnesium, zinc and titanium have far fewer and are notably harder to form at room temperature. Why the trap works: packing factor and coordination number are the numbers printed in the table, so they look like the answer to everything. The examinable point is that the property table and the behaviour table are not the same table.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

An FCC metal has \(A=58.69\) g/mol and \(a=0.352\) nm. Calculate its theoretical density in g/cm³, then state one check that the answer is believable. Take \(N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\) mol\(^{-1}\).

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(\rho\approx8.9\) g/cm³ (nickel).
1. FCC — face-centred cubic — has \(n=4\) atoms per unit cell: \(8\) corners \(\times\tfrac18=1\), plus \(6\) faces \(\times\tfrac12=3\).
2. Convert the lattice parameter before cubing it: \(a=0.352\) nm \(=3.52\times10^{-8}\) cm.
3. Cell volume: \(V_c=a^3=(3.52\times10^{-8})^3=4.36\times10^{-23}\) cm³.
4. Mass of the atoms in one cell: \(nA/N_A=4(58.69)/(6.022\times10^{23})=3.90\times10^{-22}\) g.
5. Divide: \(\rho=3.90\times10^{-22}\text{ g}/4.36\times10^{-23}\text{ cm}^3=8.94\) g/cm³.
Sanity check: the handbook value for nickel is about 8.90 g/cm³, and the result sits sensibly between aluminium (2.7) and lead (11.3). If the answer had come out near \(10^{21}\), the nanometre-to-centimetre conversion was skipped; if near 2.2, \(n=1\) was used instead of 4.
Design meaning: at roughly 8.9 g/cm³ this metal is over three times heavier than aluminium for the same volume, so any weight-driven design has to justify it on strength, stiffness or corrosion grounds.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A BCC metal is measured at \(\rho=7.87\) g/cm³ and has \(A=55.85\) g/mol. Work backwards to find its lattice parameter in nanometres.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(a\approx0.287\) nm.
1. Rearrange \(\rho=nA/(V_cN_A)\) for the cell volume: \(V_c=nA/(\rho N_A)\).
2. Substitute with \(n=2\) for BCC: \(V_c=2(55.85)/[(7.87\text{ g/cm}^3)(6.022\times10^{23}\text{ mol}^{-1})]=111.7/(4.74\times10^{24})=2.36\times10^{-23}\) cm³.
3. For a cubic cell \(V_c=a^3\), so \(a=(2.36\times10^{-23})^{1/3}=2.87\times10^{-8}\) cm.
4. Convert back: \(2.87\times10^{-8}\) cm \(=2.87\times10^{-1}\) nm \(=0.287\) nm.
Sanity check: lattice parameters of metals are a few tenths of a nanometre, so 0.287 nm is the right size — and it matches the standard figure for iron at room temperature, which is consistent with \(A=55.85\) g/mol and a density of 7.87 g/cm³.
Design meaning: this is the reverse of the usual question, and it is how a measured density plus a known structure can be used to confirm which phase or allotrope you actually have in hand.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Two unit-cell sketches are drawn side by side. Cell 1 is a cube with an atom at each of the eight corners and one atom at the geometric centre of the cube. Cell 2 is a cube with an atom at each of the eight corners and one atom at the centre of every face. For each cell, name the structure, count the atoms per cell showing how the shared atoms are counted, and state which of the two metals you would expect to press more easily into a curved panel.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: Cell 1 is BCC with \(n=2\); Cell 2 is FCC with \(n=4\); Cell 2 presses more easily.
Cell 1 — BCC (body-centred cubic). A corner atom is shared between the eight cubes that meet at that corner, so each contributes \(\tfrac18\): \(8\times\tfrac18=1\). The body-centre atom lies wholly inside this cube and contributes 1. Total \(n=1+1=2\), coordination number 8, APF 0.68.
Cell 2 — FCC (face-centred cubic). Corners again give \(8\times\tfrac18=1\). Each face atom is shared between two neighbouring cubes, so contributes \(\tfrac12\): \(6\times\tfrac12=3\). Total \(n=1+3=4\), coordination number 12, APF 0.74.
Which forms more easily: Cell 2. FCC offers the largest number of easy sliding planes, so its atomic planes can slip past one another readily and the metal deforms plastically rather than cracking. This is why the FCC metals — aluminium, copper, nickel — dominate pressed and drawn products.
The counting trap: a quick glance at Cell 2 suggests 14 atoms and Cell 1 suggests 9. Both are wrong because they ignore sharing. Always count fractions, because \(n\) goes straight into the density calculation.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A junior engineer writes the specification "use a strong material" for a small bracket that must also carry an earth path back to the chassis and be bent to its final angle by hand on site. Rewrite the specification as property targets, and say which bonding family this rules out and why.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: replace "strong" with three measurable targets — yield strength, ductility and electrical conductivity — which together rule out ionic and covalent solids.
Step 1 — turn each function into the property that controls it. "Does not stay bent in service" is controlled by yield strength, so the target is a working stress comfortably below yield. "Carries an earth path" is controlled by electrical conductivity, so the target is enough conduction for the grounding role. "Can be bent by hand on site without cracking" is controlled by ductility, so the target is a material that deforms plastically at room temperature.
Step 2 — read the targets back as a bonding requirement. Conductivity needs delocalised electrons, which only metallic bonding provides. Site bending needs atomic planes that can slide, which again points to metallic bonding.
Step 3 — state the exclusions with reasons. Ionic and covalent solids are ruled out twice over: they have no free electrons, so they cannot carry the earth path, and their locked directional bonds give no easy sliding, so bending them by hand would fracture them. Polymers, held together by weak secondary bonds, are ruled out on conductivity as well.
Why the original wording fails: "strong" is not measurable, so it cannot be checked, and it hides the fact that two of the three real requirements here are not about strength at all. Selection answers score on the justified rejections, and you cannot reject anything until the targets are written down.
Q11 · Multiple choiceQuick

Which row best describes covalent bonding in a ceramic such as silicon carbide?

  1. Electrons are shared between specific atom pairs; the solid is hard, stiff and an electrical insulator
  2. Positive and negative ions attract; the solid conducts heat well but is brittle
  3. Delocalised electrons hold the lattice together; the solid is ductile and conducts electricity
  4. Weak secondary forces between molecules; the solid melts at low temperature
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. Covalent bonding shares electrons between neighbouring atoms in fixed directions, which gives very strong, directional bonds. The electrons are localised in those bonds, so conductivity is poor, and the locked directions give high stiffness with little plastic deformation. Option B is ionic, option C is metallic, option D is secondary bonding in polymers. Design meaning: a covalent ceramic is chosen when stiffness and hardness at high temperature matter more than toughness or conductivity.
Q12 · Multiple choiceQuick

Hexagonal close-packed (HCP) metals have which coordination number and atomic packing factor?

  1. 8 nearest neighbours, APF 0.68
  2. 12 nearest neighbours, APF 0.74
  3. 6 nearest neighbours, APF 0.52
  4. 4 atoms per unit cell, APF 0.74
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. HCP is a close-packed structure with 12 nearest neighbours and APF 0.74, the same geometric packing as FCC. Option A is BCC, option C confuses coordination with a simple cubic arrangement, and option D mixes atoms per cell with packing factor. Design meaning: HCP shares the tight packing of FCC but offers fewer easy slip systems, which affects formability at room temperature.
Q13 · Multiple choiceOne step

An FCC metal has \(A=63.55\) g/mol and lattice parameter \(a=0.361\) nm. Using \(N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\) mol\(^{-1}\), which density is closest?

  1. 2.2 g/cm³
  2. 4.5 g/cm³
  3. 8.9 g/cm³
  4. 17.8 g/cm³
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. FCC has \(n=4\). Convert \(a=0.361\) nm \(=3.61\times10^{-8}\) cm, so \(V_c=a^3=4.71\times10^{-23}\) cm³. Mass per cell \(=4(63.55)/(6.022\times10^{23})=4.22\times10^{-22}\) g, giving \(\rho=8.96\) g/cm³ — copper. Sanity check: copper is about 8.9 g/cm³, so the answer sits in the right band. Option A uses \(n=1\), option D uses \(n=8\). Design meaning: copper's density is three times aluminium's for the same volume, which matters in weight-sensitive conductors even when strength is adequate.
Q14 · Multiple choiceTrap

Two metals are both close-packed with coordination number 12. Metal P is FCC and metal Q is HCP. A design brief requires deep drawing into a hemispherical dome at room temperature. Which statement is safest?

  1. Either is equally suitable because both have APF 0.74
  2. P is the better choice because FCC offers more slip systems for plastic forming
  3. Q is the better choice because HCP packs atoms more efficiently along the draw axis
  4. Both will fail equally because close-packed structures cannot be formed
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Identical packing fraction and coordination number describe geometry only. Formability depends on how many independent slip systems are available, and FCC metals typically offer more at room temperature. Magnesium and zinc (HCP) are harder to deep-draw than aluminium and copper (FCC) despite similar packing. Design meaning: when the brief says 'press' or 'draw', check crystal structure and slip, not just the numbers in the packing-factor table.
Q15 · True/falseQuick

True or false: metallic bonding relies on a sea of delocalised electrons that carry both mechanical load transfer and electrical current.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. In a metal, valence electrons are shared across the whole lattice. They allow planes to slide while maintaining cohesion, and they move freely under an electric field. Design meaning: any part that must be both formed and earthed points straight at a metallic solid.
Q16 · True/falseTrap

True or false: once the crystal structure of a metal is known, its yield strength, electrical conductivity and corrosion resistance are fully determined.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. Structure fixes geometry — atoms per cell, packing factor, slip systems — but mechanical and environmental behaviour also depend on composition, grain size, defects and temperature. Two FCC metals, aluminium and gold, share a structure yet differ enormously in strength and chemistry. Design meaning: structure is a starting prediction, not a complete specification; always state the property targets you still need to verify.
Q17 · Short calculationWritten

HCP titanium has \(A=47.87\) g/mol, \(a=0.295\) nm and \(c=0.468\) nm. The unit-cell volume is \(V_c=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}a^2c\). Find the theoretical density in g/cm³. HCP has \(n=6\) atoms per cell. Take \(N_A=6.022\times10^{23}\) mol\(^{-1}\) and give one sanity check.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(\rho\approx4.5\) g/cm³.
1. Convert lengths to cm: \(a=2.95\times10^{-8}\) cm, \(c=4.68\times10^{-8}\) cm.
2. \(V_c=\tfrac{\sqrt{3}}{2}(2.95\times10^{-8})^2(4.68\times10^{-8})=3.53\times10^{-23}\) cm³.
3. Cell mass \(=6(47.87)/(6.022\times10^{23})=4.77\times10^{-22}\) g.
4. \(\rho=4.77\times10^{-22}/3.53\times10^{-23}=4.51\) g/cm³.
Sanity check: titanium handbook value is about 4.5 g/cm³ — lighter than steel, heavier than aluminium.
Design meaning: titanium buys strength-to-weight in aerospace because this density sits well below steel while stiffness remains high.
Q18 · Short calculationWritten

Two FCC alloys share the same atomic mass \(A=58.7\) g/mol. Alloy X has \(a=0.352\) nm; alloy Y has \(a=0.362\) nm. Without full working, which is denser and by roughly what percentage? Then verify with \(\rho=nA/(V_cN_A)\), \(n=4\).

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: X is denser; about 8.5% higher density than Y.
1. With \(n\) and \(A\) fixed, \(\rho\propto1/a^3\). Y's edge is 2.8% larger, so volume is \((1.028)^3=1.087\) times bigger and density is lower by about 8%.
2. X: \(a=3.52\times10^{-8}\) cm, \(V_c=4.36\times10^{-23}\) cm³, \(\rho=8.94\) g/cm³.
3. Y: \(a=3.62\times10^{-8}\) cm, \(V_c=4.74\times10^{-23}\) cm³, \(\rho=8.24\) g/cm³.
4. Ratio \(8.94/8.24=1.08\).
Sanity check: a few percent change in lattice parameter moves density by roughly three times that percentage — believable for a small alloying shift.
Design meaning: composition changes that swell the lattice lighten the part even when the chemistry looks similar on a data sheet.
Q19 · Diagram interpretationWritten

A table lists four solids with measured properties:

Material 1: shiny, \(\sigma\approx10^7\) S/m, deforms plastically.
Material 2: transparent, \(\sigma<10^{-12}\) S/m, hard, shatters without yield.
Material 3: opaque, \(\sigma<10^{-10}\) S/m, very high compressive strength.
Material 4: flexible film, \(\sigma<10^{-14}\) S/m, low \(T_m\), melts before 200 °C.

For each, name the dominant bonding family and one engineering application that fits those properties.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer:
1 — metallic: delocalised electrons explain conductivity and ductility; e.g. busbar or formed bracket.
2 — covalent/ionic glass or ceramic: no free electrons, no slip; e.g. optical window (if flaw-tolerant design allows).
3 — ionic/covalent ceramic: strong bonds, poor tension, good compression; e.g. kiln brick or bearing race in compression.
4 — polymer with secondary bonding: weak intermolecular forces, low melting point; e.g. packaging film.
Design meaning: the table is read bond type → properties → application, the same chain used when rewriting vague briefs into measurable targets.
Q20 · Short explanationWritten

A satellite antenna reflector must be stiff, lightweight, corrosion-resistant in salt air, and manufacturable as a thin shell. A colleague writes 'use stainless steel because it is strong.' Rewrite the brief as property targets, name two measurable constraints the steel may fail, and suggest what information you would look up next.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: replace 'strong' with specific stiffness \(E/\rho\), yield strength for handling loads without permanent set, corrosion resistance in marine exposure, and minimum shell thickness for forming.
Stainless steel may fail the mass budget because \(\rho\approx7.8\) g/cm³ limits \(E/\rho\) compared with aluminium or CFRP, and it may fail forming limits if the required shell is thinner than the mill can roll reliably.
Next lookups: handbook \(E\), \(\rho\), \(\sigma_y\) for candidates; galvanic compatibility in the stack; and manufacturing gauge limits.
Design meaning: 'strong' hides whether the driver is weight, stiffness or environment — writing targets first is what makes rejection of steel (or acceptance) defensible.
