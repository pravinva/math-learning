What this week is really asking
How freely can charge move through this material, and what changes that?

One equation carries most of the week: conductivity is carrier count times charge times mobility. Nearly every question is about which of those three terms a change affects, and whether two of them move in opposite directions.

Working through the week

Read this section first if the topic is new. It builds the ideas in order. Everything below — the glossary, cards, worked examples and exam practice — stays as your summary and drill.

Back to free electrons

Metallic bonding left electrons free to move. Apply a voltage and they drift — electrical conduction. Resistivity \(\rho\) (or conductivity \(\sigma = 1/\rho\)) measures how easily. Impurities, temperature and crystal defects scatter electrons and raise resistivity.

Metals vs semiconductors vs insulators

Metals have partially filled bands — many empty states at the same energy, so electrons move easily. Insulators have a large gap with no states available. Semiconductors have a small gap; heating promotes electrons across the gap, so conductivity rises with temperature — opposite to metals.

Hall effect and carrier type

The Hall voltage tells you whether the main carriers are electrons or holes and gives carrier concentration. It connects measured voltage to the physics of conduction in doped semiconductors.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 The conductivity equation

Three factors multiplied together. How many carriers there are, how much charge each carries, and how freely each moves.

\[\sigma=nq\mu\]

\(\sigma\) is conductivity, \(n\) the carrier concentration per cubic metre, \(q\) the charge per carrier and \(\mu\) the mobility.

Because it is a product, any change that raises one term while lowering another may leave conductivity almost unmoved. This is the source of most exam questions in this week, and the reason a "more must be better" instinct fails here.

Metals have an enormous \(n\) that barely changes — the free electrons are already there and no more can be added. Semiconductors have a small \(n\) that can be changed by orders of magnitude through doping or heating, which is exactly what makes them useful.

Feeds: the temperature and doping behaviour below, and any question about why a processing change did not deliver what was expected.
Say it cold: "Conductivity is carrier count times charge times mobility, so raising one term while cutting another can leave it unchanged."

2 Resistivity versus resistance

One belongs to the material and one belongs to the object. Confusing them makes a calculation impossible to complete.

\[\rho=\frac{1}{\sigma}\qquad R=\frac{\rho L}{A}\]

\(\rho\) is resistivity in ohm-metres, \(L\) the length of the object and \(A\) its cross-sectional area.

Resistivity is a material property — every piece of pure copper has the same value regardless of shape. Resistance depends on geometry, so a long thin copper wire and a short fat one have very different resistances despite identical resistivity.

When a question gives you a material property and asks for a circuit quantity, the geometry conversion is the step being tested. Data sheets quote \(\rho\); multimeters measure \(R\).

Feeds: any calculation linking a materials choice to circuit behaviour or resistive heating.
Say it cold: "Resistivity belongs to the material; resistance belongs to the object and needs its length and area."

3 Temperature — opposite directions

Heat a metal and it conducts worse. Heat a semiconductor and it conducts better. Same equation, different dominant term.

  • Metal: \(n\) is fixed — the electrons are already free. Heating makes the atoms vibrate harder, so carriers scatter more and \(\mu\) falls. Conductivity falls, and resistivity rises roughly linearly.
  • Semiconductor: heating promotes many more electrons into the conducting state, so \(n\) rises exponentially. Mobility still falls, but the rise in \(n\) overwhelms it. Conductivity rises sharply.

The graph of resistivity against temperature is a favourite exam figure precisely because the two lines slope opposite ways. If you can say which is which and give the reason in terms of \(n\) and \(\mu\), you have the whole answer.

Adding impurities to a metal also lowers conductivity, for the same scattering reason. This is why pure copper is used for wiring and alloyed copper is not — alloying buys strength and pays in conductivity.

Feeds: the reason resistance thermometers work at all, and why power cables are sized for hot-weather conditions.
Say it cold: "Metals lose conductivity when heated because mobility falls; semiconductors gain it because carrier count rises exponentially."

4 The Hall effect

Put a current-carrying sample in a magnetic field and the carriers get pushed sideways, piling up on one face until the resulting voltage stops any further pile-up.

\[R_H=\frac{E_H}{JB}\approx-\frac{1}{nq}\]

\(R_H\) is the Hall coefficient, \(E_H\) the sideways electric field, \(J\) the current density and \(B\) the magnetic field.

The measurement is valuable for two reasons. It gives you \(n\), the carrier concentration, which is otherwise hard to reach. And its sign tells you whether the carriers are negative electrons or positive holes — something no conductivity measurement can reveal.

The physical picture is a balance. The magnetic force pushes carriers to one side; charge accumulates there; the resulting electric field pushes back; equilibrium is reached when the two forces cancel, and the voltage at that point is what you measure.

Practically this is everywhere — Hall sensors measure wheel speed, crankshaft position and current without any physical contact.

Feeds: sensor selection, and any semiconductor characterisation question.
Say it cold: "A magnetic field pushes carriers sideways until the voltage balances it, and that voltage gives carrier concentration and sign."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — why doping disappointed

Given: Doping doubles \(n\), but the measured conductivity barely changes.
  1. Start from \(\sigma=nq\mu\).
  2. Doubling \(n\) alone would double \(\sigma\).
  3. But the dopant atoms are foreign atoms in the lattice, and they scatter carriers.
  4. That extra scattering lowers \(\mu\), roughly halving it here.
  5. The product \(n\mu\) is therefore almost unchanged, so \(\sigma\) barely moves.
What it means: This is the classic Week 9 question. The instinct that more carriers must mean more conductivity ignores that the way you added them also obstructs them.

Worked example 2 — resistivity to resistance

Given: \(\rho=2\times10^{-6}\) Ω·m, \(L=0.4\) m, \(A=2\times10^{-6}\) m².
  1. \(R=\rho L/A\).
  2. Numerator: \(2\times10^{-6}\times0.4=8\times10^{-7}\).
  3. Divide by area: \(8\times10^{-7}/2\times10^{-6}=0.4\) Ω.
  4. Check the units: Ω·m × m ÷ m² leaves Ω, as it must.
What it means: 0.4 Ω. The step being examined is the geometry conversion — a data sheet gives you \(\rho\), and a circuit needs \(R\).

Worked example 3 — a fractional change

Given: Resistivity falls from \(5\times10^{-6}\) to \(4\times10^{-6}\) Ω·m.
  1. Conductivity is the reciprocal, so \(\sigma_{\text{old}}=1/(5\times10^{-6})\) and \(\sigma_{\text{new}}=1/(4\times10^{-6})\).
  2. Ratio: \(\sigma_{\text{new}}/\sigma_{\text{old}}=5/4=1.25\).
  3. So conductivity rises by 25%.
  4. Note the asymmetry: resistivity fell by 20%, but conductivity rose by 25%. Reciprocals do not give matching percentages.
What it means: That mismatch is deliberately examinable. A 20% fall in one is not a 20% rise in the other.

Where marks get lost

  • Assuming more carriers always means more conductivity. If the method of adding them also lowers mobility, \(\sigma\) may not move at all.
  • Getting the temperature direction backwards. Metals get worse when heated, semiconductors get better. Always give the reason in terms of \(n\) and \(\mu\).
  • Using resistivity where resistance is needed. Without \(L\) and \(A\) you cannot get from one to the other.
  • Matching percentage changes across a reciprocal. A 20% fall in resistivity is a 25% rise in conductivity.
  • Confusing \(\sigma\) for conductivity with \(\sigma\) for stress. Same symbol, unrelated meanings. State which one you mean if there is any doubt.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

In \(\sigma=nq\mu\), what does the mobility term \(\mu\) describe?

  1. How many charge carriers there are per cubic metre
  2. The charge carried by each individual carrier
  3. How freely a carrier moves before it scatters off something
  4. The cross-sectional area available for current to flow through
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Mobility measures how far a carrier gets before an obstruction deflects it — vibrating atoms, impurity atoms or other lattice defects. Option A is the carrier concentration \(n\) and option B is \(q\), which for an electron is \(1.6\times10^{-19}\) C. Option D is not in the equation at all: \(\sigma\) is a material property, so geometry never enters it. Why this matters: in a metal \(n\) is essentially fixed, so mobility is the only term a designer can influence, and every practical trick — purity, annealing, running cooler — works through \(\mu\).
Q2 · Multiple choiceOne step

A copper wire is replaced by one of the same copper but twice the length and twice the diameter. What happens to its electrical resistance?

  1. It is unchanged, because the material is the same
  2. It doubles
  3. It halves
  4. It becomes four times larger
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Use \(R=\rho L/A\). Doubling the length multiplies \(R\) by 2. Doubling the diameter multiplies the area by \(2^2=4\), because \(A=\pi d^2/4\), which divides \(R\) by 4. Net effect \(2/4=0.5\), so the resistance halves. Option A is the classic confusion: resistivity \(\rho\) is unchanged because the material is unchanged, but resistance belongs to the object and geometry has changed. Option D forgets that area scales with the square of diameter. Design meaning: the cheapest way to cut resistive loss in a cable is usually more copper cross-section, not a better material.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A Hall-effect measurement on a doped semiconductor returns a positive Hall coefficient \(R_H\). What does the sign tell you?

  1. The sample is a metal rather than a semiconductor
  2. The dominant carriers are positive holes rather than electrons
  3. The carrier concentration is unusually low
  4. The mobility is negative, so the doping has failed
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. With \(R_H\approx-1/(nq)\), a negative coefficient corresponds to negative carriers — electrons — and a positive coefficient to positive carriers, meaning holes (a hole is a missing electron in a semiconductor that behaves as a positive carrier). This sign information is the whole reason the measurement is worth doing: conductivity tells you only the product \(n\mu\) and cannot distinguish carrier type at all. Option C confuses sign with magnitude — the magnitude gives \(n\), the sign gives the type. Mobility is never negative, ruling out D. Design meaning: it is how you confirm that a wafer was doped p-type rather than n-type before committing it to a process.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

Which single change would genuinely raise the electrical conductivity of a copper conductor?

  1. Raising its operating temperature from 20 °C to 120 °C
  2. Alloying it with 2% tin to make it stronger
  3. Annealing a cold-worked sample to reduce its dislocation density
  4. Reducing its cross-sectional area by half
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Annealing does not remove impurity atoms, but it does reduce the dislocation density and internal strain left by cold work. With fewer lattice defects to scatter electrons, mobility \(\mu\) rises and with it \(\sigma=nq\mu\). Option A does the opposite: heating a metal makes the atoms vibrate harder, scattering increases, \(\mu\) falls and conductivity falls. Option B deliberately adds permanent scattering sites — solute atoms buy strength and pay in conductivity, which is why power cable is drawn from high-purity copper. Option D is the trap: halving the area doubles the resistance of that conductor but leaves conductivity, a material property, completely untouched. Design meaning: conductivity is bought with purity, low defect density and temperature control; resistance is bought with geometry. Do not reach for the wrong lever.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: Heating a semiconductor raises its resistivity, just as heating a metal does.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. The two classes move in opposite directions, and the reason lives in different terms of \(\sigma=nq\mu\). In a metal the carriers are already free, so \(n\) is fixed; heating only increases scattering, \(\mu\) falls, and resistivity rises roughly linearly. In a semiconductor, heating promotes many more electrons into the conducting state, so \(n\) rises exponentially. Mobility still falls, but the exponential rise in \(n\) overwhelms it, so conductivity rises sharply and resistivity falls. Design meaning: a resistance thermometer relies on the metallic behaviour being predictable and near-linear, while a thermistor exploits the far steeper semiconductor response.
Q6 · True/falseQuick

True or false: Two copper wires of different diameter have the same resistivity but different resistance.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Resistivity \(\rho\), in ohm-metres, is a property of the material — every piece of pure copper at the same temperature has the same value regardless of shape. Resistance \(R\), in ohms, is a property of a specific object and follows \(R=\rho L/A\), so the thinner wire has the larger resistance for the same length. Sanity check on the units: Ω·m × m ÷ m² leaves Ω, which is exactly what a multimeter reads. Design meaning: data sheets quote \(\rho\) because it is transferable between designs; a circuit calculation always needs the geometry conversion before the number means anything.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

A metal has conductivity \(\sigma=6.0\times10^{7}\) S/m and carrier mobility \(\mu=3.5\times10^{-3}\) m²/(V·s). Estimate the carrier concentration \(n\), and say whether the value is plausible for a metal.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: \(n\approx1.1\times10^{29}\) carriers per cubic metre.
1. Rearrange \(\sigma=nq\mu\) to give \(n=\sigma/(q\mu)\), with \(q=1.6\times10^{-19}\) C for an electron.
2. Denominator: \(q\mu=1.6\times10^{-19}\times3.5\times10^{-3}=5.6\times10^{-22}\).
3. Divide: \(n=6.0\times10^{7}/5.6\times10^{-22}=1.07\times10^{29}\) m⁻³.
4. Sanity check. A metal packs roughly \(10^{28}\)–\(10^{29}\) atoms per cubic metre — copper is about \(8.5\times10^{28}\) — so this result corresponds to roughly one free electron per atom. That is exactly what metallic bonding predicts, so the magnitude is credible. An answer near \(10^{22}\) would signal an arithmetic slip of several orders, or a semiconductor rather than a metal.
5. Design meaning. Because \(n\) is set by the bonding and cannot be increased, a metal offers no equivalent of doping. Every improvement in a metallic conductor has to come through mobility, which means purity, a well-annealed lattice and a cooler operating temperature.
Q8 · Diagram interpretationWritten

A figure plots resistivity on the vertical axis against temperature on the horizontal axis, with three curves. Curve 1 rises almost as a straight line across the whole range. Curve 2 begins far higher than Curve 1 and falls steeply, dropping by several orders of magnitude. Curve 3 is a dashed line running parallel to Curve 1 but sitting above it at every temperature. Identify what each curve represents, explain each using \(n\) and \(\mu\), and give one design consequence.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: Curve 1 is a pure metal, Curve 2 a semiconductor, Curve 3 the same metal with impurities or alloying additions.
Curve 1 — pure metal. Carrier concentration \(n\) is fixed because the electrons are already free and none can be added. Heating increases atomic vibration, carriers scatter more often, mobility \(\mu\) falls, and since \(\rho=1/(nq\mu)\) the resistivity rises. The near-linear shape reflects vibration amplitude growing steadily with temperature.
Curve 2 — semiconductor. It starts high because there are very few carriers at room temperature. Heating promotes electrons into the conducting state, so \(n\) rises exponentially. Mobility still falls, but exponential growth in \(n\) dominates a modest fall in \(\mu\), so resistivity collapses. The orders-of-magnitude drop is itself the identifying clue — no metal behaves like that.
Curve 3 — impure or alloyed metal. The parallel offset is the giveaway. Impurity atoms add a fixed population of scattering sites that does not depend on temperature, so mobility is lower at every temperature by a roughly constant amount. The temperature-dependent vibration scattering is unchanged, which is why the slope matches Curve 1 rather than steepening.
Trend check. All three are consistent with a single equation, \(\sigma=nq\mu\). Only the dominant term differs, so if you ever find yourself needing two contradictory rules, one of the readings is wrong.
Design meaning. Size a power cable for the hottest expected day, because its resistance is worst there. Size a semiconductor device for its hottest condition too, but for the opposite reason — falling resistivity can draw more current, heat it further and run away. And never substitute an alloyed conductor for a pure one on the assumption that "it is still copper": Curve 3 sits above Curve 1 permanently.
Q9 · Short calculationWritten

A Hall measurement on a conductor returns a Hall coefficient \(R_H=-7.4\times10^{-11}\) m³/C. Determine the carrier concentration, state what the sign tells you, and explain why this measurement is worth doing when conductivity is easier to measure.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: \(n\approx8.4\times10^{28}\) carriers per cubic metre, and the carriers are negative electrons.
1. From \(R_H\approx-1/(nq)\), rearrange for magnitude: \(n=1/(|R_H|\,q)\).
2. Denominator: \(|R_H|\,q=7.4\times10^{-11}\times1.6\times10^{-19}=1.184\times10^{-29}\) m³·C/C.
3. Invert: \(n=1/1.184\times10^{-29}=8.4\times10^{28}\) m⁻³.
4. The sign. \(R_H\) is negative, so the dominant carriers are electrons. A positive value would have indicated holes — positive carriers — as in a p-type semiconductor.
5. Sanity check. \(8.4\times10^{28}\) m⁻³ is about one carrier per atom for a typical metal, and it is very close to the accepted figure for copper, so both the magnitude and the negative sign are self-consistent with a metallic conductor.
6. Why bother. A conductivity measurement gives only the product \(n\mu\). If conductivity is disappointing you cannot tell whether too few carriers or too much scattering is responsible, and those two problems have opposite fixes. The Hall measurement separates them: it gives \(n\) directly, after which \(\mu=\sigma/(nq)\) follows. Design meaning: it turns "this material underperforms" into an actionable diagnosis — change the doping, or clean up the lattice.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A busbar in pure copper carries 200 A and currently dissipates 40 W per metre. Purchasing proposes an identical-section copper alloy that is stronger but has 15% lower conductivity. Quantify the effect on resistive heating and recommend a course of action.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: heating rises by about 18%, to roughly 47 W per metre. Restore the original conductance by increasing the cross-section by about 18%, or keep pure copper and solve the strength problem structurally.
1. Classify. A material property is being changed with the geometry held fixed, so this is \(\rho=1/\sigma\) followed by \(R=\rho L/A\), then ohmic heating \(P=I^2R\).
2. Resistivity change. Conductivity falls to \(0.85\sigma\), so resistivity rises to \(1/0.85=1.176\) times its original value — an 18% increase, not 15%. This asymmetry across the reciprocal is deliberately examinable.
3. Resistance change. With \(L\) and \(A\) unchanged, \(R\) rises by the same factor of 1.176.
4. Power. \(P=I^2R\) at fixed current, so \(P_{\text{new}}=1.176\times40=47.1\) W per metre. The current is set by the load, so the extra resistance shows up entirely as heat.
5. Why conductivity fell. The alloying atoms are foreign atoms in the lattice. They obstruct dislocations, which is where the strength comes from, and they obstruct conduction electrons, which lowers \(\mu\). One mechanism, two consequences — you cannot take the strength without the conductivity penalty.
6. Trend check. Extra heat raises the operating temperature, which raises metallic resistivity further, which produces still more heat. The 18% figure is therefore a floor, not the final answer, unless the thermal design is revisited.
7. Recommendation. If the alloy is genuinely needed, increase the cross-sectional area by roughly 18% so \(R=\rho L/A\) returns to its original value, and re-check clearances, supports and the temperature rise of adjacent insulation. The better first question, though, is why strength was wanted at all: extra brackets or a thicker section usually solve a mechanical problem far more cheaply than degrading the conductor that the whole component exists to be.
