Electrical Properties

Back to Week 1 bonding, now asking a different question. Metallic bonding left electrons free to roam, and this week is about what those free electrons do when you apply a voltage — plus why heating a metal and heating a semiconductor have opposite effects.

What this week is really asking
How freely can charge move through this material, and what changes that?

One equation carries most of the week: conductivity is carrier count times charge times mobility. Nearly every question is about which of those three terms a change affects, and whether two of them move in opposite directions.

Where this sits. This is card 8a of the core map, a branch off Week 1 bonding rather than a new foundation. Week 10 does the same for magnetism. Textbook sections: Callister Ch2 §2.2–2.3, Ch18 §18.1–18.17.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
The main equation
\(\sigma\)conductivityHow readily the material carries current. Units of siemens per metre. Note this is the same Greek letter used for stress — context tells you which.
\(n\)carrier concentrationHow many charge carriers there are per cubic metre.
\(q\)charge per carrierFor an electron, \(1.6\times10^{-19}\) coulombs.
\(\mu\)mobilityHow freely a carrier moves before scattering off something. Higher mobility means less obstruction.
Carrier—Anything transporting charge — usually an electron, sometimes a hole or an ion.
Hole—A missing electron in a semiconductor, which behaves like a positive carrier.
Resistivity versus resistance
\(\rho\)resistivityThe inverse of conductivity. A property of the material, independent of shape.
\(R\)resistanceA property of a specific object, so it depends on length and cross-section as well as the material.
Classes and effects
Conductor—A metal. Huge carrier concentration, and resistivity that rises with temperature.
Semiconductor—Few carriers at room temperature, but resistivity that falls sharply with temperature.
Doping—Adding controlled impurities to a semiconductor to increase carrier concentration.
Scattering—Carriers bouncing off vibrating atoms or impurities. More scattering means lower mobility.

The ideas, one card at a time

1 The conductivity equation

Three factors multiplied together. How many carriers there are, how much charge each carries, and how freely each moves.

\[\sigma=nq\mu\]

\(\sigma\) is conductivity, \(n\) the carrier concentration per cubic metre, \(q\) the charge per carrier and \(\mu\) the mobility.

Because it is a product, any change that raises one term while lowering another may leave conductivity almost unmoved. This is the source of most exam questions in this week, and the reason a "more must be better" instinct fails here.

Metals have an enormous \(n\) that barely changes — the free electrons are already there and no more can be added. Semiconductors have a small \(n\) that can be changed by orders of magnitude through doping or heating, which is exactly what makes them useful.

Feeds: the temperature and doping behaviour below, and any question about why a processing change did not deliver what was expected.
Say it cold: "Conductivity is carrier count times charge times mobility, so raising one term while cutting another can leave it unchanged."

2 Resistivity versus resistance

One belongs to the material and one belongs to the object. Confusing them makes a calculation impossible to complete.

\[\rho=\frac{1}{\sigma}\qquad R=\frac{\rho L}{A}\]

\(\rho\) is resistivity in ohm-metres, \(L\) the length of the object and \(A\) its cross-sectional area.

Resistivity is a material property — every piece of pure copper has the same value regardless of shape. Resistance depends on geometry, so a long thin copper wire and a short fat one have very different resistances despite identical resistivity.

When a question gives you a material property and asks for a circuit quantity, the geometry conversion is the step being tested. Data sheets quote \(\rho\); multimeters measure \(R\).

Feeds: any calculation linking a materials choice to circuit behaviour or resistive heating.
Say it cold: "Resistivity belongs to the material; resistance belongs to the object and needs its length and area."

3 Temperature — opposite directions

Heat a metal and it conducts worse. Heat a semiconductor and it conducts better. Same equation, different dominant term.

  • Metal: \(n\) is fixed — the electrons are already free. Heating makes the atoms vibrate harder, so carriers scatter more and \(\mu\) falls. Conductivity falls, and resistivity rises roughly linearly.
  • Semiconductor: heating promotes many more electrons into the conducting state, so \(n\) rises exponentially. Mobility still falls, but the rise in \(n\) overwhelms it. Conductivity rises sharply.

The graph of resistivity against temperature is a favourite exam figure precisely because the two lines slope opposite ways. If you can say which is which and give the reason in terms of \(n\) and \(\mu\), you have the whole answer.

Adding impurities to a metal also lowers conductivity, for the same scattering reason. This is why pure copper is used for wiring and alloyed copper is not — alloying buys strength and pays in conductivity.

Feeds: the reason resistance thermometers work at all, and why power cables are sized for hot-weather conditions.
Say it cold: "Metals lose conductivity when heated because mobility falls; semiconductors gain it because carrier count rises exponentially."

4 The Hall effect

Put a current-carrying sample in a magnetic field and the carriers get pushed sideways, piling up on one face until the resulting voltage stops any further pile-up.

\[R_H=\frac{E_H}{JB}\approx-\frac{1}{nq}\]

\(R_H\) is the Hall coefficient, \(E_H\) the sideways electric field, \(J\) the current density and \(B\) the magnetic field.

The measurement is valuable for two reasons. It gives you \(n\), the carrier concentration, which is otherwise hard to reach. And its sign tells you whether the carriers are negative electrons or positive holes — something no conductivity measurement can reveal.

The physical picture is a balance. The magnetic force pushes carriers to one side; charge accumulates there; the resulting electric field pushes back; equilibrium is reached when the two forces cancel, and the voltage at that point is what you measure.

Practically this is everywhere — Hall sensors measure wheel speed, crankshaft position and current without any physical contact.

Feeds: sensor selection, and any semiconductor characterisation question.
Say it cold: "A magnetic field pushes carriers sideways until the voltage balances it, and that voltage gives carrier concentration and sign."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — why doping disappointed

Given: Doping doubles \(n\), but the measured conductivity barely changes.
  1. Start from \(\sigma=nq\mu\).
  2. Doubling \(n\) alone would double \(\sigma\).
  3. But the dopant atoms are foreign atoms in the lattice, and they scatter carriers.
  4. That extra scattering lowers \(\mu\), roughly halving it here.
  5. The product \(n\mu\) is therefore almost unchanged, so \(\sigma\) barely moves.
What it means: This is the classic Week 9 question. The instinct that more carriers must mean more conductivity ignores that the way you added them also obstructs them.

Worked example 2 — resistivity to resistance

Given: \(\rho=2\times10^{-6}\) Ω·m, \(L=0.4\) m, \(A=2\times10^{-6}\) m².
  1. \(R=\rho L/A\).
  2. Numerator: \(2\times10^{-6}\times0.4=8\times10^{-7}\).
  3. Divide by area: \(8\times10^{-7}/2\times10^{-6}=0.4\) Ω.
  4. Check the units: Ω·m × m ÷ m² leaves Ω, as it must.
What it means: 0.4 Ω. The step being examined is the geometry conversion — a data sheet gives you \(\rho\), and a circuit needs \(R\).

Worked example 3 — a fractional change

Given: Resistivity falls from \(5\times10^{-6}\) to \(4\times10^{-6}\) Ω·m.
  1. Conductivity is the reciprocal, so \(\sigma_{\text{old}}=1/(5\times10^{-6})\) and \(\sigma_{\text{new}}=1/(4\times10^{-6})\).
  2. Ratio: \(\sigma_{\text{new}}/\sigma_{\text{old}}=5/4=1.25\).
  3. So conductivity rises by 25%.
  4. Note the asymmetry: resistivity fell by 20%, but conductivity rose by 25%. Reciprocals do not give matching percentages.
What it means: That mismatch is deliberately examinable. A 20% fall in one is not a 20% rise in the other.

Where marks get lost

  • Assuming more carriers always means more conductivity. If the method of adding them also lowers mobility, \(\sigma\) may not move at all.
  • Getting the temperature direction backwards. Metals get worse when heated, semiconductors get better. Always give the reason in terms of \(n\) and \(\mu\).
  • Using resistivity where resistance is needed. Without \(L\) and \(A\) you cannot get from one to the other.
  • Matching percentage changes across a reciprocal. A 20% fall in resistivity is a 25% rise in conductivity.
  • Confusing \(\sigma\) for conductivity with \(\sigma\) for stress. Same symbol, unrelated meanings. State which one you mean if there is any doubt.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open. An answer you can only recognise is not an answer you can write in an exam.

1. Why can increased doping fail to improve conductivity as much as expected?
Because \(\sigma=nq\mu\) is a product. Doping raises \(n\), but the dopant atoms are lattice irregularities that scatter carriers and reduce \(\mu\). If mobility falls by roughly the factor that carrier concentration rises, the product barely changes and conductivity stays almost flat.
2. Resistivity falls from \(5\times10^{-6}\) to \(4\times10^{-6}\) Ω·m. By what fraction does conductivity change?
Conductivity is \(1/\rho\), so the ratio is \((1/4)/(1/5)=5/4=1.25\) — a 25% increase. Note that resistivity fell by 20% while conductivity rose by 25%; reciprocal changes do not give equal percentages.
3. What does the Hall coefficient mainly tell you?
Carrier concentration \(n\), through \(R_H\approx-1/(nq)\). Its sign also reveals whether the dominant carriers are negative electrons or positive holes, which is information no ordinary conductivity measurement can provide.
4. Why does heating a metal reduce conductivity while heating a semiconductor increases it?
In a metal, \(n\) is fixed because the electrons are already free; heating increases atomic vibration and therefore scattering, so \(\mu\) falls and conductivity falls with it. In a semiconductor, heating promotes many more carriers into conduction, so \(n\) rises exponentially — enough to overwhelm the mobility drop and raise conductivity sharply.
5. A copper conductor is alloyed to make it stronger and its conductivity drops. Why?
The alloying atoms are foreign atoms disrupting the regular lattice, and they scatter conduction electrons, reducing mobility \(\mu\). Carrier concentration is essentially unchanged, so \(\sigma=nq\mu\) falls. This is the same obstruction that provides solid-solution strengthening in Week 3 — one mechanism, blocking dislocations and electrons alike.
