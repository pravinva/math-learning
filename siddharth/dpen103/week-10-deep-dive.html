Magnetic Properties

What this week is really asking
What shape is the loop, and what does that shape make the material good for?

Almost every magnetics question in this subject reduces to reading a loop. A thin loop means low losses and a transformer core. A fat loop means it holds its magnetism and makes a permanent magnet. The rest is detail.

Where this sits. This is card 8b of the core map, the twin of Week 9 — both are functional properties branching off Week 1 bonding. There is a worked hysteresis diagram on the reading the pictures page. Textbook sections: Callister Ch20 §20.1–20.12.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
The two axes
\(H\)applied magnetic field strengthThe field you apply from outside, usually by running current through a coil. This is the horizontal axis — the cause.
\(B\)magnetic flux densityThe magnetism you get out of the material as a result. The vertical axis — the effect.
\(M\)magnetisationThe material's own contribution, over and above the field you applied.
\(\chi\)susceptibilityHow strongly the material responds to an applied field.
\(\mu_0\)permeability of free spaceA constant, \(4\pi\times10^{-7}\) H/m.
Features of the loop
Hysteresis—The lag that makes the return path differ from the outward path. It is why the graph is a loop rather than a line.
Saturation—The point where every domain is aligned and more applied field produces no more magnetisation.
Remanence, \(B_r\)—The magnetism still left when the applied field is switched off. Where the loop crosses the vertical axis.
Coercivity, \(H_c\)—How hard you must push in reverse to wipe that leftover magnetism out. Where the loop crosses the horizontal axis.
Loop area—Energy lost as heat per cycle, per unit volume. Fat loop, big losses.
Classes
Domain—A small region where all the atomic magnets already point the same way. Magnetising a material is mostly about growing the favourably aligned domains.
Ferromagnetic—Strongly magnetic and able to stay magnetised. Iron, nickel, cobalt.
Soft magnetic—Thin loop, low coercivity, easy to magnetise and demagnetise. For transformer and motor cores.
Hard magnetic—Fat loop, high coercivity and remanence. For permanent magnets.
Eddy currents—Circulating currents induced in a conducting core by a changing field. A second loss mechanism on top of hysteresis, and the reason cores are laminated.

The ideas, one card at a time

1 Reading the loop

Start at the origin with an unmagnetised sample, apply a field, then reverse it. The path does not retrace itself, and every feature of the resulting loop has a name and a use.

Trace it in order
  • Raise \(H\) from zero and \(B\) climbs steeply as domains aligned with the field grow at the expense of the others.
  • Keep going and it flattens at saturation — every domain is aligned and there is nothing left to recruit.
  • Now reduce \(H\) back to zero. \(B\) does not return to zero. What remains is remanence, \(B_r\).
  • Push \(H\) negative until \(B\) finally reaches zero. That reverse field is the coercivity, \(H_c\).
  • Continue and the loop completes symmetrically in the negative direction.
\[M=\chi H\qquad B=\mu_0(H+M)\]

Magnetisation is the response to the applied field; flux density combines the applied field with the material response.

The reason it lags is that domain walls have to move through a real, imperfect crystal, and they get pinned on defects and grain boundaries. Energy is spent breaking them loose, and that energy is not recovered — it becomes heat.

Feeds: the soft-versus-hard decision below, which is the whole engineering content of the week.
Say it cold: "Saturation is where it maxes out, remanence is what stays at zero field, and coercivity is the reverse field needed to clear it."

2 Loop area is wasted energy

The area enclosed by the loop is the energy lost as heat every time the material goes round it once. In anything running on alternating current, that happens fifty times a second or more.

Each cycle, energy goes into dragging domain walls past pinning sites, and it does not come back out. The area of the loop measures exactly how much per cubic metre per cycle.

Multiply by frequency and volume and you have a heating rate. A mains transformer cycles 50 times a second, so even a modest loop area becomes a continuous thermal load that has to be removed — and at higher frequencies it gets much worse.

Eddy currents are the other half of core loss. A changing field induces circulating currents in the conducting core, and those dissipate power too. The fix is to laminate the core into thin insulated sheets, which breaks up the circulating paths. It is a geometry fix rather than a material one, but it belongs in any complete answer about core losses.

Feeds: transformer and motor design, and the thermal side of Week 8 selection for electrical machines.
Say it cold: "Loop area is energy lost as heat per cycle, so at mains frequency a fat loop is a continuous heating problem."

3 Soft versus hard — the whole decision

Two shapes of loop, two entirely different jobs. Naming which one a given application needs, and saying why, is most of the marks in this week.

Soft magneticHard magnetic
Loop shapeThin and tallWide and full
Coercivity \(H_c\)LowHigh
Remanence \(B_r\)Can be highHigh
Loss per cycleSmallLarge
Used forTransformer and motor coresPermanent magnets, magnetic storage
Wanted becauseIt must reverse constantly, cheaplyIt must refuse to be demagnetised

The logic runs from the duty, not from the material. Ask first whether this material is going to be reversed many times a second or is meant to sit there holding a field. Reversing constantly means you need a thin loop. Holding a field means you need a fat one.

A material optimised for one is necessarily poor at the other, and this is not a manufacturing shortcoming — the requirements genuinely conflict. High coercivity means hard to demagnetise, which is precisely what a transformer core must not be.

Feeds: Week 13, where a combined question typically asks for low loss plus some mechanical or manufacturing requirement.
Say it cold: "Reversed constantly means soft and thin-looped; meant to hold its field means hard and fat-looped, and no material does both."

4 Judging a permanent magnet properly

High remanence alone does not make a good permanent magnet. Without the coercivity to defend it, that remanence will not survive contact with the real world.

Remanence tells you how much magnetism the material holds at zero applied field. Coercivity tells you how much abuse it takes to remove it. A magnet with high \(B_r\) and low \(H_c\) starts strong and is easily wiped by a stray field, a knock, or heat.

So a good permanent magnet needs both, and the combined figure of merit used in practice is the maximum energy product \((BH)_{\max}\), essentially the biggest rectangle that fits under the second-quadrant part of the curve. It rewards having both at once rather than either alone.

A useful exam instinct: if you are offered high \(B_r\) and told nothing about \(H_c\), the question is almost certainly testing whether you notice the omission.

Feeds: motor and sensor design, and it is a favourite short-answer question.
Say it cold: "High remanence with low coercivity is a bad permanent magnet, because it starts strong and is trivially wiped."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — choosing between two core materials

Given: Materials A and B have similar saturation, but A has a 35% smaller loop area. The application is a mains transformer core.
  1. Loop area is energy lost per cycle per unit volume, so A loses 35% less energy each cycle.
  2. A mains transformer cycles 50 times per second, continuously, for years.
  3. So the loss difference is not a one-off — it is 35% less continuous heat generation and 35% less wasted energy on the bill.
  4. Less heat also means a smaller cooling requirement, or the option of running a more compact core at the same temperature.
  5. Choose A.
What it means: Note how the answer converts loop area into something a client cares about: running cost, heat and size. That translation is what turns a correct answer into a complete one.

Worked example 2 — a tempting but bad permanent magnet

Given: A material has very high remanence \(B_r\) but low coercivity \(H_c\). Is it a good permanent magnet?
  1. High \(B_r\) means it holds a lot of magnetism once the field is removed, which looks promising.
  2. But low \(H_c\) means only a small reverse field is needed to destroy that magnetisation.
  3. In service it will meet stray fields from nearby currents, mechanical shock and elevated temperature — all of which demagnetise it.
  4. So no. It starts strong and does not stay strong, which is the opposite of what "permanent" requires.
What it means: A permanent magnet needs both properties. High remanence is the headline number and coercivity is what protects it.

Worked example 3 — why the frequency changes the answer

A core material is acceptable at 50 Hz. The design moves to 20 kHz. What happens?

  1. Hysteresis loss per cycle is fixed by loop area, but the number of cycles per second has risen by a factor of 400.
  2. So hysteresis heating scales up roughly in proportion to frequency.
  3. Eddy-current losses grow faster still, rising roughly with the square of frequency.
  4. A material and lamination scheme that was comfortable at mains frequency can overheat badly at 20 kHz, which is why high-frequency designs use ferrites — magnetically soft but electrically insulating, so eddy currents are largely eliminated.
What it means: Frequency is part of the specification, not a detail. "Low loss" only means something once you say at what frequency.

Where marks get lost

  • Confusing remanence with coercivity. Remanence is the vertical-axis crossing — what is left. Coercivity is the horizontal-axis crossing — what it takes to remove it.
  • Calling high remanence sufficient for a permanent magnet. Without coercivity it demagnetises at the first stray field.
  • Forgetting that loop area is per cycle. The total loss is area times frequency times volume, and frequency is often the dominant term.
  • Ignoring eddy currents. Core loss has two parts. A complete answer mentions lamination as well as loop shape.
  • Recommending a hard magnetic material for an AC core. It has to reverse every cycle, and a fat loop makes that ruinously lossy.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open. An answer you can only recognise is not an answer you can write in an exam.

1. Why is loop area a practical thermal design concern in magnetic cores?
The area enclosed by the B–H loop is energy lost as heat per cycle per unit volume. In AC service the material traverses the loop continuously — 50 times a second at mains — so the loss becomes a steady heating rate equal to area times frequency times volume. That heat must be removed, and it sets the cooling design and often the size of the core.
2. A material has very high \(B_r\) but low \(H_c\). Is it automatically a good permanent magnet?
No. High remanence means it retains a lot of magnetism at zero applied field, but low coercivity means only a small reverse field, mechanical shock or temperature rise is needed to wipe it. A permanent magnet needs both, which is why \((BH)_{\max}\) rather than \(B_r\) alone is the figure of merit.
3. Why do AC devices avoid hard magnetic materials?
Because the core must reverse its magnetisation every cycle. Hard materials have high coercivity, so each reversal requires a large driving field, and their wide loop means a large area — meaning large energy loss per cycle. At 50 Hz that becomes continuous, substantial heating. Soft materials with thin loops reverse cheaply.
4. Trace the B–H loop from an unmagnetised sample and name each feature.
From the origin, \(B\) rises steeply as favourably aligned domains grow, then flattens at saturation where all domains are aligned. Reducing \(H\) to zero leaves remanence \(B_r\) on the vertical axis. Applying reverse field until \(B\) reaches zero gives coercivity \(H_c\) on the horizontal axis. Continuing completes the loop symmetrically. The lag exists because domain walls pin on defects, and freeing them costs energy that becomes heat.
5. A transformer core design moves from 50 Hz to 20 kHz. Why might the same material now fail?
Hysteresis loss per cycle is unchanged, but cycles occur 400 times more often, so hysteresis heating scales roughly with frequency. Eddy-current losses rise roughly with frequency squared, so they grow far faster still. The core can overheat despite an unchanged loop, which is why high-frequency designs use ferrites — soft magnetically but electrically insulating, so eddy currents are suppressed.
