What this week is really asking
What shape is the loop, and what does that shape make the material good for?

Almost every magnetics question in this subject reduces to reading a loop. A thin loop means low losses and a transformer core. A fat loop means it holds its magnetism and makes a permanent magnet. The rest is detail.

Where this sits. This is card 8b of the core map, the twin of Week 9 — both are functional properties branching off Week 1 bonding. There is a worked hysteresis diagram on the reading the pictures page. Textbook sections: Callister Ch20 §20.1–20.12.

Working through the week

Read this section first if the topic is new. It builds the ideas in order. Everything below — the glossary, cards, worked examples and exam practice — stays as your summary and drill.

Magnetism from aligned moments

Atomic magnetic moments align in ferromagnetic materials (iron, nickel, cobalt) below the Curie temperature. Paramagnetic materials weakly follow a field; diamagnetic materials weakly oppose it. Engineering use is almost always ferromagnetic or ferrite-based.

The B–H loop tells the application

Magnetise and demagnetise a ferromagnetic sample and plot flux density \(B\) against field \(H\). The loop's area is energy lost per cycle — hysteresis loss. Narrow loops suit transformer cores (frequent reversal, low loss). Wide loops suit permanent magnets (high remanence, high coercivity).

Soft vs hard magnetic materials

Soft magnetic: easy to magnetise and demagnetise, low coercivity, used where the field reverses often. Hard magnetic: high coercivity and remanence, used where the magnet must stay magnetised. Read the loop shape; it predicts the job.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
The two axes
\(H\)applied magnetic field strengthThe field you apply from outside, usually by running current through a coil. This is the horizontal axis — the cause.
\(B\)magnetic flux densityThe magnetism you get out of the material as a result. The vertical axis — the effect.
\(M\)magnetisationThe material's own contribution, over and above the field you applied.
\(\chi\)susceptibilityHow strongly the material responds to an applied field.
\(\mu_0\)permeability of free spaceA constant, \(4\pi\times10^{-7}\) H/m.
Features of the loop
Hysteresis—The lag that makes the return path differ from the outward path. It is why the graph is a loop rather than a line.
Saturation—The point where every domain is aligned and more applied field produces no more magnetisation.
Remanence, \(B_r\)—The magnetism still left when the applied field is switched off. Where the loop crosses the vertical axis.
Coercivity, \(H_c\)—How hard you must push in reverse to wipe that leftover magnetism out. Where the loop crosses the horizontal axis.
Loop area—Energy lost as heat per cycle, per unit volume. Fat loop, big losses.
Classes
Domain—A small region where all the atomic magnets already point the same way. Magnetising a material is mostly about growing the favourably aligned domains.
Ferromagnetic—Strongly magnetic and able to stay magnetised. Iron, nickel, cobalt.
Soft magnetic—Thin loop, low coercivity, easy to magnetise and demagnetise. For transformer and motor cores.
Hard magnetic—Fat loop, high coercivity and remanence. For permanent magnets.
Eddy currents—Circulating currents induced in a conducting core by a changing field. A second loss mechanism on top of hysteresis, and the reason cores are laminated.

The ideas, one card at a time

1 Reading the loop

Start at the origin with an unmagnetised sample, apply a field, then reverse it. The path does not retrace itself, and every feature of the resulting loop has a name and a use.

Trace it in order
  • Raise \(H\) from zero and \(B\) climbs steeply as domains aligned with the field grow at the expense of the others.
  • Keep going and it flattens at saturation — every domain is aligned and there is nothing left to recruit.
  • Now reduce \(H\) back to zero. \(B\) does not return to zero. What remains is remanence, \(B_r\).
  • Push \(H\) negative until \(B\) finally reaches zero. That reverse field is the coercivity, \(H_c\).
  • Continue and the loop completes symmetrically in the negative direction.
\[M=\chi H\qquad B=\mu_0(H+M)\]

Magnetisation is the response to the applied field; flux density combines the applied field with the material response.

The reason it lags is that domain walls have to move through a real, imperfect crystal, and they get pinned on defects and grain boundaries. Energy is spent breaking them loose, and that energy is not recovered — it becomes heat.

Feeds: the soft-versus-hard decision below, which is the whole engineering content of the week.
Say it cold: "Saturation is where it maxes out, remanence is what stays at zero field, and coercivity is the reverse field needed to clear it."

2 Loop area is wasted energy

The area enclosed by the loop is the energy lost as heat every time the material goes round it once. In anything running on alternating current, that happens fifty times a second or more.

Each cycle, energy goes into dragging domain walls past pinning sites, and it does not come back out. The area of the loop measures exactly how much per cubic metre per cycle.

Multiply by frequency and volume and you have a heating rate. A mains transformer cycles 50 times a second, so even a modest loop area becomes a continuous thermal load that has to be removed — and at higher frequencies it gets much worse.

Eddy currents are the other half of core loss. A changing field induces circulating currents in the conducting core, and those dissipate power too. The fix is to laminate the core into thin insulated sheets, which breaks up the circulating paths. It is a geometry fix rather than a material one, but it belongs in any complete answer about core losses.

Feeds: transformer and motor design, and the thermal side of Week 8 selection for electrical machines.
Say it cold: "Loop area is energy lost as heat per cycle, so at mains frequency a fat loop is a continuous heating problem."

3 Soft versus hard — the whole decision

Two shapes of loop, two entirely different jobs. Naming which one a given application needs, and saying why, is most of the marks in this week.

Soft magneticHard magnetic
Loop shapeThin and tallWide and full
Coercivity \(H_c\)LowHigh
Remanence \(B_r\)Can be highHigh
Loss per cycleSmallLarge
Used forTransformer and motor coresPermanent magnets, magnetic storage
Wanted becauseIt must reverse constantly, cheaplyIt must refuse to be demagnetised

The logic runs from the duty, not from the material. Ask first whether this material is going to be reversed many times a second or is meant to sit there holding a field. Reversing constantly means you need a thin loop. Holding a field means you need a fat one.

A material optimised for one is necessarily poor at the other, and this is not a manufacturing shortcoming — the requirements genuinely conflict. High coercivity means hard to demagnetise, which is precisely what a transformer core must not be.

Feeds: Week 13, where a combined question typically asks for low loss plus some mechanical or manufacturing requirement.
Say it cold: "Reversed constantly means soft and thin-looped; meant to hold its field means hard and fat-looped, and no material does both."

4 Judging a permanent magnet properly

High remanence alone does not make a good permanent magnet. Without the coercivity to defend it, that remanence will not survive contact with the real world.

Remanence tells you how much magnetism the material holds at zero applied field. Coercivity tells you how much abuse it takes to remove it. A magnet with high \(B_r\) and low \(H_c\) starts strong and is easily wiped by a stray field, a knock, or heat.

So a good permanent magnet needs both, and the combined figure of merit used in practice is the maximum energy product \((BH)_{\max}\), essentially the biggest rectangle that fits under the second-quadrant part of the curve. It rewards having both at once rather than either alone.

A useful exam instinct: if you are offered high \(B_r\) and told nothing about \(H_c\), the question is almost certainly testing whether you notice the omission.

Feeds: motor and sensor design, and it is a favourite short-answer question.
Say it cold: "High remanence with low coercivity is a bad permanent magnet, because it starts strong and is trivially wiped."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — choosing between two core materials

Given: Materials A and B have similar saturation, but A has a 35% smaller loop area. The application is a mains transformer core.
  1. Loop area is energy lost per cycle per unit volume, so A loses 35% less energy each cycle.
  2. A mains transformer cycles 50 times per second, continuously, for years.
  3. So the loss difference is not a one-off — it is 35% less continuous heat generation and 35% less wasted energy on the bill.
  4. Less heat also means a smaller cooling requirement, or the option of running a more compact core at the same temperature.
  5. Choose A.
What it means: Note how the answer converts loop area into something a client cares about: running cost, heat and size. That translation is what turns a correct answer into a complete one.

Worked example 2 — a tempting but bad permanent magnet

Given: A material has very high remanence \(B_r\) but low coercivity \(H_c\). Is it a good permanent magnet?
  1. High \(B_r\) means it holds a lot of magnetism once the field is removed, which looks promising.
  2. But low \(H_c\) means only a small reverse field is needed to destroy that magnetisation.
  3. In service it will meet stray fields from nearby currents, mechanical shock and elevated temperature — all of which demagnetise it.
  4. So no. It starts strong and does not stay strong, which is the opposite of what "permanent" requires.
What it means: A permanent magnet needs both properties. High remanence is the headline number and coercivity is what protects it.

Worked example 3 — why the frequency changes the answer

A core material is acceptable at 50 Hz. The design moves to 20 kHz. What happens?

  1. Hysteresis loss per cycle is fixed by loop area, but the number of cycles per second has risen by a factor of 400.
  2. So hysteresis heating scales up roughly in proportion to frequency.
  3. Eddy-current losses grow faster still, rising roughly with the square of frequency.
  4. A material and lamination scheme that was comfortable at mains frequency can overheat badly at 20 kHz, which is why high-frequency designs use ferrites — magnetically soft but electrically insulating, so eddy currents are largely eliminated.
What it means: Frequency is part of the specification, not a detail. "Low loss" only means something once you say at what frequency.

Where marks get lost

  • Confusing remanence with coercivity. Remanence is the vertical-axis crossing — what is left. Coercivity is the horizontal-axis crossing — what it takes to remove it.
  • Calling high remanence sufficient for a permanent magnet. Without coercivity it demagnetises at the first stray field.
  • Forgetting that loop area is per cycle. The total loss is area times frequency times volume, and frequency is often the dominant term.
  • Ignoring eddy currents. Core loss has two parts. A complete answer mentions lamination as well as loop shape.
  • Recommending a hard magnetic material for an AC core. It has to reverse every cycle, and a fat loop makes that ruinously lossy.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

On a B–H loop, where \(H\) is the applied field strength and \(B\) the resulting flux density, where do you read the remanence \(B_r\)?

  1. Where the loop crosses the vertical axis, at \(H=0\)
  2. Where the loop crosses the horizontal axis, at \(B=0\)
  3. At the flat top of the loop, where it stops climbing
  4. From the total area enclosed by the loop
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. Remanence is what remains when the applied field is switched off, so it is the flux density at \(H=0\) — the vertical-axis intercept. Option B is the coercivity \(H_c\), the reverse field needed to drive \(B\) back to zero. Option C is saturation, where every domain is aligned and further field produces no more magnetisation. Option D is energy lost per cycle per unit volume. Design meaning: these four readings answer four different questions, so mislabelling one axis crossing for the other reverses the recommendation you make about the material.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

What does the area enclosed by a hysteresis loop represent physically?

  1. The maximum flux density the material can reach
  2. The energy dissipated as heat per cycle, per unit volume of material
  3. The stiffness of the material under magnetic load
  4. The total energy stored in the magnet, recoverable on demagnetising
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Going once round the loop requires energy to drag domain walls past pinning sites at defects and grain boundaries, and that energy is not recovered — it becomes heat. The enclosed area measures exactly how much, per cubic metre per cycle. Option D is the tempting wrong answer: the loss is dissipative, not stored, which is precisely why the outward and return paths differ and the graph is a loop rather than a line. Design meaning: loop area is a thermal specification in disguise, and in AC (alternating current) service it converts into a continuous heating rate.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A core material has a hysteresis loop area of 300 J/m³ per cycle. The core volume is \(2.0\times10^{-3}\) m³ and it operates at 50 Hz. What is the hysteresis power loss?

  1. 0.6 W
  2. 6 W
  3. 30 W
  4. 600 W
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Loss = area × volume × frequency = \(300\text{ J/m³} \times 2.0\times10^{-3}\text{ m³} \times 50\text{ s}^{-1}=30\) W. Unit check: J/m³ × m³ × 1/s = J/s = W, which confirms the grouping is right. Sanity check: the energy lost in one cycle is only 0.6 J, but at 50 cycles per second that becomes 30 W of continuous heat, so option A is the answer you get by forgetting frequency entirely — a very common slip. Design meaning: 30 W inside a sealed enclosure has to be removed, and this figure excludes eddy-current loss, so the real core loss is higher still.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

A design that worked at 50 Hz is moving to 400 Hz. Core losses are now unacceptable. Which change addresses the dominant new problem?

  1. Switch to a hard magnetic material with higher coercivity
  2. Use thicker laminations to reduce the number of insulated interfaces
  3. Use thinner laminations, or a ferrite, to suppress eddy currents
  4. Select for higher remanence so less applied field is needed
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Hysteresis loss scales roughly with frequency, so an eightfold frequency increase raises it eightfold. Eddy-current loss scales roughly with the square of frequency, so it rises about 64-fold and becomes the dominant term. Eddy currents are circulating currents induced in the conducting core, so the fixes are geometric — thinner insulated laminations break up the circulating paths — or material-based, using a ferrite that is magnetically soft but electrically insulating. Option B makes it worse by enlarging the loops available for circulating current. Option A is the opposite of what an AC core needs, since a wide loop must be traversed every cycle. Option D confuses the permanent-magnet figures of merit with the AC loss problem. Design meaning: frequency is part of the specification, and "low loss" is meaningless until you say at what frequency.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: Coercivity is read where the hysteresis loop crosses the vertical axis.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. That crossing is the remanence \(B_r\) — the flux density remaining at zero applied field. Coercivity \(H_c\) is the horizontal-axis crossing: the reverse field strength, in amperes per metre, needed to drive \(B\) back to zero. The units settle it immediately, since coercivity is a field strength and cannot be read from an axis measured in tesla. Design meaning: remanence says how much magnetism is left, coercivity says how hard it is to remove. A permanent magnet needs both, and confusing them is the fastest way to recommend a material that starts strong and is wiped by the first stray field.
Q6 · True/falseOne step

True or false: Laminating a core reduces eddy-current loss but leaves hysteresis loss per cycle essentially unchanged.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Core loss has two separate mechanisms. Eddy currents are induced circulating currents in the conducting core, and their magnitude depends on the size of the conducting loop available — so slicing the core into thin insulated sheets breaks those paths and cuts the loss substantially. Hysteresis loss comes from domain walls pinning on defects, which is a property of the material's microstructure, not of the sheet thickness, so lamination barely touches it. Design meaning: a complete answer about core losses always names both mechanisms, because they need different fixes — lamination or higher resistivity for eddy currents, a magnetically softer material for hysteresis. Fixing one and declaring victory is how designs overheat.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

A ferromagnetic core is placed in an applied field \(H=800\) A/m and has magnetic susceptibility \(\chi=1200\). Using \(M=\chi H\) and \(B=\mu_0(H+M)\) with \(\mu_0=4\pi\times10^{-7}\) H/m, find the magnetisation and the flux density, then comment on which term dominates.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: \(M=9.6\times10^{5}\) A/m and \(B\approx1.21\) T.
1. Magnetisation. \(M=\chi H=1200\times800=9.6\times10^{5}\) A/m. Susceptibility is dimensionless, so \(M\) carries the same units as \(H\).
2. Sum inside the bracket. \(H+M=800+960{,}000=960{,}800\) A/m.
3. Flux density. \(B=4\pi\times10^{-7}\times960{,}800=1.2566\times10^{-6}\times9.608\times10^{5}=1.21\) T.
4. Which term dominates. The applied field contributes 800 of 960,800 A/m — under 0.1%. Essentially all of the flux density comes from the material's own response, which is what "ferromagnetic" means: the material amplifies a modest applied field enormously.
5. Sanity check. Practical ferromagnetic materials saturate somewhere around 1.5–2 T, so 1.21 T is plausible but not far below the ceiling. Had the arithmetic produced 12 T you would know something was wrong, because no material reaches that. This also flags the limit of the calculation: \(M=\chi H\) treats susceptibility as constant, which stops being true as saturation approaches, so pushing \(H\) up by another factor of ten would not give 12 T in reality.
6. Design meaning. Working close to saturation means further increases in coil current buy almost no extra flux while still costing resistive heating in the winding. The useful operating point sits below the knee of the curve, and this calculation is how you check you are on the right side of it.
Q8 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Two hysteresis loops are drawn on the same axes. Loop A is tall and very narrow, crossing the horizontal axis at \(H_c=50\) A/m, and reaching \(B_r=1.2\) T. Loop B is broad and full, crossing at \(H_c=800\) kA/m, and also reaching \(B_r=1.2\) T. Assign each loop to a transformer core and a motor permanent magnet, and explain why the identical remanence does not decide the question.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: Loop A is the transformer core (soft magnetic); Loop B is the permanent magnet (hard magnetic).
Reading Loop A. A coercivity of 50 A/m is tiny, so the material reverses under almost no driving field, and the narrow shape means a small enclosed area — little energy lost per cycle. That is exactly the transformer requirement, because the core reverses 50 times a second continuously and every joule of loop area becomes heat.
Reading Loop B. A coercivity of 800 kA/m is larger by a factor of 16,000. That means the magnetisation is extremely difficult to remove, which is the defining requirement for a permanent magnet in a motor, where it meets stray fields from the windings, mechanical shock and elevated temperature. Its large enclosed area would be ruinous in AC service, but the magnet is not being cycled — it sits there holding a field, so loop area costs nothing.
Why equal remanence does not decide it. Remanence only says how much magnetism survives at zero applied field. It says nothing about how easily that magnetism can be destroyed, and that is what separates these two materials. Loop A would lose its magnetisation to the slightest reverse field; Loop B would not. For a permanent magnet the practical figure of merit is the maximum energy product \((BH)_{\max}\), roughly the largest rectangle fitting under the second-quadrant part of the curve, because it rewards having high \(B_r\) and high \(H_c\) together rather than either alone.
The trap to notice. Being offered a high remanence with no mention of coercivity is a signal that the question is testing whether you spot the omission.
Design meaning. Choose from the duty, not from the headline number. Reversed many times per second means soft and thin-looped; expected to hold a field against abuse means hard and fat-looped. The requirements genuinely conflict, so no single material does both well, and substituting one for the other is not a compromise — it is a failure.
Q9 · Short calculationWritten

At 50 Hz a core dissipates 20 W of hysteresis loss and 5 W of eddy-current loss. The same core, unchanged, is driven at 200 Hz. Estimate each loss and the total, then state what the result implies for the design.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: hysteresis 80 W, eddy-current 80 W, total about 160 W — a 6.4-fold increase from 25 W.
1. Frequency ratio. \(200/50=4\).
2. Hysteresis. Loop area is energy lost per cycle and does not change, so the loss scales with the number of cycles per second: \(20\times4=80\) W.
3. Eddy currents. These scale roughly with the square of frequency: \(5\times4^2=5\times16=80\) W.
4. Total. \(80+80=160\) W, against \(20+5=25\) W originally, a factor of 6.4.
5. Trend check. Note what happened to the balance: eddy currents were only 20% of the loss at 50 Hz and are half of it at 200 Hz. That reordering is the substance of the answer, because it changes which fix is worth applying. Extrapolate further and eddy currents dominate completely, which is why 20 kHz designs abandon laminated steel for ferrites.
6. Design meaning. A core qualified at mains frequency cannot be assumed safe at four times that frequency, even though nothing about the material changed. Either re-laminate more thinly, move to a higher-resistivity or insulating magnetic material, reduce the flux swing, or provide six times the cooling capacity. Quoting "low-loss grade" without stating the frequency is not a specification.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A permanent magnet is to sit next to the windings of a motor that runs at up to 120 °C. The proposed grade has the highest remanence available, and its coercivity is described only as "adequate at room temperature". Explain the risk and say what you would ask for before approving it.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: the magnet is at real risk of partial or total demagnetisation in service, because coercivity has been treated as an afterthought and it is the property that protects the remanence.
1. Classify. This is a hard magnetic material question, and specifically the remanence-versus-coercivity trap rather than a loss calculation. Nothing is being cycled, so loop area is not the issue.
2. What each property does. Remanence \(B_r\) is how much flux density survives when the applied field is removed — the headline number, and the one that sets the torque available. Coercivity \(H_c\) is the reverse field needed to destroy that magnetisation. High \(B_r\) with modest \(H_c\) gives a magnet that starts strong and does not stay strong.
3. The service environment. Sitting beside motor windings means exposure to substantial stray fields, including the reverse fields present during current transients and fault conditions. Add mechanical shock in handling and assembly, and the demagnetising influences are not hypothetical.
4. Why the temperature phrasing is the real warning. Coercivity falls as temperature rises, so a value that is "adequate at room temperature" may be well short of adequate at 120 °C — which is the condition that matters, because the motor is hottest exactly when it is working hardest and drawing the largest currents. The two worst conditions coincide.
5. Trend check. If demagnetisation begins, the loss is largely permanent: flux falls, so more current is needed for the same torque, so the windings run hotter, which drives coercivity down further. The failure mode drifts rather than announcing itself, and it can be misdiagnosed as a control or bearing problem.
6. What to ask for. Coercivity and the demagnetisation curve at 120 °C, not at 20 °C; the maximum energy product \((BH)_{\max}\), since it rewards having both properties together and is the honest figure of merit; the reverse field the magnet will actually see under worst-case current; and the manufacturer's stated maximum operating temperature.
7. Design meaning. Approve on the coercivity margin at peak temperature, then check that remanence is sufficient — that order, not the reverse. If margin is thin, the answers are a higher-coercivity grade, a magnetic circuit that reduces the reverse field seen by the magnet, or better cooling. Selecting on remanence alone is how a motor quietly loses torque over its first year.
