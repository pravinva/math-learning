Creep, Phase Basics and Thermal Properties

Three topics that only look unrelated. All three are about what happens when a part is hot: it stretches slowly under load that it could hold forever when cold, it changes size, and if something stops it changing size, it generates stress out of nowhere.

What this week is really asking
What does heat do to a part that is already carrying load?

Temperature is the theme. Creep is time plus heat plus load. Thermal expansion is heat plus geometry. The connection between them is that both are driven by atoms having enough energy to move, which is the exponential from Week 3.

Working through the week

Read this section first if the topic is new. It builds the ideas in order. Everything below — the glossary, cards, worked examples and exam practice — stays as your summary and drill.

Creep: slow motion at high temperature

Hold a metal below yield but at high temperature (high relative to its melting point) and it slowly extends. Creep curves show strain versus time: primary (decelerating), secondary (steady rate), tertiary (accelerating to failure). Design extrapolations usually use the secondary slope.

Polymers creep at room temperature; steels do not notice room temperature. Always think in \(T/T_m\), not absolute comfort temperature.

Thermal expansion and constrained stress

Heating makes most solids expand by \(\Delta L/L = \alpha \Delta T\). If a bar is free to expand, no stress appears. If it is clamped, expansion is prevented and stress builds: \(\sigma \approx E \alpha \Delta T\) for full restraint. A 100 °C rise in a restrained steel bar can produce stress near yield.

Thermal mismatch between joined materials (steel bolt in aluminium, ceramic coating on metal) creates stress even when each piece alone would be fine.

Phases as a preview

Week 5 introduces phase as a region with uniform structure and composition. Microstructure is how those regions are arranged. Same phases, different arrangement — different properties. That distinction becomes the whole of Week 6 on phase diagrams.

The words and symbols, in plain English

The ideas, one card at a time

1 Creep and its three stages

Hold a metal hot and loaded, and it slowly stretches — not because the load rose, but because atoms have enough energy to rearrange under the load they already carry.

The curve, left to right
  • Primary — starts quickly, then slows as the material work-hardens against its own deformation.
  • Secondary — a long straight stretch at nearly constant rate, where hardening and softening balance. Almost all of the service life sits here.
  • Tertiary — accelerates away as voids form and the section thins, ending in rupture.

Design uses the secondary slope because it is steady, predictable, and covers the vast majority of the life. If a question asks which region to measure, it is this one.

Creep needs heat, and "hot" is relative to the melting point of that material — roughly above 40% of it in kelvin. Lead creeps in a warm room. Steel does not care until it is glowing. Polymers creep at room temperature, which is why a plastic shelf sags over a summer.

Feeds: Week 7, where polymer creep at everyday temperatures is a routine design problem, and Week 8 material selection for anything hot.
Say it cold: "Creep is slow stretching under steady load when hot, and design uses the straight secondary stage."

2 Thermal expansion — strain without stress

Heat something and it grows. On its own, that produces no stress at all. Stress only appears when something refuses to let it grow.

\[\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\]

\(\Delta L\) is the change in length, \(\alpha\) the expansion coefficient, \(L_0\) the original length and \(\Delta T\) the temperature change.

Keep the two cases completely separate in your head, because exam questions are built on the distinction:

  • Free to expand — the part changes length, and the stress stays essentially zero.
  • Fully constrained — the length cannot change, so the strain that wanted to happen turns into stress instead, of order \(E\alpha\Delta T\).
\[\sigma_{\text{thermal}}\approx E\alpha\Delta T\]

The stress in a fully constrained bar. Note that a stiffer material makes this worse, not better.

That last point is counterintuitive and worth holding onto: for thermal stress, high stiffness is a liability. Steel generates more thermal stress than aluminium for the same restrained temperature change, despite expanding less.

Feeds: joint design everywhere — expansion loops in pipework, gaps in rails and bridges, sliding supports.
Say it cold: "Free to expand means strain and no stress; fully constrained means no strain and stress of about E alpha delta T."

3 Heat flow and thermal shock

Conductivity decides how fast heat moves through a material, and how fast it moves decides whether the object survives a sudden temperature change.

\[q_x=-kA\frac{dT}{dx}\]

Fourier's law in one dimension: \(q_x\) is heat flow rate, \(k\) thermal conductivity, \(A\) the area and \(dT/dx\) the temperature gradient. The minus sign only says heat flows from hot to cold.

Thermal shock follows from combining this with the previous card. Plunge a hot object into cold water and the surface cools and tries to contract while the interior is still hot and large. The surface is restrained by the interior, goes into tension, and if the material is brittle it cracks.

This is why ceramics fail thermal shock so readily: low conductivity means a steep temperature gradient, and near-zero ductility means the resulting stress cannot be relieved by yielding. A metal in the same situation simply deforms a little.

Feeds: Week 12 ceramics, where thermal shock resistance is a headline design property.
Say it cold: "Low conductivity plus brittleness gives poor thermal shock resistance, because the gradient makes stress the material cannot yield away."

4 Phase vocabulary — the setup for Week 6

Two words that get used interchangeably in conversation and mean quite different things here. Week 6 is much easier if this distinction is already solid.

  • Phase — a region that is uniform in structure and composition throughout. Ice and liquid water are two phases of the same substance.
  • Microstructure — how those phases are actually arranged: how big the regions are, what shape, how distributed.

The reason this matters is that two samples can contain identical phases in identical proportions and still behave completely differently, because the arrangement differs. Same ingredients, different cake. Steel is the standard demonstration — the same iron and carbon, cooled at different rates, gives soft pearlite or hard martensite.

So when a question asks what changed after a heat treatment, "the phases" and "the microstructure" are different claims, and often only the second is true.

Feeds: Week 6 entirely, where phase diagrams let you predict which phases appear and in what amounts.
Say it cold: "A phase is a uniform region; microstructure is how the phases are arranged, and the arrangement controls properties."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — free expansion

Given: \(L_0=2\) m, \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\)/°C, \(\Delta T=100\) °C, bar free to move.
  1. \(\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T=12\times10^{-6}\times2\times100\).
  2. \(\Delta L=2.4\times10^{-3}\) m \(=2.4\) mm.
  3. The bar is unrestrained, so this is pure strain and the stress is essentially zero.
What it means: 2.4 mm sounds trivial until you picture a 2 m gap that was designed as 1 mm. Most thermal design is about leaving somewhere for this to go.

Worked example 2 — the same bar, fully constrained

Given: Same bar and temperature rise, but now held rigidly at both ends. \(E=210\) GPa.
  1. The bar cannot lengthen, so the thermal strain that wanted to occur is suppressed.
  2. The suppressed strain is \(\varepsilon=\alpha\Delta T=12\times10^{-6}\times100=1.2\times10^{-3}\).
  3. That strain appears as stress: \(\sigma=E\varepsilon=210\times10^9\times1.2\times10^{-3}=252\) MPa.
  4. Compare with a typical structural steel yield of around 250 MPa — this alone is enough to yield the bar.
What it means: A 100 °C rise, which is not dramatic, is enough to yield a fully restrained steel bar. Note that the length dropped out entirely: constrained thermal stress does not depend on how long the bar is.

Worked example 3 — why a sliding support changes the answer

A part has high thermal expansion but is mounted on a sliding support. What happens to thermal stress?

  1. The sliding support lets the part change length freely.
  2. Thermal strain is therefore accommodated rather than suppressed.
  3. With little suppressed strain there is little thermal stress — dramatically less than the fixed-fixed case.
What it means: This is the whole design principle. You cannot stop a material expanding, so you give it somewhere to go: sliding bearings, expansion joints, pipe loops, gaps between rails.

Where marks get lost

  • Calculating thermal stress for a part that is free to move. No constraint means essentially no stress. Read the mounting description first.
  • Thinking a stiffer material reduces thermal stress. The opposite — \(\sigma\approx E\alpha\Delta T\) rises with \(E\).
  • Using the tertiary creep slope. Design uses the steady secondary stage; tertiary means the part is already failing.
  • Treating "hot" as an absolute. Creep depends on temperature relative to the melting point. Room temperature is hot for a polymer and cold for steel.
  • Using phase and microstructure as synonyms. Same phases, different arrangement, very different properties.

Exam practice — 20 questions

Eight multiple choice, four true/false, and eight short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

A support inside a furnace carries a steady load that is comfortably below its yield strength, yet over several months it is measured to have lengthened permanently. Which set of conditions is required for this mechanism?

  1. A load that exceeds the ultimate tensile strength (UTS), the highest stress the material can carry.
  2. A steady load, a temperature that is a large fraction of the melting temperature measured in kelvin, and time.
  3. A load that reverses direction through many thousands of cycles.
  4. A brittle material containing a pre-existing crack.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. This is creep: slow permanent stretching under a load that never changes, driven by atoms having enough thermal energy to rearrange. All three ingredients are needed — stress, high homologous temperature (roughly above 40% of the melting temperature in kelvin) and time. A is wrong because creep occurs well below yield, let alone UTS. C describes fatigue, which needs cyclic loading rather than time at temperature. D describes fast fracture from a flaw, which is instantaneous rather than progressive. Design meaning: "hot" is relative, so the same 20 °C room is cold for steel and hot for a polymer shelf or a lead sheet.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

In Fourier's law of one-dimensional heat conduction, \(q_x=-kA\,dT/dx\), what does the minus sign tell you?

  1. Heat flows from the colder region towards the hotter region.
  2. The thermal conductivity \(k\) takes negative values in insulating materials.
  3. Heat flows down the temperature gradient, that is, from hot towards cold.
  4. The direction of heat flow reverses once the cross-sectional area \(A\) becomes large enough.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. The gradient \(dT/dx\) is positive in the direction of increasing temperature, so a positive heat flow must point the other way. The minus sign is purely a bookkeeping device for direction and carries no physics beyond "hot to cold". B is wrong because \(k\) is a positive material property, high for metals and low for polymers and ceramics. A would violate the direction heat actually travels. Design meaning: for a fixed temperature difference, a low-\(k\) material holds a steeper gradient across itself, which is exactly what sets up thermal shock stresses.
Q3 · Multiple choiceTrap

Two bars of identical geometry are each held rigidly between fixed supports and heated by the same \(\Delta T\). Steel has \(E=210\) GPa and \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\)/°C; aluminium has \(E=70\) GPa and \(\alpha=23\times10^{-6}\)/°C. Which bar develops the higher thermal stress?

  1. Aluminium, because its coefficient of thermal expansion \(\alpha\) is nearly twice as large.
  2. Steel, because the product \(E\alpha\) is larger even though \(\alpha\) is smaller.
  3. Neither — with the same \(\Delta T\) the stress is the same in both.
  4. Neither — constrained thermal stress depends only on the length of the bar.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. For full constraint \(\sigma\approx E\alpha\Delta T\), so compare the products per degree. Steel: \(210\times10^{9}\times12\times10^{-6}=2.52\times10^{6}\) Pa/°C, that is 2.52 MPa per °C. Aluminium: \(70\times10^{9}\times23\times10^{-6}=1.61\times10^{6}\) Pa/°C, or 1.61 MPa per °C. Steel is about 1.6 times worse despite expanding less, because stiffness is a liability here. D is the other common error — length cancels out of \(\sigma\approx E\alpha\Delta T\) entirely. Design meaning: when a joint is restrained, choosing the stiffer material makes the thermal stress problem worse, not better.
Q4 · Multiple choiceOne step

A test report plots creep strain (vertical axis) against time (horizontal axis) for the same alloy at the same applied stress, one curve at 500 °C and one at 600 °C. Which description matches the 600 °C curve?

  1. A shallower straight middle portion and rupture at a later time.
  2. A steeper straight middle portion and rupture at an earlier time.
  3. An identical curve, because the applied stress is the same in both tests.
  4. A curve with no final accelerating portion, because heat prevents void formation.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Raising temperature gives atoms more energy to rearrange, so the secondary (steady-state) creep rate — the slope of the straight middle section — rises, and the accumulated strain reaches the rupture condition sooner. Read the two curves as a family: hotter sits above and to the left. C ignores that creep is driven by temperature as well as stress. D is backwards, because the tertiary stage where voids form and the section necks is reached earlier, not skipped. Trend check: if a "hotter" curve came out shallower and longer-lived, the axes or the labels have been read the wrong way round.
Q5 · True/falseTrap

True or false: A bar that is completely free to move develops no thermal strain when it is heated.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. The bar develops the full thermal strain \(\varepsilon=\alpha\Delta T\) — it genuinely gets longer by \(\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\). What it does not develop is thermal stress, because nothing is stopping that strain from happening. The statement swaps the two words, which is the single most common error on this topic. The two extreme cases are: free to expand gives strain and essentially zero stress; fully constrained gives zero strain and stress of about \(E\alpha\Delta T\). Design meaning: you cannot stop a material expanding, so expansion joints, sliding supports and pipe loops exist to convert the fully constrained case into the free case.
Q6 · True/falseQuick

True or false: Whether a temperature counts as "hot" for creep purposes depends on the temperature relative to that material's melting temperature in kelvin, not on the value in degrees Celsius alone.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Creep becomes significant roughly above 40% of the melting temperature expressed in kelvin, so the meaningful quantity is the ratio \(T/T_m\), not \(T\) by itself. Lead has a low \(T_m\) and creeps in a warm room; steel needs to be glowing before it cares; polymers creep at 20 °C, which is why a plastic shelf sags over a summer. Kelvin matters because a ratio of two temperatures is meaningless on a scale whose zero is arbitrary. Design meaning: the first question for any hot component is not "how hot?" but "how hot compared with this material's melting point?".
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

A loaded component is held at constant temperature and its creep strain is monitored. It measures 0.90% at 1,000 h and 2.10% at 5,000 h, and both readings sit on the straight secondary portion of the curve. (a) Find the secondary creep rate. (b) Predict the strain at 20,000 h and say whether a 5.0% strain limit is met. (c) State one reason the prediction may be optimistic.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: rate \(=3.0\times10^{-4}\) %/h; about 6.6% at 20,000 h, so the 5.0% limit fails.
(a) The secondary creep rate is the slope of the straight section: \(\dot\varepsilon=\dfrac{\Delta\varepsilon}{\Delta t}=\dfrac{2.10\%-0.90\%}{5{,}000\text{ h}-1{,}000\text{ h}}=\dfrac{1.20\%}{4{,}000\text{ h}}=3.0\times10^{-4}\) %/h, which is \(3.0\times10^{-6}\) per hour as a dimensionless strain rate.
(b) Extrapolate along the same straight line from the 5,000 h reading: \(\varepsilon=2.10\%+(3.0\times10^{-4}\text{ \%/h})(20{,}000-5{,}000)\text{ h}=2.10\%+4.50\%=6.60\%\). That exceeds 5.0%, so the component fails the limit. Solving for when the limit is reached: \((5.00-2.10)\%\div(3.0\times10^{-4}\text{ \%/h})\approx9{,}700\) h beyond the 5,000 h reading, so at roughly 14,700 h total.
(c) The extrapolation assumes the material stays in secondary creep for the whole 20,000 h. Once tertiary creep begins the rate accelerates as voids form and the section thins, so the real strain will be higher and rupture earlier than the straight-line estimate. A rise in temperature or stress would also raise the slope.
Sanity check: the units work out (%/h × h = %), the strain increases with time as creep must, and the answer is a few per cent rather than a fraction of a per cent or hundreds of per cent.
Self-mark: one point for the rate with units taken over the secondary portion; one for the extrapolated strain compared against the 5.0% limit; one for a stated reason tied to the tertiary stage or to a temperature/stress rise.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

An aluminium pipe run is \(L_0=6.0\) m long, with \(\alpha=23\times10^{-6}\)/°C and \(E=70\) GPa. In service it is heated by \(\Delta T=80\) °C. (a) Find the free expansion. (b) Find the stress if both ends are rigidly fixed. (c) Find the stress if a 5.0 mm expansion gap is provided, and comment on the design.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: 11.0 mm free expansion; about 129 MPa fully fixed; about 71 MPa with a 5.0 mm gap.
(a) \(\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T=(23\times10^{-6}\text{/°C})(6.0\text{ m})(80\text{ °C})=1.104\times10^{-2}\) m \(=11.0\) mm.
(b) Fully fixed, none of that movement is allowed, so the suppressed strain becomes stress: \(\varepsilon=\alpha\Delta T=(23\times10^{-6})(80)=1.84\times10^{-3}\), and \(\sigma=E\varepsilon=(70\times10^{9}\text{ Pa})(1.84\times10^{-3})=1.29\times10^{8}\) Pa \(=129\) MPa. Note that the 6.0 m length cancels out — length affects the movement, not the fully constrained stress.
(c) With a 5.0 mm gap, 5.0 mm of the 11.0 mm is accommodated and only \(11.0-5.0=6.0\) mm is suppressed. Suppressed strain \(=6.04\times10^{-3}\text{ m}/6.0\text{ m}=1.01\times10^{-3}\), so \(\sigma=(70\times10^{9})(1.01\times10^{-3})=7.1\times10^{7}\) Pa \(\approx71\) MPa.
Sanity check: the gap absorbs \(6.04/11.04=55\%\) of the movement, so the stress should be about 45% of the fully fixed value: \(0.45\times129\approx71\) MPa. The two routes agree, and the answer sits correctly between zero (free) and 129 MPa (fixed).
Design meaning: the gap is a stress dial. Sizing it for the full 11.0 mm — measured at the coldest condition the pipe will ever see, so the gap is not already closed — brings the thermal stress essentially to zero.
Self-mark: one point for the free expansion in mm; one for the fully fixed stress with the observation that length cancels; one for the partial-constraint stress; one for a comment that ties gap size to stress and mentions the cold-condition check.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

A creep test result is plotted as strain (vertical axis) against time (horizontal axis). The curve rises steeply from the origin then flattens, runs almost straight for a long stretch, and finally sweeps upward to a cross marking rupture. (a) Name the three regions and say what is physically happening in each. (b) Explain which slope you would measure for a design calculation and how you would take it off the plot. (c) State how the shape changes if the applied stress is raised at constant temperature.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: primary, secondary and tertiary creep; measure the secondary slope.
(a) Primary — the steep-then-flattening start. Deformation is initially rapid but the material work-hardens against its own deformation, so the rate falls. Secondary — the long straight stretch, where hardening and softening processes balance and the rate is nearly constant. Almost all of the service life sits here. Tertiary — the final upward sweep, where voids form and the cross-section thins, so the true stress climbs and the rate runs away to rupture.
(b) Use the secondary slope, because it is steady, predictable and covers most of the life, which makes it safe to extrapolate. Take it as \(\dot\varepsilon=\Delta\varepsilon/\Delta t\) between two points that both clearly lie on the straight portion — for example a rise of 0.60% between 2,000 h and 6,000 h gives \(1.5\times10^{-4}\) %/h. Choose the points far apart to reduce reading error, and stay clear of the curved ends: a slope taken in primary overestimates the long-term rate, and one taken in tertiary describes a component that is already failing.
(c) Raising the stress lifts the whole curve, steepens the secondary slope and shortens the time to rupture — the same qualitative change as raising the temperature. The tertiary stage also arrives earlier.
Design meaning: a creep design check has two separate questions — will the strain exceed the allowable distortion within the design life, and will the part actually rupture (stress rupture). The secondary slope answers the first; time to rupture answers the second.
Self-mark: one point for naming all three regions with a mechanism each; one for identifying the secondary slope and describing \(\Delta\varepsilon/\Delta t\) taken on the straight portion; one for the correct trend with rising stress; one for separating excessive strain from stress rupture.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A supplier certifies two batches of the same alloy as containing the same phases in the same proportions, and argues on that basis that the two batches must perform identically in service. Explain why the argument is incomplete, and state what additional information you would ask for.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: identical phases and proportions do not fix the microstructure, and properties follow the microstructure.
Why the argument fails. A phase is a region uniform in structure and composition — the supplier has only certified which uniform regions exist and how much of each. The microstructure is how those regions are arranged: their size, shape and distribution. Two samples can be identical on the first count and quite different on the second, and mechanical properties respond to the arrangement. Same ingredients, different cake. Steel is the standard demonstration: identical iron and carbon, cooled at different rates, gives structures with very different hardness.
What to ask for. Micrographs of each batch; a grain-size measurement, since finer grains raise yield strength; the actual heat-treatment and cooling-rate records, because that is what sets the arrangement; and hardness or tensile data measured on the delivered batches rather than quoted from a general specification.
Sanity framing: if the supplier's logic held, heat treatment could not work at all — yet the whole point of a heat treatment is to change properties without changing what you put in.
Design meaning: a certificate of composition is not a certificate of properties. When performance depends on strength or toughness, specify the process route and the tested property, not just the phases present.
Self-mark: one point for a correct definition of both phase and microstructure; one for stating that arrangement controls properties, with an example; one for at least two sensible pieces of extra information; one for the design conclusion about specifying process and tested properties.

Q11 · Multiple choiceQuick

Secondary (steady-state) creep rate is read from a creep curve as:

  1. The initial steep slope at zero time
  2. The slope of the nearly straight middle portion
  3. The vertical drop at rupture
  4. The total strain divided by elastic modulus
Show answer and reasoning

Q12 · Multiple choiceQuick

Thermal shock resistance improves when a ceramic has:

  1. High \(E\), high \(\alpha\), low strength
  2. Low \(E\), low \(\alpha\), high strength
  3. High conductivity and high \(\alpha\)
  4. Low density only
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Shock parameter \(R\propto\sigma_f/(E\alpha)\) — low stiffness and expansion, high strength. Design meaning: quench-cracking favours low-\(E\) porous refractories over dense stiff ones.

  1. 0.36 mm
  2. 0.72 mm
  3. 1.44 mm
  4. 7.2 mm

Q14 · Multiple choiceTrap

Two ceramics have the same \(\alpha\) but ceramic P has \(E=400\) GPa and ceramic Q has \(E=200\) GPa. Which develops lower thermal stress when quenched into rigid constraint?

  1. P, because higher \(E\) resists cracking
  2. Q, because \(\sigma\approx E\alpha\Delta T\) is halved
  3. Equal, because \(\alpha\) matches
  4. P, because stiff materials distribute stress better
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. At equal \(\Delta T\) and constraint, \(\sigma\propto E\). Lower \(E\) halves thermal stress. Design meaning: thermal shock favours compliant microstructures, not stiffer ones.

Q17 · Short calculationWritten

Creep data: \(\varepsilon=0.40\%\) at 2,000 h and 1.00% at 10,000 h on the secondary line. Find \(\dot\varepsilon\) in %/h and predict strain at 25,000 h.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(\dot\varepsilon=7.5\times10^{-5}\) %/h; \(\varepsilon\approx2.13\%\) at 25,000 h.
1. \(\dot\varepsilon=(1.00-0.40)/(10{,}000-2{,}000)=0.60/8000=7.5\times10^{-5}\) %/h.
2. \(\varepsilon=1.00+7.5\times10^{-5}(25{,}000-10{,}000)=2.125\%\).
Sanity check: strain grows linearly on secondary portion. Design meaning: a 2% distortion limit fails before rupture — strain and time-to-rupture are separate checks.
Q18 · Short calculationWritten

Brass bar \(L=1.5\) m, \(\alpha=20\times10^{-6}\)/°C, \(E=110\) GPa, \(\Delta T=60\) °C. Find free \(\Delta L\) and fully fixed stress.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(\Delta L=1.8\) mm; \(\sigma=132\) MPa.
1. \(\Delta L=20\times10^{-6}(1500)(60)=1.8\) mm.
2. \(\sigma=E\alpha\Delta T=110\times10^9(20\times10^{-6})(60)=132\) MPa.
Sanity check: 132 MPa is a real load — comparable to working stresses. Design meaning: rigid clamps on a 60 °C swing need slip joints or the bar yields.
Q19 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Sketch description: Gibbs phase rule regions on a unary diagram — single-phase solid, liquid, and two-phase solid+liquid band. A vertical line at fixed composition crosses liquid, then L+S, then solid. Name phases present in each segment and state what happens at each boundary crossing.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: above liquidus — all liquid; between liquidus and solidus — L+S coexist; below solidus — solid only. At liquidus, solid nucleates; at solidus, liquid disappears. Design meaning: this unary picture previews binary lever-rule thinking — temperature fixes which phases can exist.
Q20 · Short explanationWritten

A polymer gear and a steel gear both operate at 120 °C in an oven conveyor. Explain why creep may govern the polymer while steel is unaffected, using homologous temperature.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: polymer \(T_m\) or \(T_g\) may be only 150–250 °C, so 120 °C is 0.5–0.8 \(T_m\) — deep in the creep regime. Steel \(T_m\approx1800\) K; 393 K is \(<0.25\,T_m\) — negligible creep. Design meaning: 'same oven temperature' is not the same material experience — always compare \(T/T_m\).
