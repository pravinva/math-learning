Creep, Phase Basics and Thermal Properties

Three topics that only look unrelated. All three are about what happens when a part is hot: it stretches slowly under load that it could hold forever when cold, it changes size, and if something stops it changing size, it generates stress out of nowhere.

What this week is really asking
What does heat do to a part that is already carrying load?

Temperature is the theme. Creep is time plus heat plus load. Thermal expansion is heat plus geometry. The connection between them is that both are driven by atoms having enough energy to move, which is the exponential from Week 3.

Where this sits. Part of card 10 of the core map, and it introduces the phase vocabulary that Week 6 turns into calculations. It inherits the vacancy exponential from Week 3. Textbook sections: Callister Ch8 §8.9–8.15, Ch9 §9.1–9.6, Ch19 §19.1–19.4.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
Creep
Creep—Slow, permanent stretching under a steady load at high temperature. The load never changes; the part just keeps getting longer.
Primary creep—The first stage. Fast at first, then slowing as the material work-hardens.
Secondary creep—The long steady middle stage, at a nearly constant rate. This is what design calculations use.
Tertiary creep—The final stage, accelerating toward rupture as the section necks and voids form.
Creep rate—The slope of the steady middle stage, in strain per unit time.
Stress rupture—Actual breaking from creep, as opposed to merely deforming too much.
Thermal
\(\alpha\)coefficient of thermal expansionHow much a material stretches per degree, per unit length. Typically around \(12\times10^{-6}\)/°C for steel.
Thermal strain—The size change from a temperature change. It happens whenever temperature changes.
Thermal stress—Stress that appears only when that size change is prevented. No constraint, no stress.
\(k\)thermal conductivityHow readily heat flows through the material. Metals high, polymers and ceramics low.
Thermal shock—Cracking caused by rapid temperature change, when one part of an object wants to change size faster than the rest.
Phase vocabulary
Phase—A region with one consistent structure and composition. Ice and water are two phases of one substance.
Microstructure—How the phases and grains are arranged — their size, shape and distribution. Processing controls it, and it controls properties.
Component—One of the pure substances making up the alloy. Copper and nickel are the two components of a Cu–Ni alloy.
Solubility limit—The most of one substance that will dissolve in another before a second phase appears.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 Creep and its three stages

Hold a metal hot and loaded, and it slowly stretches — not because the load rose, but because atoms have enough energy to rearrange under the load they already carry.

The curve, left to right
  • Primary — starts quickly, then slows as the material work-hardens against its own deformation.
  • Secondary — a long straight stretch at nearly constant rate, where hardening and softening balance. Almost all of the service life sits here.
  • Tertiary — accelerates away as voids form and the section thins, ending in rupture.

Design uses the secondary slope because it is steady, predictable, and covers the vast majority of the life. If a question asks which region to measure, it is this one.

Creep needs heat, and "hot" is relative to the melting point of that material — roughly above 40% of it in kelvin. Lead creeps in a warm room. Steel does not care until it is glowing. Polymers creep at room temperature, which is why a plastic shelf sags over a summer.

Feeds: Week 7, where polymer creep at everyday temperatures is a routine design problem, and Week 8 material selection for anything hot.
Say it cold: "Creep is slow stretching under steady load when hot, and design uses the straight secondary stage."

2 Thermal expansion — strain without stress

Heat something and it grows. On its own, that produces no stress at all. Stress only appears when something refuses to let it grow.

\[\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T\]

\(\Delta L\) is the change in length, \(\alpha\) the expansion coefficient, \(L_0\) the original length and \(\Delta T\) the temperature change.

Keep the two cases completely separate in your head, because exam questions are built on the distinction:

  • Free to expand — the part changes length, and the stress stays essentially zero.
  • Fully constrained — the length cannot change, so the strain that wanted to happen turns into stress instead, of order \(E\alpha\Delta T\).
\[\sigma_{\text{thermal}}\approx E\alpha\Delta T\]

The stress in a fully constrained bar. Note that a stiffer material makes this worse, not better.

That last point is counterintuitive and worth holding onto: for thermal stress, high stiffness is a liability. Steel generates more thermal stress than aluminium for the same restrained temperature change, despite expanding less.

Feeds: joint design everywhere — expansion loops in pipework, gaps in rails and bridges, sliding supports.
Say it cold: "Free to expand means strain and no stress; fully constrained means no strain and stress of about E alpha delta T."

3 Heat flow and thermal shock

Conductivity decides how fast heat moves through a material, and how fast it moves decides whether the object survives a sudden temperature change.

\[q_x=-kA\frac{dT}{dx}\]

Fourier's law in one dimension: \(q_x\) is heat flow rate, \(k\) thermal conductivity, \(A\) the area and \(dT/dx\) the temperature gradient. The minus sign only says heat flows from hot to cold.

Thermal shock follows from combining this with the previous card. Plunge a hot object into cold water and the surface cools and tries to contract while the interior is still hot and large. The surface is restrained by the interior, goes into tension, and if the material is brittle it cracks.

This is why ceramics fail thermal shock so readily: low conductivity means a steep temperature gradient, and near-zero ductility means the resulting stress cannot be relieved by yielding. A metal in the same situation simply deforms a little.

Feeds: Week 12 ceramics, where thermal shock resistance is a headline design property.
Say it cold: "Low conductivity plus brittleness gives poor thermal shock resistance, because the gradient makes stress the material cannot yield away."

4 Phase vocabulary — the setup for Week 6

Two words that get used interchangeably in conversation and mean quite different things here. Week 6 is much easier if this distinction is already solid.

  • Phase — a region that is uniform in structure and composition throughout. Ice and liquid water are two phases of the same substance.
  • Microstructure — how those phases are actually arranged: how big the regions are, what shape, how distributed.

The reason this matters is that two samples can contain identical phases in identical proportions and still behave completely differently, because the arrangement differs. Same ingredients, different cake. Steel is the standard demonstration — the same iron and carbon, cooled at different rates, gives soft pearlite or hard martensite.

So when a question asks what changed after a heat treatment, "the phases" and "the microstructure" are different claims, and often only the second is true.

Feeds: Week 6 entirely, where phase diagrams let you predict which phases appear and in what amounts.
Say it cold: "A phase is a uniform region; microstructure is how the phases are arranged, and the arrangement controls properties."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — free expansion

Given: \(L_0=2\) m, \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\)/°C, \(\Delta T=100\) °C, bar free to move.
  1. \(\Delta L=\alpha L_0\Delta T=12\times10^{-6}\times2\times100\).
  2. \(\Delta L=2.4\times10^{-3}\) m \(=2.4\) mm.
  3. The bar is unrestrained, so this is pure strain and the stress is essentially zero.
What it means: 2.4 mm sounds trivial until you picture a 2 m gap that was designed as 1 mm. Most thermal design is about leaving somewhere for this to go.

Worked example 2 — the same bar, fully constrained

Given: Same bar and temperature rise, but now held rigidly at both ends. \(E=210\) GPa.
  1. The bar cannot lengthen, so the thermal strain that wanted to occur is suppressed.
  2. The suppressed strain is \(\varepsilon=\alpha\Delta T=12\times10^{-6}\times100=1.2\times10^{-3}\).
  3. That strain appears as stress: \(\sigma=E\varepsilon=210\times10^9\times1.2\times10^{-3}=252\) MPa.
  4. Compare with a typical structural steel yield of around 250 MPa — this alone is enough to yield the bar.
What it means: A 100 °C rise, which is not dramatic, is enough to yield a fully restrained steel bar. Note that the length dropped out entirely: constrained thermal stress does not depend on how long the bar is.

Worked example 3 — why a sliding support changes the answer

A part has high thermal expansion but is mounted on a sliding support. What happens to thermal stress?

  1. The sliding support lets the part change length freely.
  2. Thermal strain is therefore accommodated rather than suppressed.
  3. With little suppressed strain there is little thermal stress — dramatically less than the fixed-fixed case.
What it means: This is the whole design principle. You cannot stop a material expanding, so you give it somewhere to go: sliding bearings, expansion joints, pipe loops, gaps between rails.

Where marks get lost

  • Calculating thermal stress for a part that is free to move. No constraint means essentially no stress. Read the mounting description first.
  • Thinking a stiffer material reduces thermal stress. The opposite — \(\sigma\approx E\alpha\Delta T\) rises with \(E\).
  • Using the tertiary creep slope. Design uses the steady secondary stage; tertiary means the part is already failing.
  • Treating "hot" as an absolute. Creep depends on temperature relative to the melting point. Room temperature is hot for a polymer and cold for steel.
  • Using phase and microstructure as synonyms. Same phases, different arrangement, very different properties.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open. An answer you can only recognise is not an answer you can write in an exam.

1. Why is secondary creep the stage used in long-service design calculations?
Because it is the long steady-rate middle stage where hardening and softening balance, and it accounts for nearly all of the service life. Its constant slope can be extrapolated to predict deformation over years. Primary is too short and still changing, and tertiary means failure has already begun.
2. A part has high thermal expansion but sits on a sliding support. What happens to thermal stress?
It is greatly reduced. Thermal stress only appears when expansion is prevented. The sliding support allows the part to change length freely, so the thermal strain is accommodated instead of converted into stress, unlike a fixed-fixed mounting.
3. How do phase and microstructure differ, and why does it matter?
A phase is a region uniform in structure and composition. Microstructure is how the phases are arranged — their size, shape and distribution. It matters because identical phases in identical proportions can give very different properties depending on arrangement, which is exactly how heat treatment of steel works.
4. A steel bar is fully restrained and heated 100 °C. Estimate the stress. \(E=210\) GPa, \(\alpha=12\times10^{-6}\)/°C.
\(\sigma\approx E\alpha\Delta T=210\times10^9\times12\times10^{-6}\times100=252\) MPa. That is around the yield strength of ordinary structural steel, so a modest restrained temperature rise can yield it. The bar length does not enter the calculation.
5. Why do ceramics survive thermal shock so much worse than metals?
Two reasons combining. Their low thermal conductivity creates a steep temperature gradient, so the surface contracts hard while the interior is still hot, putting the surface in tension. And having almost no ductility, they cannot relieve that stress by yielding as a metal would, so it goes straight into cracking from an existing surface flaw.
