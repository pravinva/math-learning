Mechanical Properties of Metals

This is the week that turns materials into numbers. One test — pull a bar until it breaks — produces a single curve, and nearly every quantitative question in the subject reads something off that curve. Learning to read it fluently pays for itself many times over.

What this week is really asking
How much load can this material take, and what happens on the way to failure?

The stress–strain curve is the most reused picture in DPEN103. Every landmark on it has a name, a formula and a design meaning, and exam questions almost always ask for all three.

Where this sits. This is card 3 of the core map and the most heavily used card in the subject. It measures what Week 1 explained, and Weeks 3 and 4 explain how to move the landmarks around. There are six worked diagrams for this curve on the reading the pictures page. Textbook sections: Callister Ch6 §6.1–6.12.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
The two axes
Stress, \(\sigma\)force per unit areaHow hard the material is being pulled at a point. Measured in pascals; 1 MPa is one newton per square millimetre.
Strain, \(\varepsilon\)extension ÷ original lengthHow much it stretched, as a fraction. It has no units. 0.01 means it got 1% longer.
Landmarks on the curve
\(E\)Young's modulus, or stiffnessThe slope of the straight first part. High \(E\) means it barely moves under load. Steel is about 210 GPa, aluminium about 70.
Elastic—It springs back when you let go. Nothing permanent has happened.
Plastic—It stays bent. Confusingly nothing to do with plastics the material.
Yield, \(\sigma_y\)yield strengthThe stress where it stops springing back. This is normally your design limit.
0.2% offset—The construction used to find yield when the curve bends gradually: draw a line parallel to the elastic slope starting at a strain of 0.002 and see where it cuts the curve.
UTSultimate tensile strengthThe highest stress the curve ever reaches — the peak. Past it the bar is on its way to breaking.
Necking—When the bar starts thinning at one spot instead of stretching evenly. It begins at the UTS and is the beginning of the end.
Ductility—How far it stretches before breaking. High ductility means plenty of visible warning.
Brittle—Breaks with almost no stretching and no warning.
Areas under the curve
Resilience, \(U_r\)—Energy absorbed while still springing back — the triangle under the elastic part only. This is what a spring needs.
Toughness—Energy absorbed all the way to fracture — the whole area. Strong and tough are not the same thing.
Engineering versus true
Engineering stress—Force divided by the original area. What the test machine reports, and what you use unless told otherwise.
True stress—Force divided by the actual, shrinking area. Always higher than engineering stress once the bar starts thinning.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 The two definitions everything rests on

Stress and strain exist to make results independent of the size of the test piece, so that a number measured on a small sample can be applied to a large part.

\[\sigma=\frac{F}{A_0}\qquad\varepsilon=\frac{L-L_0}{L_0}\]

\(F\) is the applied force, \(A_0\) the original cross-sectional area, \(L_0\) the original length and \(L\) the stretched length.

Dividing by area is what lets you compare a thin wire with a thick bar. The same 10 kN is harmless in a thick bar and fatal in a thin one, and stress is the number that captures the difference.

Watch the units. Newtons over square millimetres gives megapascals directly, which is usually the least error-prone route.

Feeds: literally every calculation for the rest of the subject.
Say it cold: "Stress is force spread over area; strain is stretch as a fraction of the original length."

2 Stiffness — the straight part

While the curve is straight, the material is behaving like a spring: load it and it stretches, unload it and it returns exactly. The slope of that straight line is the stiffness.

\[\sigma=E\varepsilon\qquad\Rightarrow\qquad E=\frac{\sigma}{\varepsilon}\]

\(E\) is Young's modulus, in gigapascals. It is the slope of the elastic part of the curve.

The thing most often confused
  • Stiffness resists deflection. Strength resists permanent damage. They are independent.
  • A part that sags too much has a stiffness problem, and a stronger alloy will not fix it.
  • \(E\) is set by the bonding and barely moves with heat treatment. If you need more stiffness, change material or change the shape of the section.
Feeds: Week 5, where a constrained bar that cannot expand generates stress of order \(E\alpha\Delta T\), and Week 11, where the rule of mixtures predicts \(E\) for a composite.
Say it cold: "Stiffness is the slope of the elastic line, it resists deflection not damage, and heat treatment barely changes it."

3 Yield, UTS and fracture

Three named points, in the order you meet them as the bar is pulled. Mixing up which one is the design limit is a classic and expensive error.

  • Yield, \(\sigma_y\) — the end of springing back. Load beyond this and the part stays deformed. This is normally the design limit.
  • UTS — ultimate tensile strength, the peak of the curve. Past it, necking has started and failure is on the way.
  • Fracture — where it finally separates. On a ductile metal this happens at a lower stress than the UTS, because the shrinking neck carries the load on a much smaller area.

When yield is not a sharp corner, it is defined by the 0.2% offset construction: draw a line parallel to the elastic slope but starting at a strain of 0.002, and take yield as where that line crosses the curve. The 0.2% is a convention, not physics — it is simply an agreed small amount of permanent set.

\[N=\frac{\sigma_y}{\sigma_{\text{working}}}\]

The safety factor \(N\): yield strength divided by the highest stress the part will actually see in service.

Feeds: the fatigue work of Week 4, where parts fail below yield, and the selection arguments of Weeks 8 and 13.
Say it cold: "Yield is the design limit, UTS is the peak, and fracture happens after the neck forms at a lower stress than the peak."

4 The two areas — resilience and toughness

Area under a stress–strain curve is energy per unit volume. Which part of the area you take answers a different design question.

\[U_r=\frac{\sigma_y^{2}}{2E}\]

Resilience: the energy stored per cubic metre while the material is still springing back. It is just the area of the elastic triangle.

  • Resilience — area under the elastic part only. Energy you get back. This is what a spring is designed for.
  • Toughness — area under the entire curve to fracture. Energy absorbed before breaking. This is what a crash structure is designed for.

The pair explains a result that looks contradictory at first: a high-strength brittle alloy can be much less tough than a weaker ductile one, because the weaker material keeps absorbing energy over a far longer stretch. A tall narrow curve can enclose less area than a short wide one.

Feeds: Week 4 on impact and fracture, and Week 12, where ceramics have high strength and almost no toughness.
Say it cold: "Resilience is the elastic triangle and you get it back; toughness is the whole area and it is what breaking costs."

5 Engineering versus true stress

Engineering stress divides by the original area even after the bar has visibly thinned. True stress uses the actual area. The gap between them grows as the bar necks.

\[\sigma_t=\sigma(1+\varepsilon)\qquad\varepsilon_t=\ln(1+\varepsilon)\]

Converting engineering values to true values, valid up to the point where necking begins.

The engineering curve turns over and comes down after the UTS. The true curve keeps rising all the way to fracture. Nothing is getting weaker — the load is being carried on a shrinking area that the engineering calculation refuses to acknowledge.

Use engineering stress unless a question explicitly involves large plastic strain or asks about true values. For design below yield the two are almost identical.

Feeds: metal forming, where large deliberate plastic strain makes true values the only sensible ones.
Say it cold: "Engineering stress uses the original area and falls after the peak; true stress uses the real area and never falls."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — the standard full calculation

Given: \(A_0=120\) mm², \(F=43.2\) kN, length goes from 50 mm to 58 mm, \(E=210\) GPa, \(\sigma_y=450\) MPa.
  1. Engineering stress: \(\sigma=43.2\times10^3/(120\times10^{-6})=360\) MPa. Working in newtons and square millimetres gives the same thing faster: \(43200/120=360\).
  2. Engineering strain: \(\varepsilon=(58-50)/50=0.16\), so it stretched 16%.
  3. Is this elastic? No — 360 MPa is below yield, but a strain of 0.16 is far beyond elastic, so this reading is well into the plastic region and the two numbers together tell you the bar is permanently stretched.
  4. True stress: \(\sigma_t=360(1+0.16)=417.6\) MPa.
  5. True strain: \(\varepsilon_t=\ln(1.16)=0.148\).
  6. Resilience: \(U_r=\sigma_y^2/(2E)=(450\times10^6)^2/(2\times210\times10^9)=4.82\times10^5\) J/m³.
What it means: True stress came out higher than engineering stress, as it always must once the bar has thinned. Resilience is the energy per cubic metre this material could store and give back if it were kept below yield.

Worked example 2 — will it yield?

Given: \(A_0=100\) mm², \(F=32\) kN, \(\sigma_y=280\) MPa.
  1. \(\sigma=32000/100=320\) MPa.
  2. Compare with yield: \(320>280\).
  3. So yes, it yields, and the part will not return to its original length.
What it means: Answer the question that was asked. "320 MPa" alone is half an answer; the comparison with yield and the words "so it stays permanently deformed" are the other half.

Worked example 3 — safety factor and what it is for

Given: Maximum service stress 180 MPa, yield strength 450 MPa.
  1. \(N=\sigma_y/\sigma_{\text{working}}=450/180=2.5\).
  2. Now ask whether 2.5 is enough, which depends on things the number cannot see: how well the load is known, how much the material varies batch to batch, whether temperature or corrosion will degrade it, and what happens to people if it fails.
  3. A bracket in a well-characterised indoor rig might be fine at 2.5. A lifting component with the same number would not be.
What it means: A safety factor is a judgement wrapped in a number. Exam answers that compute it and stop miss the point; say what it is covering for.

The pattern to carry forward: strong and tough are different

This is the distinction Week 2 exists to install, and it comes back in Weeks 4, 11 and 12.

Strength is how high the curve goes. Toughness is how much area sits underneath it. A material can be excellent at one and poor at the other, which is precisely why ceramics — very strong in compression, almost no toughness — need completely different design rules from metals.

Whenever a question offers you two materials with the same yield strength, it is almost always about toughness or ductility.

Where marks get lost

  • Using UTS as the design limit. Yield is the design limit. A part that has passed its UTS is already necking.
  • Mixing up stiffness and strength. A sagging shelf needs higher \(E\); a bent one needs higher \(\sigma_y\). Heat treatment changes the second and barely touches the first.
  • Forgetting that fracture stress is below UTS on a ductile metal. It looks wrong on the graph until you remember the neck is carrying the load on a much smaller area.
  • Quoting resilience when the question wants toughness. Resilience is the elastic triangle only; toughness is the whole area.
  • Unit slips between MPa and Pa. Newtons over square millimetres gives MPa directly. Pick one system and stay in it for the whole question.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

A tensile specimen is recorded at a strain of \(\varepsilon=0.01\). What does that number mean?

  1. It is carrying 0.01 MPa of stress
  2. It has stretched by 1% of its original length, and the number has no units
  3. It has stretched by 0.01 mm, whatever its original length
  4. It has stretched by 10% of its original length
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Strain is defined as \(\varepsilon=(L-L_0)/L_0\) — the extension divided by the original length — so it is a ratio of two lengths and the units cancel. A value of 0.01 is one hundredth, or 1%. Option C is wrong because strain is deliberately independent of specimen size: that is the whole reason it is defined as a fraction rather than as a millimetre reading. Design meaning: because strain carries no units, a strain measured on a 50 mm test coupon can be applied directly to a 5 m structural member.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

Which statement correctly describes the 0.2% offset construction?

  1. Draw a line parallel to the elastic slope starting at a strain of 0.002, and take yield where it cuts the curve
  2. Draw a line parallel to the elastic slope starting at a strain of 0.2, and take yield where it cuts the curve
  3. Take the stress at which the specimen has stretched by 0.2 mm
  4. Take 0.2% of the ultimate tensile strength as the yield strength
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. When the curve bends gradually there is no obvious corner to call yield, so an agreed convention is used: offset a line parallel to the elastic slope by a strain of 0.002 — which is what 0.2% means as a fraction — and read yield where that line crosses the measured curve. Option B is out by a factor of 100 and would land near fracture on most metals. Option C fails because a millimetre reading depends on the gauge length. Why it matters: the 0.2% is a convention, not physics — it is simply an agreed small amount of permanent set that everyone can reproduce, which is what makes quoted yield strengths comparable between laboratories.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A bar of original cross-sectional area \(A_0=80\) mm² carries a tensile force of 24 kN. What is the engineering stress?

  1. 0.3 MPa
  2. 3.3 MPa
  3. 300 MPa
  4. 1920 MPa
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Engineering stress is \(\sigma=F/A_0\), using the original area. Newtons divided by square millimetres gives megapascals directly, which is the least error-prone route: \(\sigma=24{,}000\text{ N}/80\text{ mm}^2=300\text{ N/mm}^2=300\) MPa. Sanity check: structural metals yield somewhere in the range of a few hundred MPa, so 300 MPa is a plausible working number, whereas 0.3 MPa would be almost no load at all. Option D comes from multiplying instead of dividing. Design meaning: 300 MPa only becomes useful once compared with the yield strength of the material, because that comparison is what tells you whether the bar springs back.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

A shelf bracket is failing its deflection limit — it visibly sags under load but always returns to shape when unloaded. Alloy P has \(E=70\) GPa and \(\sigma_y=500\) MPa. Alloy Q has \(E=210\) GPa and \(\sigma_y=250\) MPa. Which alloy addresses the problem, and on what grounds?

  1. Alloy P, because its higher yield strength resists deformation better
  2. Alloy Q, because deflection is governed by stiffness and its \(E\) is three times higher
  3. Either, because both have adequate strength for the load
  4. Neither, because deflection cannot be changed by material choice
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. The bracket returns to shape, which proves the stress never reached yield — so this is an elastic deflection problem and yield strength is irrelevant to it. Elastic deflection is set by Young's modulus \(E\), the slope of the elastic line, and alloy Q is three times stiffer at 210 GPa against 70 GPa. Doubling the yield strength as option A suggests would change nothing about how far the bracket moves. Why the trap works: "sags too much" reads as a strength complaint, and P has the bigger strength number. The distinction to hold onto is that stiffness resists deflection while strength resists permanent damage, and the two are independent. Design meaning: the cheaper fix is often not a material change at all — deepening the section raises the second moment of area and cuts deflection without touching the alloy.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: the 0.2% offset line for yield is drawn from a strain of 0.2 on the strain axis.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. It is drawn from a strain of 0.002. The "0.2%" is a percentage, and converting it to the fraction the strain axis actually uses means dividing by 100. A strain of 0.2 would be a 20% permanent stretch, which on most metals is close to fracture rather than to the start of yielding. Sanity check: the offset is meant to represent a small amount of permanent set — small enough that the part is still usable — so any offset that lands near the far right of the curve must be wrong by a factor of 100.
Q6 · True/falseTrap

True or false: a heat treatment that doubles the yield strength of a steel while leaving its stiffness unchanged will roughly quadruple its modulus of resilience.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Resilience is the elastic triangle under the curve, \(U_r=\sigma_y^2/(2E)\). Yield strength appears squared, so doubling \(\sigma_y\) at constant \(E\) multiplies \(U_r\) by \(2^2=4\). The physical picture agrees: raising yield lets the material travel further up the same elastic line before deforming permanently, so both the height and the base of the triangle grow. Why this looks like a trap: most heat-treatment statements in this subject are false because they claim stiffness changes too — \(E\) is set by bonding and barely moves with heat treatment. Here stiffness is explicitly held fixed, so the squared relationship is allowed to do its work. Design meaning: this is exactly why spring steels are heat treated hard. A spring is designed on resilience, and resilience rewards yield strength quadratically.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

An aluminium tie rod has \(A_0=150\) mm², \(L_0=200\) mm, \(E=70\) GPa and \(\sigma_y=410\) MPa. It carries 54 kN. Find the engineering stress, the elastic strain and the extension in millimetres, then state whether the rod returns to length when the load is removed.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(\sigma=360\) MPa, \(\varepsilon=0.0051\), extension \(\approx1.03\) mm, and yes it springs back.
1. Stress: \(\sigma=F/A_0=54{,}000\text{ N}/150\text{ mm}^2=360\text{ N/mm}^2=360\) MPa.
2. Check against yield first, because the next step is only valid below yield: \(360<410\) MPa, so the rod is still elastic.
3. Strain from Hooke's law \(\sigma=E\varepsilon\): \(\varepsilon=\sigma/E=360\text{ MPa}/70{,}000\text{ MPa}=0.00514\). Keeping both quantities in MPa avoids any \(10^9\) slip.
4. Extension: \(\Delta L=\varepsilon L_0=0.00514\times200\text{ mm}=1.03\) mm.
5. Because the stress never reached yield, all of that 1.03 mm is elastic and disappears on unloading.
Sanity check: a strain of about 0.005 is typical of a metal working near its elastic limit — elastic strains in metals are always a fraction of a percent, so an answer of 0.5 or 0.00005 would signal a unit error. Note also that the same load in steel (\(E=210\) GPa) would give a third of the extension, which is the trend you should expect from a three-times stiffer material.
Design meaning: the rod is safe against permanent deformation with a margin of \(410/360=1.14\), which is thin. If the load estimate is uncertain, that margin is the number to argue about, not the extension.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A spring steel has \(\sigma_y=350\) MPa and \(E=200\) GPa. Calculate its modulus of resilience in J/m³. A second grade has the same \(E\) but \(\sigma_y=700\) MPa — state its resilience and explain the ratio without a second full calculation.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(U_r\approx3.06\times10^5\) J/m³, and the second grade gives \(1.22\times10^6\) J/m³ — four times as much.
1. Resilience is the area of the elastic triangle: \(U_r=\sigma_y^2/(2E)\).
2. Work in base SI units so the answer comes out in joules per cubic metre: \(\sigma_y=350\times10^6\) Pa and \(E=200\times10^9\) Pa.
3. Substitute: \(U_r=(350\times10^6)^2/(2\times200\times10^9)=1.225\times10^{17}/4.00\times10^{11}=3.06\times10^5\) J/m³.
4. For the second grade, only \(\sigma_y\) changed and it appears squared, so \(U_r\) scales by \((700/350)^2=4\): \(4\times3.06\times10^5=1.22\times10^6\) J/m³.
Sanity check: the trend must be an increase, because a higher yield strength lets the material store more energy before it stops springing back — and the increase must be more than proportional because of the square. Spotting that only one term varies is the whole skill in part two; recomputing from scratch wastes exam time.
Design meaning: resilience is energy per cubic metre that you get back, which is what a spring is for. Note that it says nothing about toughness — the whole area to fracture — so the stronger grade could still be the one that shatters first under an impact.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Two engineering stress–strain curves are plotted on the same axes. Curve A rises very steeply, peaks at 900 MPa and fractures at a strain of 0.02. Curve B rises about a third as steeply, peaks at 450 MPa and fractures at a strain of 0.28. Identify which material is stiffer, which is stronger, and which is tougher, and choose one for a vehicle crash structure.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A is both stiffer and stronger, B is far tougher, and B is the crash structure material.
Stiffness is the slope of the straight first part of the curve, which is Young's modulus \(E\). A is roughly three times steeper, so A is about three times stiffer and will deflect a third as much under the same stress.
Strength is how high the curve reaches. A peaks at 900 MPa against B's 450 MPa, so A has double the ultimate tensile strength (UTS) — the highest stress the curve ever attains.
Toughness is the entire area under the curve to fracture, and that is where the ranking reverses. A rough rectangular estimate for A gives an area of order \(900\text{ MPa}\times0.02=18\) MPa, while B gives \(450\text{ MPa}\times0.28=126\) MPa — roughly seven times more energy absorbed per unit volume. A tall narrow curve can enclose far less area than a short wide one.
Choice for a crash structure: B. A crash structure exists to absorb kinetic energy while deforming, which is precisely area under the curve, not curve height. B also stretches 14 times further before separating, so it deforms visibly and progressively instead of shattering — the structure gets to do its job over a usable crush distance.
The trap to avoid: answering "A, because it is stronger". Strong and tough are different quantities read off different features of the same picture, and the crash question asks for the second one.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A bolt in a lifting fixture sees a maximum service stress of 190 MPa. The material has \(\sigma_y=480\) MPa and UTS \(=620\) MPa. Calculate the safety factor, explain what the number is covering for, and say why a factor quoted against the UTS instead would be misleading.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(N\approx2.5\) against yield; the same calculation against UTS would flatter the design to 3.3 while allowing permanent deformation.
1. Safety factor is \(N=\sigma_y/\sigma_{\text{working}}=480\text{ MPa}/190\text{ MPa}=2.53\). The units cancel, as they must — \(N\) is a ratio.
2. What it covers for: everything the single working-stress number cannot see. How accurately the service load is known; how much the material varies from batch to batch; whether temperature, corrosion or wear will degrade it over time; and what the consequence of failure is. A factor of 2.5 might be ample in a well-instrumented indoor rig and clearly inadequate in a lifting fixture, where a failure drops a load on people.
3. Why UTS is the wrong denominator: \(620/190=3.3\) looks like a more comfortable design, but the ultimate tensile strength is the peak of the curve, and a part loaded to its UTS has already yielded badly and started to neck. Yield is the design limit because it marks the end of springing back — beyond it the bolt is permanently stretched, the fixture no longer holds its geometry, and preload is lost even though nothing has broken.
Sanity check on the direction: UTS is always above yield on a ductile metal, so a UTS-based factor is always the larger and always the more flattering number. If a quoted safety factor seems generous, check which strength it was divided by.
What a full-mark answer adds: the observation that a safety factor is a judgement wrapped in a number. Computing 2.5 and stopping leaves out the part that carries the marks — saying what the margin is protecting against.
