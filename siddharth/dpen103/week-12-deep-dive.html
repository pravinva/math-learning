Ceramics

Ceramics are the materials that break the rules learned so far. Enormously strong in compression, unreliable in tension, and with a strength that is not really a property of the material at all — it is a property of the largest flaw that happens to be in the piece you are holding.

What this week is really asking
How big is the worst flaw, and is this part in tension?

The whole week follows from one fact: ceramics have no easy dislocation movement, so they cannot yield. With no way to blunt a crack tip by deforming, every flaw stays sharp and stress concentrates there until it fractures.

Where this sits. This is card 9b of the core map. It applies the Week 4 fracture equation to materials where it dominates completely, and follows from the locked ionic and covalent bonding of Week 1. Textbook sections: Callister Ch12, selected sections.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Why ceramics differ
Brittle—Breaks with essentially no plastic deformation and no warning.
Flaw-controlled—Strength is set by the largest defect present rather than by the bulk material. Two identical-looking samples can differ substantially.
Scatter—The spread in measured strength between nominally identical specimens. Large for ceramics, small for metals.
Weibull modulus, \(m\)—A number describing how much that scatter there is. Higher means more consistent and more predictable.
Flexural strength—Strength measured by bending rather than pulling, because gripping a ceramic in tension tends to break it in the grips.
Carried over from Week 4
\(K_{IC}\)fracture toughnessThe limit on stress intensity. Very low for ceramics — often under 5 MPa·√m against 50 or more for steel.
\(a\)flaw sizeThe size of the worst defect. For a ceramic this is usually a surface scratch or machining chip.
\(Y\)geometry factorA dimensionless shape factor, generally near 1.
Design responses
Proof testing—Deliberately loading every part above its service stress. Anything with a dangerous flaw breaks during the test rather than in service.
Compressive prestress—Building compression into the surface so an applied tension must first cancel it. Toughened glass works this way.
Thermal shock—Cracking from rapid temperature change, worsened by low conductivity and no ductility.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 Why ceramics are brittle

Metals bend because dislocations move. In a ceramic, the ionic and covalent bonds are directional and locked, so there is no easy dislocation motion — and no way to relieve stress except by cracking.

Recall from Week 3 that a metal facing a stress concentration deforms locally, which blunts the crack tip and spreads the load. That is the safety valve. Ceramics do not have it.

In an ionic crystal, sliding a plane would push like charges next to like charges, which is energetically prohibitive. In a covalent one, the bonds point in fixed directions and cannot be rearranged. Either way there is no low-energy way for planes to slip past each other.

So stress at a flaw tip keeps rising until the bonds there simply break, and once the crack starts moving there is nothing to stop it. Failure is instantaneous and complete, with no deformation beforehand to warn anyone.

Feeds: the flaw-sensitivity argument, which is the whole of the rest of the week.
Say it cold: "Ceramics have no easy dislocation movement, so they cannot blunt a crack tip, and stress at a flaw rises until it fractures."

2 Strength is a property of the flaw

The same equation as Week 4, but for ceramics it is not one consideration among several — it is the entire strength calculation.

\[\sigma_f\approx\frac{K_{IC}}{Y\sqrt{\pi a}}\]

\(\sigma_f\) is the fracture strength, \(K_{IC}\) the fracture toughness of the material and \(a\) the size of the largest flaw.

Read what this says. Strength depends on \(a\) — a property of that particular specimen, not of the material. Change the surface finish and you change the strength, without touching the chemistry.

That is why quoting a single tensile strength for a ceramic is misleading. Measure twenty nominally identical bars and you get twenty different answers, spread widely, because each contains a different worst flaw. The Weibull modulus is how that spread gets reported.

It also explains why machining a ceramic is dangerous. Grinding leaves surface damage, and every chip is a potential largest-flaw. A ceramic part is often at its strongest before anyone finishes it.

Feeds: the design responses below, all of which are about controlling \(a\) rather than improving the material.
Say it cold: "Ceramic strength is fracture toughness over the root of the worst flaw size, so finish and handling change the strength."

3 Tension versus compression

A ceramic can be superb in compression and unreliable in tension, and the reason is entirely about what each does to a crack.

Tension pulls a crack open. The two faces separate, stress concentrates at the tip, and the crack runs. Compression pushes the faces together, so the crack cannot open and cannot propagate in the same way.

The practical result is that ceramic compressive strength is often ten times or more its tensile strength — a ratio with no real equivalent in metals, where the two are broadly similar.

This is why concrete is reinforced with steel: concrete handles compression well and tension badly, so the steel is placed exactly where tension occurs. It is also why arches are built from stone. The whole geometry exists to keep the material in compression.

So the first question about any ceramic part is not what its strength is. It is whether the part is in tension anywhere — including from bending, thermal gradients or bolt preload.

Feeds: the design-response card, and structural questions in Weeks 8 and 13.
Say it cold: "Tension opens cracks and compression closes them, so ceramics are perhaps ten times stronger in compression."

4 Designing around brittleness

You cannot make a ceramic ductile. Every practical response instead reduces the flaw size, avoids tension, or removes the bad parts before they reach service.

  • Improve the surface finish. Smaller worst-flaw, higher strength. Polishing a ceramic genuinely strengthens it, which is not true of a metal.
  • Keep it in compression. Design the geometry so service loads never put the material in tension. Arches, and prestressed concrete.
  • Build in compressive surface stress. Toughened glass has a compressed skin, so an applied tension must first cancel that before the surface feels any tension at all.
  • Generous radii. Sharp internal corners concentrate stress on a material with no way to yield and redistribute it.
  • Proof test. Load every part above its service stress. Anything containing a dangerous flaw fails in the test rig, and the survivors are proven.
  • Use a statistical allowable. Design to a low percentile of the strength distribution, not the average, because the average has a 50% failure rate by definition.

Notice how few of these are material changes. Almost all are process, geometry or quality control, which is the real lesson of ceramic design: with a brittle material, process control is mechanical property control.

Feeds: Week 13, where a ceramic question is nearly always about the design response rather than the number.
Say it cold: "Improve finish, stay in compression, prestress the surface, avoid sharp corners, proof test, and design to a low percentile."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — flaw size and strength

Given: A ceramic component where the worst flaw grows from \(a\) to \(4a\), perhaps through machining damage.
  1. \(\sigma_f\propto1/\sqrt a\) from the fracture relation.
  2. A fourfold flaw scales strength by \(1/\sqrt4=1/2\).
  3. The usable tensile strength halves.
  4. The chemistry, the density and the fracture toughness are all unchanged.
What it means: The material did not get worse; the part did. This is the single most important idea about ceramics, and it is why handling and finishing procedures are treated as seriously as material specification.

Worked example 2 — reacting to a process problem

Edge chipping is observed after machining a batch of ceramic parts. What follows?

  1. Chips are surface flaws, so the effective worst-flaw size \(a\) has increased.
  2. Since \(\sigma_f\propto1/\sqrt a\), the allowable tensile stress for this batch has dropped.
  3. Immediate action: reduce the allowable stress for the affected batch until the process is corrected — do not keep using the original design value.
  4. Corrective action: improve the finishing operation, add an edge radius rather than a sharp corner, and consider a surface treatment that leaves compression.
  5. Verification: proof test the batch so any part with a critical flaw fails in the rig rather than in service.
What it means: The examinable pattern is to connect an observed process defect to a quantified strength consequence, and then to both an immediate and a permanent action.

Worked example 3 — the doubling case

If surface flaw size doubles, what happens to allowable tensile stress?

  1. \(\sigma_f\propto1/\sqrt a\).
  2. Doubling \(a\) scales it by \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.707\).
  3. So allowable stress falls to about 71% of its previous value.
What it means: Same square-root pattern as the crack growth in Week 4. Doubling gives 0.71 and quadrupling gives 0.50 — two anchors worth having memorised.

Where marks get lost

  • Quoting one tensile strength for a ceramic. It scatters with flaw size. Give a range, or a statistical allowable, and say why.
  • Using a compressive strength figure for a part in tension. They can differ by a factor of ten or more.
  • Forgetting that machining reduces strength. Grinding leaves surface damage, and surface damage sets \(a\).
  • Designing to average strength. By definition half the parts fall below it. Use a low percentile.
  • Missing hidden tension. Bending, thermal gradients and bolt preload all put ceramics in tension even when the applied load looks compressive.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open. An answer you can only recognise is not an answer you can write in an exam.

1. Why can two ceramic batches of identical composition show different measured strengths?
Because ceramic strength is flaw-controlled: \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), where \(a\) is the largest flaw in that specimen. Different surface finish, machining or handling produces a different worst-flaw size, so measured strength scatters widely even with identical chemistry. The Weibull modulus quantifies that scatter.
2. If surface flaw size doubles, what happens to allowable tensile stress?
Allowable stress scales as \(1/\sqrt a\), so doubling the flaw gives \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.707\) — about 71% of the previous value, a 29% reduction. A fourfold flaw would halve it.
3. Give two practical ways to improve ceramic reliability in tension-sensitive service.
Improve surface finish and handling to reduce the largest flaw size, since strength is set by that flaw. And redesign the geometry to reduce stress concentration — generous fillet radii instead of sharp corners — or better, to keep the material in compression. Building in compressive surface stress and proof testing every part are two further options.
4. Why are ceramics so much stronger in compression than in tension?
Because failure is crack propagation from an existing flaw. Tension pulls crack faces apart, concentrating stress at the tip and driving the crack. Compression pushes them together so the crack cannot open. With no dislocation motion available to blunt the tip, the tensile case is entirely flaw-limited while the compressive case is not — giving a strength ratio of ten or more.
5. Why does proof testing make sense for ceramics but not usually for ductile metals?
Because ceramic strength is governed by the worst flaw in each individual part and scatters widely, so batch statistics cannot guarantee any particular part. Loading every part above its service stress makes the ones with critical flaws fail in the rig, leaving survivors demonstrated to be flaw-free at that stress. Ductile metals are far more consistent and yield rather than shatter, so a sample-based approach is adequate.
