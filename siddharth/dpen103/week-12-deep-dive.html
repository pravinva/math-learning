Ceramics

Ceramics are the materials that break the rules learned so far. Enormously strong in compression, unreliable in tension, and with a strength that is not really a property of the material at all — it is a property of the largest flaw that happens to be in the piece you are holding.

Working through the week

This section develops the week's ideas in order — from first principles through to how they are used in engineering practice.

Mechanical behaviour of ceramics and glasses

Ceramics and glasses are strong in compression but weak in tension. Their ionic and covalent bonds resist compression well, but a tensile stress opens microcracks and allows rapid propagation. Without mobile dislocations to blunt a crack tip, fracture occurs at stresses far below the theoretical strength of a flawless crystal.

Measured tensile strength therefore depends on the size of the largest flaw present — surface scratches, pores, or inclusions — rather than on bulk chemistry alone. Handling, machining and environmental exposure all influence the flaw population and hence the strength of a given part.

Griffith's approach to brittle fracture

Griffith showed that the condition for a crack to propagate is that the release of elastic strain energy exceeds the energy required to create new fracture surfaces. This leads to the practical estimate:

\[\sigma_f \approx \frac{K_{Ic}}{Y\sqrt{\pi a}}\]

where \(K_{Ic}\) is fracture toughness, \(a\) is flaw size and \(Y\) is a geometry factor. Larger flaws lower the failure stress. Surface finish and proof testing — loading to a level that eliminates weak specimens — are standard strategies for ceramic components.

Design keeps ceramics in compression where possible, avoids sharp corners that concentrate stress, and accounts for scatter in strength by designing to a low percentile of the measured distribution.

Thermal shock in brittle materials

Rapid heating or cooling sets up temperature gradients within a component. Low thermal conductivity allows steep gradients; thermal expansion mismatch and restraint generate stress; and the inability to deform plastically means that stress cannot be relieved by yielding. When the thermal stress exceeds the material's strength, thermal shock fracture results.

Thermal shock resistance improves with higher thermal conductivity (flatter gradients), lower thermal expansion coefficient (smaller strain for a given \(\Delta T\)), and higher fracture toughness. Sudden quenching of a hot ceramic vessel illustrates the failure mode: the cold surface contracts while the interior remains hot, placing the surface in tension.

The ideas, one card at a time

1 Why ceramics are brittle

Metals bend because dislocations move. In a ceramic, the ionic and covalent bonds are directional and locked, so there is no easy dislocation motion — and no way to relieve stress except by cracking.

Recall from Week 3 that a metal facing a stress concentration deforms locally, which blunts the crack tip and spreads the load. That is the safety valve. Ceramics do not have it.

In an ionic crystal, sliding a plane would push like charges next to like charges, which is energetically prohibitive. In a covalent one, the bonds point in fixed directions and cannot be rearranged. Either way there is no low-energy way for planes to slip past each other.

So stress at a flaw tip keeps rising until the bonds there simply break, and once the crack starts moving there is nothing to stop it. Failure is instantaneous and complete, with no deformation beforehand to warn anyone.

Feeds: the flaw-sensitivity argument, which is the whole of the rest of the week.
Say it cold: "Ceramics have no easy dislocation movement, so they cannot blunt a crack tip, and stress at a flaw rises until it fractures."

2 Strength is a property of the flaw

The same equation as Week 4, but for ceramics it is not one consideration among several — it is the entire strength calculation.

\[\sigma_f\approx\frac{K_{IC}}{Y\sqrt{\pi a}}\]

\(\sigma_f\) is the fracture strength, \(K_{IC}\) the fracture toughness of the material and \(a\) the size of the largest flaw.

Read what this says. Strength depends on \(a\) — a property of that particular specimen, not of the material. Change the surface finish and you change the strength, without touching the chemistry.

That is why quoting a single tensile strength for a ceramic is misleading. Measure twenty nominally identical bars and you get twenty different answers, spread widely, because each contains a different worst flaw. The Weibull modulus is how that spread gets reported.

It also explains why machining a ceramic is dangerous. Grinding leaves surface damage, and every chip is a potential largest-flaw. A ceramic part is often at its strongest before anyone finishes it.

Feeds: the design responses below, all of which are about controlling \(a\) rather than improving the material.
Say it cold: "Ceramic strength is fracture toughness over the root of the worst flaw size, so finish and handling change the strength."

3 Tension versus compression

A ceramic can be superb in compression and unreliable in tension, and the reason is entirely about what each does to a crack.

Tension pulls a crack open. The two faces separate, stress concentrates at the tip, and the crack runs. Compression pushes the faces together, so the crack cannot open and cannot propagate in the same way.

The practical result is that ceramic compressive strength is often ten times or more its tensile strength — a ratio with no real equivalent in metals, where the two are broadly similar.

This is why concrete is reinforced with steel: concrete handles compression well and tension badly, so the steel is placed exactly where tension occurs. It is also why arches are built from stone. The whole geometry exists to keep the material in compression.

So the first question about any ceramic part is not what its strength is. It is whether the part is in tension anywhere — including from bending, thermal gradients or bolt preload.

Feeds: the design-response card, and structural questions in Weeks 8 and 13.
Say it cold: "Tension opens cracks and compression closes them, so ceramics are perhaps ten times stronger in compression."

4 Designing around brittleness

You cannot make a ceramic ductile. Every practical response instead reduces the flaw size, avoids tension, or removes the bad parts before they reach service.

  • Improve the surface finish. Smaller worst-flaw, higher strength. Polishing a ceramic genuinely strengthens it, which is not true of a metal.
  • Keep it in compression. Design the geometry so service loads never put the material in tension. Arches, and prestressed concrete.
  • Build in compressive surface stress. Toughened glass has a compressed skin, so an applied tension must first cancel that before the surface feels any tension at all.
  • Generous radii. Sharp internal corners concentrate stress on a material with no way to yield and redistribute it.
  • Proof test. Load every part above its service stress. Anything containing a dangerous flaw fails in the test rig, and the survivors are proven.
  • Use a statistical allowable. Design to a low percentile of the strength distribution, not the average, because the average has a 50% failure rate by definition.

Notice how few of these are material changes. Almost all are process, geometry or quality control, which is the real lesson of ceramic design: with a brittle material, process control is mechanical property control.

Feeds: Week 13, where a ceramic question is nearly always about the design response rather than the number.
Say it cold: "Improve finish, stay in compression, prestress the surface, avoid sharp corners, proof test, and design to a low percentile."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — flaw size and strength

Given: A ceramic component where the worst flaw grows from \(a\) to \(4a\), perhaps through machining damage.
  1. \(\sigma_f\propto1/\sqrt a\) from the fracture relation.
  2. A fourfold flaw scales strength by \(1/\sqrt4=1/2\).
  3. The usable tensile strength halves.
  4. The chemistry, the density and the fracture toughness are all unchanged.
What it means: The material did not get worse; the part did. This is the single most important idea about ceramics, and it is why handling and finishing procedures are treated as seriously as material specification.

Worked example 2 — reacting to a process problem

Edge chipping is observed after machining a batch of ceramic parts. What follows?

  1. Chips are surface flaws, so the effective worst-flaw size \(a\) has increased.
  2. Since \(\sigma_f\propto1/\sqrt a\), the allowable tensile stress for this batch has dropped.
  3. Immediate action: reduce the allowable stress for the affected batch until the process is corrected — do not keep using the original design value.
  4. Corrective action: improve the finishing operation, add an edge radius rather than a sharp corner, and consider a surface treatment that leaves compression.
  5. Verification: proof test the batch so any part with a critical flaw fails in the rig rather than in service.
What it means: The examinable pattern is to connect an observed process defect to a quantified strength consequence, and then to both an immediate and a permanent action.

Worked example 3 — the doubling case

If surface flaw size doubles, what happens to allowable tensile stress?

  1. \(\sigma_f\propto1/\sqrt a\).
  2. Doubling \(a\) scales it by \(1/\sqrt2\approx0.707\).
  3. So allowable stress falls to about 71% of its previous value.
What it means: Same square-root pattern as the crack growth in Week 4. Doubling gives 0.71 and quadrupling gives 0.50 — two anchors worth having memorised.

Where marks get lost

  • Quoting one tensile strength for a ceramic. It scatters with flaw size. Give a range, or a statistical allowable, and say why.
  • Using a compressive strength figure for a part in tension. They can differ by a factor of ten or more.
  • Forgetting that machining reduces strength. Grinding leaves surface damage, and surface damage sets \(a\).
  • Designing to average strength. By definition half the parts fall below it. Use a low percentile.
  • Missing hidden tension. Bending, thermal gradients and bolt preload all put ceramics in tension even when the applied load looks compressive.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

What is the fundamental reason ceramics cannot yield before they fracture?

  1. They contain no flaws for a crack to start from
  2. Their ionic and covalent bonds are directional and locked, so there is no easy dislocation motion
  3. Their Young's modulus is too low to support plastic flow
  4. They are always used at temperatures above their melting point
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Metals deform because dislocations glide on close-packed planes at modest stress. In an ionic crystal, sliding a plane would bring like charges next to like charges, which is energetically prohibitive; in a covalent one, the bonds point in fixed directions and cannot be rearranged. Either way there is no low-energy route for planes to slip past one another. Option A is the reverse of the truth — ceramics are full of flaws, and that is the whole problem. Option C confuses stiffness with strength: ceramic moduli are typically high, not low. Design meaning: the missing safety valve is what matters. A metal facing a stress concentration yields locally, blunting the crack tip and spreading load. A ceramic cannot, so stress at a flaw tip climbs until the bonds break, and failure arrives suddenly and completely with no deformation to warn anyone.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

Ceramic strength is commonly reported as a flexural (bending) strength rather than a tensile strength. Why?

  1. Ceramics are only ever loaded in bending in service
  2. Bending produces no tension anywhere in the specimen
  3. Gripping a brittle specimen for a tensile test tends to break it in the grips, so the test measures the fixture rather than the material
  4. Flexural strength is numerically identical to tensile strength, so either can be used
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. A tensile test needs the specimen clamped or threaded, and those features introduce local stress concentrations and contact damage in a material that cannot yield to relieve them. Failure then initiates in the grip region and the number obtained describes the fixture, not the material. A three- or four-point bend test loads the specimen through simple contacts and puts the peak tension on a free surface where it can be seen. Option B is wrong and important: bending places the far surface squarely in tension, which is exactly why the test works. Option A is untrue, and option D overstates the case, since the two values differ because different volumes of material are exposed to peak stress. Design meaning: always check which test a quoted ceramic strength came from before comparing two materials or using the value in a calculation.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A ceramic has fracture toughness \(K_{IC}=3.5\) MPa·√m and a worst surface flaw of \(a=25\) µm, with geometry factor \(Y=1\). Using \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), what is its fracture strength?

  1. About 40 MPa
  2. About 125 MPa
  3. About 395 MPa
  4. About 1250 MPa
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. Work inside the root first: \(\pi a=\pi\times25\times10^{-6}=7.854\times10^{-5}\) m, and \(\sqrt{7.854\times10^{-5}}=8.86\times10^{-3}\) √m. Then \(\sigma_f=3.5/(1\times8.86\times10^{-3})=395\) MPa. Unit check: MPa·√m ÷ √m leaves MPa, as it must. Sanity check: a few hundred MPa is the right order for a ceramic with a small, well-controlled surface flaw, and it should be far below the compressive strength of the same material. Option D is what you get by forgetting to take the square root; option A comes from leaving \(a\) in micrometres. Design meaning: this number belongs to this specimen with this flaw. Grind the surface more roughly and the same material will give a much lower value.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

A ceramic seal ring has a quoted compressive strength of 2000 MPa. It is clamped between two flanges by bolts tightened onto its face, and the flanges deflect slightly under internal pressure. What is the main concern?

  1. None — the loading is compressive, which is where ceramics are strongest
  2. Bolt preload and flange deflection put parts of the ring into bending, and therefore into tension, where its strength is perhaps a tenth of the quoted figure
  3. The compressive strength figure will be too low once the bolts are tightened
  4. The ring will yield and lose its shape under the preload
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. The trap is accepting "compressive loading" at face value. Bending always produces tension on the convex face, and a flange that deflects imposes bending on anything clamped between its faces; uneven bolt preload does the same locally. Ceramic compressive strength is often ten times or more its tensile strength — a ratio with no real equivalent in metals — so the governing number here is the tensile one, and the 2000 MPa figure is close to irrelevant. Option D describes a metal: ceramics do not yield, they fracture. Design meaning: the first question about any ceramic part is not what its strength is, it is whether the part goes into tension anywhere — including from bending, thermal gradients or bolt preload. Then design the joint to keep it compressive: a compliant gasket, a spherical or self-aligning seat, controlled torque, and generous radii instead of sharp corners.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: Polishing a ceramic bar can genuinely raise its measured tensile strength, whereas polishing a mild steel bar has little effect on its yield strength.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. Ceramic strength follows \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), where \(a\) is the largest flaw present — and in a ceramic that flaw is almost always a surface scratch or machining chip. Polishing removes the deepest scratches, reduces \(a\), and genuinely raises strength without any change to chemistry, density or fracture toughness. In a ductile metal, yielding is governed by dislocation motion through the bulk microstructure, so surface finish barely shifts the yield strength — though it does matter for fatigue, where cracks initiate at the surface. Design meaning: for a brittle material, process control is mechanical property control. Surface finish, handling and grinding parameters belong on the drawing alongside the material specification, and a ceramic part is often at its strongest before anyone finishes it.
Q6 · True/falseOne step

True or false: A higher Weibull modulus \(m\) means the ceramic is stronger on average.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. The Weibull modulus describes the scatter in measured strength between nominally identical specimens, not the average level. A high \(m\) means a narrow distribution — consistent, predictable, tightly controlled flaw population — while a low \(m\) means a wide spread. Two ceramics can share the same mean strength with very different values of \(m\). Why it still matters enormously: you design to a low percentile of the distribution, not to the mean, because by definition half of all parts fall below the mean. A material with a high \(m\) lets you design much closer to its average strength, so the usable allowable stress is higher even with no improvement in the average. Design meaning: for a brittle material, consistency is worth as much as strength, and it is bought through process control rather than through chemistry.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

Machining changes are suspected of increasing the worst surface flaw in a ceramic component from 20 µm to 45 µm. By what fraction does the allowable tensile stress change, and what should be done about the affected batch?

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: allowable stress falls to about 67% of its previous value — a reduction of roughly one third.
1. Governing relation. \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), so with the same material and geometry \(\sigma_f\propto1/\sqrt a\). Note that \(K_{IC}\), the fracture toughness, has not changed — the material is identical.
2. Ratio. \(\sigma_{f,\text{new}}/\sigma_{f,\text{old}}=\sqrt{a_{\text{old}}/a_{\text{new}}}=\sqrt{20/45}=\sqrt{0.4444}=0.667\).
3. Interpret. Allowable tensile stress is 67% of its former value, a 33% loss, from a flaw that grew by 25 µm — a quarter the thickness of a sheet of paper.
4. Sanity check against known anchors. Doubling the flaw gives \(1/\sqrt2=0.707\) and quadrupling gives 0.500. A 2.25-fold increase should land between those two, and 0.667 does. The square-root dependence also means the answer must fall more slowly than the flaw grows, which it does.
5. Immediate action. Reduce the allowable stress for the affected batch to the new value rather than continuing with the original design figure, or quarantine the batch. The design did not change, but the parts did.
6. Corrective action. Fix the finishing operation, add an edge radius rather than a sharp corner, and consider a treatment that leaves the surface in compression so any applied tension must first cancel it.
7. Verification and design meaning. Proof test the batch — load every part above its service stress so anything carrying a critical flaw fails in the rig rather than in service. The pattern worth carrying into the exam is to connect an observed process defect to a quantified strength consequence, then to both an immediate and a permanent action.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A ceramic part with \(K_{IC}=3.5\) MPa·√m and \(Y=1\) must carry a peak tensile stress of 150 MPa with a safety factor of 2. What is the largest surface flaw that can be tolerated, and how does that translate into a manufacturing requirement?

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: the worst flaw must stay below about 43 µm, which is a surface-finish and inspection specification rather than a material one.
1. Required fracture strength. A safety factor of 2 on a 150 MPa peak tension means the part must not fracture below \(\sigma_f=2\times150=300\) MPa.
2. Rearrange for flaw size. From \(\sigma_f=K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\), square both sides and solve: \(a=\left(K_{IC}/(Y\sigma_f)\right)^2/\pi\).
3. Substitute in consistent units. \(K_{IC}/\sigma_f=3.5/300=1.1667\times10^{-2}\) √m.
4. Square and divide. \((1.1667\times10^{-2})^2=1.361\times10^{-4}\) m, then \(a=1.361\times10^{-4}/\pi=4.33\times10^{-5}\) m \(=43\) µm.
5. Sanity check. Q3 showed that a 25 µm flaw in this same material gives 395 MPa. We are asking for less strength than that, 300 MPa, so the tolerable flaw must be larger than 25 µm — and 43 µm is. The direction of the answer confirms the algebra.
6. Trend check on the sensitivity. Because \(\sigma_f\propto1/\sqrt a\), the safety factor of 2 on stress corresponds to a factor of 4 on flaw size. That is the real reassurance the factor buys, and it is worth stating explicitly: doubling the stress margin quadruples the flaw margin.
7. Design meaning. The specification is not "use a stronger ceramic" — it is "guarantee no surface flaw exceeds 43 µm". That converts into a maximum surface roughness and grinding procedure, an edge-radius requirement, handling and packaging controls to prevent chipping after finishing, an inspection method capable of resolving flaws well below 43 µm, and ideally a proof test at 300 MPa so any part carrying a larger flaw eliminates itself. With a brittle material the mechanical property is bought on the shop floor.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

Two stress–strain curves are shown on the same axes. Curve M rises linearly, bends over at a clear yield point, extends a long way at nearly constant stress, then falls to fracture at a strain of about 0.25. Curve C rises linearly to a higher stress and stops abruptly at a strain of about 0.001, with no curvature at all. Beside Curve C, three faint lines are drawn showing three nominally identical specimens stopping at noticeably different stresses. Interpret the figure.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: Curve M is a ductile metal, Curve C is a ceramic, and the three faint lines illustrate flaw-controlled scatter — which is why quoting a single tensile strength for a ceramic is misleading.
Curve M. The yield point, the long plastic plateau and the 25% fracture strain are all signatures of dislocation motion. The area under the curve is large, meaning high toughness — a genuine capacity to absorb energy — and the plateau is the safety valve: the material deforms visibly before it breaks, giving warning, and it yields locally at a stress concentration to blunt a crack tip and spread load.
Curve C. A straight line to fracture with no curvature means no plastic deformation at all, and 0.001 strain is roughly 250 times less extension than the metal manages. The higher peak stress with a tiny area under the curve is the key contrast: stiff and strong in this test, but with almost no energy absorption. Failure is instantaneous and complete with no warning.
The three faint lines. They are the most informative feature. Identical chemistry cannot give identical strength in a ceramic, because \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\) depends on \(a\), the largest flaw in that specimen. Each bar contains a different worst flaw from its own surface finish and handling history, so each fails at a different stress. Strength is behaving as a property of the specimen, not of the material, and the Weibull modulus is how that spread gets reported.
What the figure cannot tell you. Both curves are single monotonic pulls at one temperature, so neither says anything about fatigue, creep, corrosion or thermal shock. For the ceramic in particular there is no compressive information here, and its compressive strength could be ten times the value shown.
Design meaning. Do not design a ceramic part to the average of a scatter band — half the parts fall below it by definition. Use a low percentile of the distribution, keep the part out of tension where possible, control the surface finish that sets \(a\), and accept that with no plastic reserve there is no warning before failure, so proof testing or generous margin has to do the job that ductility does in a metal.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A ceramic furnace liner survives steady operation at 900 °C but cracks when the furnace door is opened and cold air reaches it. Explain the mechanism and recommend design responses.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: this is thermal shock. Rapid surface cooling makes the surface try to contract while the hot interior holds it stretched, which puts the surface into tension — the one loading state a ceramic cannot tolerate.
1. Classify. The failure is triggered by a rapid temperature change, not by the temperature level, since steady operation at 900 °C was fine. So the driver is a thermal gradient, not thermal capability.
2. Mechanism. Cold air chills the surface first. The chilled layer wants to contract, but the still-hot interior beneath it has not contracted and physically prevents it. The surface is therefore held in tension by its own substrate — no external load is required at all.
3. Why a ceramic in particular fails. Two properties compound. Ceramics have low thermal conductivity, so heat leaves the surface far faster than it can be replenished from the interior, which makes the gradient steep and the mismatch large. And with no dislocation motion available, the material cannot yield to relieve the stress, so it goes straight to cracking from whatever surface flaw happens to be largest, following \(\sigma_f\approx K_{IC}/(Y\sqrt{\pi a})\).
4. Trend check. Everything that increases the gradient makes it worse: a faster temperature change, a thicker wall, a larger temperature difference, a sharper corner where the geometry concentrates the stress. Everything that reduces the gradient helps. This correctly predicts the observed pattern — steady state is safe, transients are not.
5. Design responses. Slow the transient, with a controlled cool-down schedule or a baffle preventing cold air impinging directly. Reduce the gradient by using thinner sections or a material with better thermal conductivity and a lower expansion coefficient. Improve the surface finish and add generous radii, since the crack still has to start from a flaw at a stress concentration. Where possible arrange the geometry and any prestress so the surface stays in compression, as in toughened glass, because applied tension must then first cancel that compression. And proof test or inspect after thermal cycling, treating cracks as a foreseeable maintenance item rather than a surprise.
6. Design meaning. Almost none of these are material substitutions — they are process, geometry and operating-procedure changes. That is the recurring lesson of designing with brittle materials: the specification has to cover how the part is made and how it is operated, not just what it is made of.

Q18 · Short calculationWritten

Ceramic \(\sigma_f=200\) MPa, \(E=300\) GPa, \(\alpha=8\times10^{-6}\)/°C. Compare thermal stress at \(\Delta T=200\) °C if fully constrained (\(\sigma=E\alpha\Delta T\)) to \(\sigma_f\).

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: \(\sigma=480\) MPa \(>200\) MPa — thermal shock fracture risk.
\(\sigma=300\times10^9(8\times10^{-6})(200)=480\) MPa.
Sanity check: large \(\Delta T\) on stiff low-\(\alpha\) ceramic still exceeds strength. Design meaning: allow expansion or use graded / porous microstructure.

Weibull plot: ceramic A steep slope (high \(m\)), ceramic B shallow slope (low \(m\)). Interpret scatter in strength, reliability at 150 MPa design stress, and quality control implication.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: high \(m\) — less scatter, predictable failure stress; low \(m\) — wide distribution, design must use low fractile strength. B needs more proof testing. Design meaning: ceramic design stress is statistical — not mean strength.
Q20 · Short explanationWritten

Window glass is strong in compression in a frame but shatters on impact. Explain using flaw size, stress state and toughening options (tempered / laminated).

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: surface flaws in tension open easily; frame puts edge in compression. Impact bends pane → tensile surface stress. Tempering introduces compressive surface layer; lamination contains shards. Design meaning: glass design is flaw and stress-sign management, not average strength.
