Composites

If no single material does what you need, combine two. Stiff fibres carry the load, a soft matrix holds them in place and passes load between them, and the result beats either ingredient alone. The price is that the answer now depends on which direction you pull.

What this week is really asking
How much does each ingredient contribute, and in which direction?

Two calculations carry the week. The rule of mixtures gives the stiffness, and the critical fibre length says whether the fibres are long enough to earn it. Both are straightforward; the marks are in knowing which direction the load is in.

Where this sits. This is card 9c of the core map. The rule of mixtures is the same weighted-average shape as the lever rule from Week 6, and the specific-stiffness argument comes from Week 8. Textbook sections: Callister Ch16 §16.1–16.15.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
The ingredients
Matrix—The continuous material surrounding the reinforcement. It holds fibres in place, passes load between them and protects them.
Fibre / reinforcement—The stiff, strong ingredient that carries most of the load.
\(V_f,\;V_m\)fibre and matrix volume fractionsWhat proportion of the volume each occupies. They must add to 1.
\(E_f,\;E_m\)fibre and matrix moduliThe stiffness of each ingredient on its own.
Laminate—Layers of fibre stacked at different angles and bonded together, so the composite is strong in more than one direction.
Layup—The chosen sequence of layer angles. It is a design decision, and it determines the directional properties.
Direction
Longitudinal, \(E_L\)—Along the fibres. The strong, stiff direction — the impressive number.
Transverse, \(E_T\)—Across the fibres. Much weaker, because load must pass through the matrix. This is usually what fails.
Anisotropic—Properties depend on direction. Composites are strongly anisotropic; most metals are not.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 The rule of mixtures

Along the fibres, the composite stiffness is just a weighted average of the two ingredients, weighted by how much of each there is.

\[E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m\qquad\text{with }V_f+V_m=1\]

\(E_L\) is the longitudinal modulus, \(E_f\) and \(E_m\) the fibre and matrix moduli, and \(V_f\) and \(V_m\) their volume fractions.

This is the isostrain case. Loaded along the fibres, both ingredients must stretch by the same amount because they are bonded together, so the stiffer one takes proportionally more of the stress.

A result worth noticing when you calculate one: the matrix contributes almost nothing. With \(E_f=230\) GPa and \(E_m=3\) GPa, the matrix supplies about 1% of the longitudinal stiffness. It is not there for stiffness — it is there to hold the fibres in place, transfer load between them, and stop them buckling.

Feeds: the transverse case immediately below, which is where the trouble starts.
Say it cold: "Along the fibres, stiffness is the volume-weighted average, and the matrix contributes almost none of it."

2 The transverse direction, where it fails

Pull across the fibres and the load has to go through the matrix. The stiffness collapses, and this is the number that decides whether the part survives.

\[\frac{1}{E_T}=\frac{V_f}{E_f}+\frac{V_m}{E_m}\]

The isostress case: both ingredients carry the same stress but strain differently, so the reciprocals add.

The reciprocal form is what makes the transverse result so poor. In a series arrangement the softest ingredient dominates, so the matrix — which contributed nothing longitudinally — now controls the answer almost entirely.

For the numbers used above, \(E_L\) comes out near 128 GPa and \(E_T\) near 6.5 GPa. That is a factor of twenty from the same piece of material, depending only on which way you pull it.

This is why layup orientation is the central design decision, and why a real part uses layers at several angles rather than all fibres one way. A unidirectional composite loaded off-axis will fail at a small fraction of its advertised strength.

Feeds: the failure discussion, and every real design question about laminates.
Say it cold: "Across the fibres the reciprocals add, so the soft matrix dominates and stiffness can drop twentyfold."

3 Critical fibre length

Load gets into a fibre through the matrix gripping its sides. A short fibre runs out of side before it picks up its full load, so it pulls out instead of breaking.

\[l_c=\frac{\sigma_f^{*}d}{2\tau_c}\]

\(l_c\) is the critical length, \(\sigma_f^*\) the fibre strength, \(d\) the fibre diameter and \(\tau_c\) the shear strength of the fibre–matrix interface.

The picture is a rope in a fist. Grip a short length and it slides out; grip enough and it breaks before it slides. Critical length is the crossover, and the two ways to shorten it are thinner fibres or a better-bonded interface.

What the comparison tells you
  • Fibres much longer than \(l_c\) reach full strength and behave essentially like continuous fibres.
  • Fibres around \(l_c\) reach full strength only at the very middle, so the average contribution is roughly half.
  • Fibres much shorter than \(l_c\) simply pull out. The reinforcement is largely wasted and failure is interface-controlled.

This is why chopped-fibre mouldings are so much weaker than continuous-fibre laminates using the same fibre and resin. Nothing is wrong with the ingredients — the fibres are just too short to be loaded properly.

Feeds: the choice between injection-moulded short-fibre parts and laid-up continuous-fibre parts in Week 8.
Say it cold: "Critical length is fibre strength times diameter over twice the interface shear strength; shorter fibres pull out instead of breaking."

4 Why composites win, and what they cost

Composites rarely beat metals on absolute properties. They win on properties per unit weight, and they charge for it in complexity.

Carbon-fibre composite has a longitudinal modulus around 150 GPa against steel at 210 — worse in absolute terms. But at 1.6 g/cm³ against 7.8, its specific stiffness is roughly three and a half times better. For anything that has to be light and stiff, that is decisive, and it is the Week 8 argument applied.

The costs, which examiners expect you to name
  • Anisotropy. Excellent one way, poor the others. Every load path must be known at design time.
  • Joining. You cannot weld it. Bolting cuts fibres and creates stress concentrations; bonding needs surface preparation and inspection.
  • Delamination. Layers can separate under impact or out-of-plane load, and the damage may be invisible from outside.
  • Cost and volume. Tooling and layup are expensive, so composites suit high value or high volume, rarely a one-off bracket.
Feeds: Weeks 8 and 13, where composites are usually the technically best and commercially rejected option.
Say it cold: "Composites win on stiffness per unit weight, and pay for it with anisotropy, joining difficulty, delamination and cost."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — longitudinal modulus

Given: \(E_f=230\) GPa, \(E_m=3\) GPa, \(V_f=0.55\), \(V_m=0.45\).
  1. Check the fractions: \(0.55+0.45=1\). Good.
  2. Fibre contribution: \(230\times0.55=126.5\) GPa.
  3. Matrix contribution: \(3\times0.45=1.35\) GPa.
  4. Add: \(E_L=127.85\) GPa.
  5. Notice the split — the matrix supplied 1.35 of 127.85, about 1%.
What it means: Just under 128 GPa along the fibres, and the matrix is essentially irrelevant to that number. It earns its place by holding the fibres, not by being stiff.

Worked example 2 — the same composite, pulled sideways

Given: Same values, but now loaded across the fibres.
  1. Use the reciprocal form: \(1/E_T=V_f/E_f+V_m/E_m\).
  2. Fibre term: \(0.55/230=0.00239\).
  3. Matrix term: \(0.45/3=0.15\).
  4. Sum: \(0.1524\), so \(E_T=1/0.1524\approx6.6\) GPa.
  5. Compare with 128 GPa longitudinally — about twenty times less.
What it means: Same material, same volume fractions, one rotation of the load, and a factor of twenty. The matrix term dominated the sum completely, which is exactly what the reciprocal form does.

Worked example 3 — critical fibre length

Given: \(\sigma_f^*=2.5\) GPa, \(d=12\) µm, \(\tau_c=35\) MPa.
  1. Convert to consistent SI: \(\sigma_f^*=2.5\times10^9\) Pa, \(d=12\times10^{-6}\) m, \(\tau_c=35\times10^6\) Pa.
  2. Numerator: \(2.5\times10^9\times12\times10^{-6}=3\times10^4\).
  3. Denominator: \(2\times35\times10^6=7\times10^7\).
  4. \(l_c=3\times10^4/7\times10^7=4.29\times10^{-4}\) m \(=0.429\) mm.
  5. So any fibre much shorter than about 0.43 mm cannot be loaded to full strength.
What it means: Under half a millimetre sounds tiny, but chopped fibres in injection mouldings are frequently shorter after processing damage — which is precisely why those parts underperform their ingredient list.

The weighted-average pattern again

\(E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m\) has exactly the structure of the mass balance behind the lever rule in Week 6, where \(C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta\).

Both combine two ingredient values weighted by how much of each is present. The difference is only what is unknown: composites give you the fractions and ask for the property, while phase diagrams give you the overall property and ask for the fractions.

The transverse case breaks the pattern deliberately, and that is the point worth remembering: parallel arrangements give weighted averages, series arrangements give reciprocal sums where the weakest link dominates.

Where marks get lost

  • Using \(E_L\) when the load is not along the fibres. The most expensive error this week. Check the direction before choosing the formula.
  • Forgetting the reciprocal in the transverse formula. \(1/E_T\) is the sum; you still have to invert it at the end.
  • Mixing units in the critical length calculation. Convert GPa, µm and MPa into base SI first, or the answer is out by orders of magnitude.
  • Volume fractions that do not add to 1. A free check that catches transcription errors before they propagate.
  • Presenting a composite as strictly better than metal. It wins on specific stiffness and loses on joining, delamination resistance and cost.

Check yourself

Answer out loud first, then open. An answer you can only recognise is not an answer you can write in an exam.

1. \(E_f=230\) GPa, \(E_m=3\) GPa, \(V_f=0.55\). Find the longitudinal modulus.
\(E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m=230(0.55)+3(0.45)=126.5+1.35=127.85\) GPa. The matrix contributes about 1% of the total, which shows it is present to hold and protect the fibres rather than to provide stiffness.
2. Why is layup orientation central for composite design under multiaxial loads?
Because composites are strongly anisotropic. Along the fibres the load is carried by the stiff fibres, giving a high \(E_L\); across them it must pass through the matrix, and the reciprocal relation lets the soft matrix dominate, giving an \(E_T\) perhaps twenty times lower. Under multiaxial load a single fibre direction would leave the part very weak in one axis, so layers are stacked at different angles to distribute the capability.
3. If fibres are shorter than the critical length, what happens to the reinforcement?
Load transfers into a fibre through shear at the interface along its length. Below \(l_c\) there is not enough length to build up to the fibre's tensile strength, so the fibre pulls out of the matrix instead of breaking. The reinforcement is largely wasted and failure becomes interface-controlled — which is why chopped-fibre mouldings underperform continuous laminates of the same materials.
4. Why might a composite with excellent \(E_L\) still fail in service?
Because service loads are rarely purely along the fibres. Off-axis and out-of-plane loads are carried by the matrix and interfaces, where stiffness and strength are far lower, so matrix-dominated deformation or delamination can occur well below the load the longitudinal number suggests. Impact and bolted joints are common triggers.
5. Carbon composite has \(E=150\) GPa against steel at 210. Why is it used for aircraft structure?
Because the relevant measure is stiffness per unit weight. At about 1.6 g/cm³ against steel's 7.8, the composite's specific stiffness \(E/\rho\) is roughly 94 against 27 — around three and a half times better. For weight-critical structures that dominates, provided the designer accepts anisotropy, joining complexity and delamination risk.
