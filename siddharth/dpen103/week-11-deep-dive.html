Composites

If no single material does what you need, combine two. Stiff fibres carry the load, a soft matrix holds them in place and passes load between them, and the result beats either ingredient alone. The price is that the answer now depends on which direction you pull.

What this week is really asking
How much does each ingredient contribute, and in which direction?

Two calculations carry the week. The rule of mixtures gives the stiffness, and the critical fibre length says whether the fibres are long enough to earn it. Both are straightforward; the marks are in knowing which direction the load is in.

Where this sits. This is card 9c of the core map. The rule of mixtures is the same weighted-average shape as the lever rule from Week 6, and the specific-stiffness argument comes from Week 8. Textbook sections: Callister Ch16 §16.1–16.15.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
The ingredients
Matrix—The continuous material surrounding the reinforcement. It holds fibres in place, passes load between them and protects them.
Fibre / reinforcement—The stiff, strong ingredient that carries most of the load.
\(V_f,\;V_m\)fibre and matrix volume fractionsWhat proportion of the volume each occupies. They must add to 1.
\(E_f,\;E_m\)fibre and matrix moduliThe stiffness of each ingredient on its own.
Laminate—Layers of fibre stacked at different angles and bonded together, so the composite is strong in more than one direction.
Layup—The chosen sequence of layer angles. It is a design decision, and it determines the directional properties.
Direction
Longitudinal, \(E_L\)—Along the fibres. The strong, stiff direction — the impressive number.
Transverse, \(E_T\)—Across the fibres. Much weaker, because load must pass through the matrix. This is usually what fails.
Anisotropic—Properties depend on direction. Composites are strongly anisotropic; most metals are not.
Isostrain—Loading along the fibres, where fibre and matrix stretch together by the same amount.
Isostress—Loading across the fibres, where both carry the same stress but stretch by different amounts.
Load transfer

\(\tau_c\)interfacial shear strengthHow well the matrix grips the fibre. Load transfers through this grip.
\(d\)fibre diameterThinner fibres need less length to develop full load.
Delamination—Layers of a laminate separating. A composite failure mode with no real metal equivalent.

The ideas, one card at a time

Cover each card and try the green line out loud before reading on. If it comes out, move to the next one.

1 The rule of mixtures

Along the fibres, the composite stiffness is just a weighted average of the two ingredients, weighted by how much of each there is.

\[E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m\qquad\text{with }V_f+V_m=1\]

\(E_L\) is the longitudinal modulus, \(E_f\) and \(E_m\) the fibre and matrix moduli, and \(V_f\) and \(V_m\) their volume fractions.

This is the isostrain case. Loaded along the fibres, both ingredients must stretch by the same amount because they are bonded together, so the stiffer one takes proportionally more of the stress.

A result worth noticing when you calculate one: the matrix contributes almost nothing. With \(E_f=230\) GPa and \(E_m=3\) GPa, the matrix supplies about 1% of the longitudinal stiffness. It is not there for stiffness — it is there to hold the fibres in place, transfer load between them, and stop them buckling.

Feeds: the transverse case immediately below, which is where the trouble starts.
Say it cold: "Along the fibres, stiffness is the volume-weighted average, and the matrix contributes almost none of it."

2 The transverse direction, where it fails

Pull across the fibres and the load has to go through the matrix. The stiffness collapses, and this is the number that decides whether the part survives.

\[\frac{1}{E_T}=\frac{V_f}{E_f}+\frac{V_m}{E_m}\]

The isostress case: both ingredients carry the same stress but strain differently, so the reciprocals add.

The reciprocal form is what makes the transverse result so poor. In a series arrangement the softest ingredient dominates, so the matrix — which contributed nothing longitudinally — now controls the answer almost entirely.

For the numbers used above, \(E_L\) comes out near 128 GPa and \(E_T\) near 6.5 GPa. That is a factor of twenty from the same piece of material, depending only on which way you pull it.

This is why layup orientation is the central design decision, and why a real part uses layers at several angles rather than all fibres one way. A unidirectional composite loaded off-axis will fail at a small fraction of its advertised strength.

Feeds: the failure discussion, and every real design question about laminates.
Say it cold: "Across the fibres the reciprocals add, so the soft matrix dominates and stiffness can drop twentyfold."

3 Critical fibre length

Load gets into a fibre through the matrix gripping its sides. A short fibre runs out of side before it picks up its full load, so it pulls out instead of breaking.

\[l_c=\frac{\sigma_f^{*}d}{2\tau_c}\]

\(l_c\) is the critical length, \(\sigma_f^*\) the fibre strength, \(d\) the fibre diameter and \(\tau_c\) the shear strength of the fibre–matrix interface.

The picture is a rope in a fist. Grip a short length and it slides out; grip enough and it breaks before it slides. Critical length is the crossover, and the two ways to shorten it are thinner fibres or a better-bonded interface.

What the comparison tells you
  • Fibres much longer than \(l_c\) reach full strength and behave essentially like continuous fibres.
  • Fibres around \(l_c\) reach full strength only at the very middle, so the average contribution is roughly half.
  • Fibres much shorter than \(l_c\) simply pull out. The reinforcement is largely wasted and failure is interface-controlled.

This is why chopped-fibre mouldings are so much weaker than continuous-fibre laminates using the same fibre and resin. Nothing is wrong with the ingredients — the fibres are just too short to be loaded properly.

Feeds: the choice between injection-moulded short-fibre parts and laid-up continuous-fibre parts in Week 8.
Say it cold: "Critical length is fibre strength times diameter over twice the interface shear strength; shorter fibres pull out instead of breaking."

4 Why composites win, and what they cost

Composites rarely beat metals on absolute properties. They win on properties per unit weight, and they charge for it in complexity.

Carbon-fibre composite has a longitudinal modulus around 150 GPa against steel at 210 — worse in absolute terms. But at 1.6 g/cm³ against 7.8, its specific stiffness is roughly three and a half times better. For anything that has to be light and stiff, that is decisive, and it is the Week 8 argument applied.

The costs, which examiners expect you to name
  • Anisotropy. Excellent one way, poor the others. Every load path must be known at design time.
  • Joining. You cannot weld it. Bolting cuts fibres and creates stress concentrations; bonding needs surface preparation and inspection.
  • Delamination. Layers can separate under impact or out-of-plane load, and the damage may be invisible from outside.
  • Cost and volume. Tooling and layup are expensive, so composites suit high value or high volume, rarely a one-off bracket.
Feeds: Weeks 8 and 13, where composites are usually the technically best and commercially rejected option.
Say it cold: "Composites win on stiffness per unit weight, and pay for it with anisotropy, joining difficulty, delamination and cost."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — longitudinal modulus

Given: \(E_f=230\) GPa, \(E_m=3\) GPa, \(V_f=0.55\), \(V_m=0.45\).
  1. Check the fractions: \(0.55+0.45=1\). Good.
  2. Fibre contribution: \(230\times0.55=126.5\) GPa.
  3. Matrix contribution: \(3\times0.45=1.35\) GPa.
  4. Add: \(E_L=127.85\) GPa.
  5. Notice the split — the matrix supplied 1.35 of 127.85, about 1%.
What it means: Just under 128 GPa along the fibres, and the matrix is essentially irrelevant to that number. It earns its place by holding the fibres, not by being stiff.

Worked example 2 — the same composite, pulled sideways

Given: Same values, but now loaded across the fibres.
  1. Use the reciprocal form: \(1/E_T=V_f/E_f+V_m/E_m\).
  2. Fibre term: \(0.55/230=0.00239\).
  3. Matrix term: \(0.45/3=0.15\).
  4. Sum: \(0.1524\), so \(E_T=1/0.1524\approx6.6\) GPa.
  5. Compare with 128 GPa longitudinally — about twenty times less.
What it means: Same material, same volume fractions, one rotation of the load, and a factor of twenty. The matrix term dominated the sum completely, which is exactly what the reciprocal form does.

Worked example 3 — critical fibre length

Given: \(\sigma_f^*=2.5\) GPa, \(d=12\) µm, \(\tau_c=35\) MPa.
  1. Convert to consistent SI: \(\sigma_f^*=2.5\times10^9\) Pa, \(d=12\times10^{-6}\) m, \(\tau_c=35\times10^6\) Pa.
  2. Numerator: \(2.5\times10^9\times12\times10^{-6}=3\times10^4\).
  3. Denominator: \(2\times35\times10^6=7\times10^7\).
  4. \(l_c=3\times10^4/7\times10^7=4.29\times10^{-4}\) m \(=0.429\) mm.
  5. So any fibre much shorter than about 0.43 mm cannot be loaded to full strength.
What it means: Under half a millimetre sounds tiny, but chopped fibres in injection mouldings are frequently shorter after processing damage — which is precisely why those parts underperform their ingredient list.

The weighted-average pattern again

\(E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m\) has exactly the structure of the mass balance behind the lever rule in Week 6, where \(C_0=W_\alpha C_\alpha+W_\beta C_\beta\).

Both combine two ingredient values weighted by how much of each is present. The difference is only what is unknown: composites give you the fractions and ask for the property, while phase diagrams give you the overall property and ask for the fractions.

The transverse case breaks the pattern deliberately, and that is the point worth remembering: parallel arrangements give weighted averages, series arrangements give reciprocal sums where the weakest link dominates.

Where marks get lost

  • Using \(E_L\) when the load is not along the fibres. The most expensive error this week. Check the direction before choosing the formula.
  • Forgetting the reciprocal in the transverse formula. \(1/E_T\) is the sum; you still have to invert it at the end.
  • Mixing units in the critical length calculation. Convert GPa, µm and MPa into base SI first, or the answer is out by orders of magnitude.
  • Volume fractions that do not add to 1. A free check that catches transcription errors before they propagate.
  • Presenting a composite as strictly better than metal. It wins on specific stiffness and loses on joining, delamination resistance and cost.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

In a continuous-fibre composite loaded along its fibres, what is the matrix's main structural job?

  1. To provide most of the stiffness, since it occupies most of the volume
  2. To hold the fibres in place, transfer load between them and protect them
  3. To carry the load directly, with the fibres acting only as filler
  4. To make the composite isotropic so direction stops mattering
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Along the fibres the matrix contributes almost nothing to stiffness — with \(E_f\) in the hundreds of GPa and \(E_m\) a few GPa, its share of \(E_L\) is around 1%. It earns its place by gripping the fibres so load can transfer into them through interfacial shear, distributing load between fibres when one breaks, stopping them buckling in compression, and shielding them from moisture and abrasion. Option A confuses volume fraction with stiffness contribution, which is the trap in the weighted average. Option D is the opposite of the truth: the matrix is why the transverse direction is weak, so it creates the anisotropy rather than removing it. Design meaning: matrix selection is driven by temperature limit, toughness, moisture resistance and processing route, not by its modulus.
Q2 · Multiple choiceOne step

A glass-fibre composite has \(E_f=76\) GPa, \(E_m=3.4\) GPa and \(V_f=0.40\). What is its longitudinal modulus \(E_L\)?

  1. About 12 GPa
  2. About 32 GPa
  3. About 40 GPa
  4. About 79 GPa
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Volume fractions must sum to one, so \(V_m=1-0.40=0.60\). Then \(E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m=76(0.40)+3.4(0.60)=30.4+2.04=32.44\) GPa. Sanity check: the answer must fall between the two ingredient moduli, 3.4 and 76 GPa, and it should remain below \(V_fE_f=30.4\) GPa plus only a small matrix contribution. The calculated 32.44 GPa does exactly that. Option D is what you get by adding the moduli and ignoring volume fractions altogether; option C forgets \(V_m\) and uses \(V_f=0.40\) on both terms incorrectly. Design meaning: the fibre supplied 30.4 of 32.44 GPa, so if this part is not stiff enough the lever is more fibre or better fibre, not a slightly stiffer resin.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

Which change would reduce the critical fibre length \(l_c=\sigma_f^{*}d/(2\tau_c)\)?

  1. Using a stronger fibre of the same diameter
  2. Using thinner fibres of the same material
  3. Using a matrix that grips the fibre less well
  4. Increasing the fibre volume fraction
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Critical length is proportional to fibre diameter \(d\), so thinner fibres need less embedded length to develop full load — the load transfers through shear over the fibre's surface, and a thinner fibre has more surface relative to its cross-section. Option A increases \(l_c\), because a stronger fibre needs more length to build up to its higher failure load; this is the counter-intuitive one worth remembering. Option C reduces \(\tau_c\), the interfacial shear strength, which sits in the denominator and so increases \(l_c\). Option D does not appear in the relation at all — volume fraction affects stiffness and strength, not the length needed to load one fibre. Design meaning: the two genuine levers are thinner fibres and better interfacial bonding, typically through fibre sizing or surface treatment.
Q4 · Multiple choiceTrap

A supplier quotes 1800 MPa tensile strength for a unidirectional carbon laminate. It is proposed for a bracket that is bolted at both ends and loaded at roughly 40° to the fibre direction. What is the most important objection?

  1. The quoted strength is measured along the fibres, so it is largely irrelevant to a 40° load and to bolt holes that sever fibres
  2. Carbon composites cannot reach 1800 MPa, so the figure must be wrong
  3. The strength will be fine, but the part will be too heavy
  4. Bolted joints are stronger in composites than in metals, so the design is over-conservative
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. Two problems compound. First, composites are strongly anisotropic: at 40° a large share of the load is carried by the matrix and the fibre–matrix interfaces, where strength is a small fraction of the longitudinal figure, so the quoted number does not describe this load case. Second, a bolt hole in a unidirectional laminate cuts the load-carrying fibres and creates a stress concentration in a material with no yielding available to redistribute it — you cannot weld a composite, and bolting is the joint that does the most damage. Option B is wrong on the facts, since unidirectional carbon genuinely reaches that longitudinal strength. Option C inverts the composite's main advantage. Option D is backwards — joints are a composite weakness, not a strength. Design meaning: either re-orient the layup so fibres follow the actual load path and use a multi-angle laminate, or accept a metal bracket. A quoted longitudinal strength is a property of a direction, not of a part.
Q5 · True/falseQuick

True or false: Fibre and matrix volume fractions must sum to one, and because the matrix usually occupies more of the volume it supplies most of the longitudinal stiffness.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False — the first half is true, the second is not, and a statement is only true if all of it is true. Volume fractions do sum to one, and checking that is a free way to catch a transcription error. But the longitudinal modulus is \(E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m\), which weights each ingredient by its own stiffness as well as its amount. With \(E_f=76\) GPa at \(V_f=0.40\) against \(E_m=3.4\) GPa at \(V_m=0.60\), the matrix contributes 2.04 of 32.44 GPa — about 6% — despite occupying 60% of the volume. Design meaning: volume share and property share are different things, and this is exactly the trap that composite questions are built around.
Q6 · True/falseQuick

True or false: Stacking laminate layers at several angles sacrifices some peak longitudinal stiffness in exchange for more balanced properties in other directions.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. A unidirectional layup puts every fibre in one direction, which maximises \(E_L\) along that axis and leaves the transverse direction matrix-dominated and weak. Placing some layers at other angles means fewer fibres aligned with the primary axis, so the primary-direction stiffness drops — but the off-axis directions gain fibre reinforcement instead of relying on the matrix alone. Trend check: the total amount of fibre has not changed, so what the layup really does is redistribute a fixed capability between directions rather than create more of it. Design meaning: the layup is a design decision that must follow the actual load paths, and it is where the honesty of the load case gets tested. Real parts see off-axis load, out-of-plane load, handling and impact, so an unbalanced layup is only defensible when every load direction is genuinely known.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

For the same composite as Q2 — \(E_f=76\) GPa, \(E_m=3.4\) GPa, \(V_f=0.40\) — calculate the transverse modulus \(E_T\), compare it with \(E_L=32.44\) GPa, and explain why the two differ so much.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: \(E_T\approx5.5\) GPa, about six times lower than \(E_L\).
1. Choose the right relation. Loading across the fibres is the isostress case — both ingredients carry the same stress but strain differently — so the reciprocals add: \(1/E_T=V_f/E_f+V_m/E_m\).
2. Fibre term. \(0.40/76=0.005263\) GPa⁻¹.
3. Matrix term. \(0.60/3.4=0.176471\) GPa⁻¹.
4. Sum and invert. \(1/E_T=0.181734\) GPa⁻¹, so \(E_T=1/0.181734=5.50\) GPa. Remember the inversion — stopping at the sum is the most common error here.
5. Sanity check. \(E_T\) must lie between the two ingredient moduli, and 5.50 GPa sits between 3.4 and 76 GPa. It should also sit much closer to the matrix value, which it does. Ratio \(E_L/E_T=32.44/5.50=5.9\).
6. Why the difference. Look at the two terms in step 2 and 3: the matrix term is 34 times the fibre term and dominates the sum almost completely. That is what a reciprocal sum does — it behaves like elements in series, where the softest link controls the result. Longitudinally the ingredients act in parallel and the stiffest one dominates; transversely they act in series and the softest one does. Same material, same volume fractions, opposite outcome.
7. Design meaning. One rotation of the load and the stiffness falls sixfold, and for a carbon system the factor is nearer twenty. Never quote a single modulus for a composite, and never use \(E_L\) without first confirming the load really is along the fibres.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A short-fibre moulding uses fibres of strength \(\sigma_f^{*}=3.4\) GPa and diameter \(d=15\) µm in a matrix giving interfacial shear strength \(\tau_c=40\) MPa. Calculate the critical fibre length. Process inspection shows the fibres average 0.25 mm after moulding. Comment.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: \(l_c\approx0.64\) mm, so the 0.25 mm fibres are well below critical and will pull out rather than break.
1. Convert everything to base SI units first, or the answer will be out by orders of magnitude: \(\sigma_f^{*}=3.4\times10^{9}\) Pa, \(d=15\times10^{-6}\) m, \(\tau_c=40\times10^{6}\) Pa.
2. Numerator. \(\sigma_f^{*}d=3.4\times10^{9}\times15\times10^{-6}=5.1\times10^{4}\) N/m.
3. Denominator. \(2\tau_c=2\times40\times10^{6}=8.0\times10^{7}\) Pa.
4. Divide. \(l_c=5.1\times10^{4}/8.0\times10^{7}=6.375\times10^{-4}\) m \(=0.64\) mm.
5. Compare. The actual fibres are 0.25 mm, which is about 39% of \(l_c\). Load enters a fibre through shear along its sides, and there is simply not enough embedded length to build the stress up to 3.4 GPa before the interface gives way. The fibres therefore slide out of the matrix instead of fracturing, so most of the reinforcement's capability is never used and failure is controlled by the interface rather than by the fibre.
6. Sanity check. The result is a fraction of a millimetre, which is the right order for a fine fibre with a decent interface. If it had come out in metres, suspect the µm conversion; if in micrometres, suspect the GPa conversion.
7. Design meaning. This explains why a chopped-fibre injection moulding badly underperforms a continuous laminate made from the identical fibre and resin — nothing is wrong with the ingredients, the fibres are just too short to be loaded. The fixes follow straight from the equation: thinner fibres or a better-bonded interface to lower \(l_c\), or a gentler process and gating that breaks the fibres less to raise the actual length. Do not quote fibre strength in a design calculation until you have confirmed the fibres are long enough to reach it.
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

A figure plots the measured modulus of a unidirectional laminate on the vertical axis against the angle between load and fibre direction on the horizontal axis, from 0° to 90°. The curve starts high, drops very steeply over the first 15°, then flattens into a long low tail out to 90°. Explain the shape and what it means for a designer who expects "about 10° of misalignment, which should be fine".

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: the curve is steepest exactly where the designer assumes tolerance exists, so 10° of misalignment is not a small effect — it removes a large fraction of the stiffness.
The 0° end. At zero degrees the loading is pure isostrain: fibre and matrix stretch together, and \(E_L=E_fV_f+E_mV_m\) applies, with the stiff fibres dominating. This is the advertised number.
Why it falls so fast. As the angle grows, the load stops being carried purely as tension along the fibres and increasingly has to be transmitted as shear in the matrix and across the fibre–matrix interface. Because the matrix modulus is perhaps twenty times lower, even a modest angle diverts load into a far more compliant path. The steepness is not a measurement artefact — it is the geometric consequence of a very large mismatch between the two ingredients.
The 90° tail. Here the loading is isostress, \(1/E_T=V_f/E_f+V_m/E_m\), the reciprocal sum lets the soft matrix dominate, and the curve flattens because the response is now essentially matrix-controlled. Once the matrix governs, further rotation changes little.
The answer to "10° should be fine". It is precisely the wrong intuition. The curve's gradient is at its maximum in the first 10–15°, so this is the least forgiving region of the whole range. Off-axis strength falls even faster than stiffness, because matrix and interface strengths are lower still and off-axis loading introduces shear at the interface.
Reading skill to carry into the exam. On any property-versus-angle or property-versus-fraction curve, look at the gradient where your design sits, not just the value. A high value on a steep slope is a fragile design; a slightly lower value on a flat region is often the better engineering choice.
Design meaning. Either control fibre alignment tightly, with the tolerance stated on the drawing and verified in production, or design the layup so no direction relies on near-perfect alignment — which is exactly why real parts use layers at several angles instead of all fibres one way.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

A weight-critical panel is currently aluminium: \(E=70\) GPa, density 2.7 g/cm³. A supplier proposes the glass composite from Q2, \(E_L=32.4\) GPa at 1.9 g/cm³, arguing that "composites are lighter". Assess the claim.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: the claim fails on its own measure. On specific stiffness the aluminium is better, roughly 26 against 17 in GPa per g/cm³, so this substitution would make a stiffness-limited panel heavier, not lighter.
1. Classify. Weight-critical and stiffness-driven, so the comparison is specific stiffness \(E/\rho\), not modulus and not density separately.
2. Aluminium. \(70/2.7=25.9\) GPa/(g/cm³).
3. Glass composite, along the fibres. \(32.4/1.9=17.1\) GPa/(g/cm³).
4. Compare. The aluminium is about 1.5 times better, and this is the composite's best direction. Transversely, using \(E_T=5.5\) GPa, specific stiffness drops to about 2.9 — roughly a ninth of the aluminium.
5. Where the supplier's intuition came from. The famous comparison is carbon fibre, around 150 GPa at 1.6 g/cm³, giving about 94 — some 3.6 times better than aluminium. That result belongs to carbon at a high fibre fraction, not to glass at \(V_f=0.40\). Lower density alone does not deliver a lighter part, because stiffness fell proportionally more than density did.
6. Trend check. Substituting a lower-density material into a stiffness-limited design forces a thicker section to restore the required deflection performance, and if specific stiffness is worse the extra thickness costs more mass than the lower density saved. The result of the swap would be a heavier panel.
7. What else the swap would cost. Even if the numbers had favoured the composite, four objections still need answering: anisotropy, so every load path must be known at design time; joining, since you cannot weld it and bolting cuts fibres; delamination after impact, which can be invisible from the outside and needs a real inspection method; and tooling and layup cost, which suits high value or high volume rather than a one-off panel.
8. Design meaning. Reject the substitution as proposed. If weight really is the binding constraint, the defensible route is carbon fibre at a high fibre fraction with a layup matched to the load paths and an inspection plan — and then the argument is made on specific stiffness with numbers, not on the category name "composite".
