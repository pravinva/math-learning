Polymers: Structure and Properties

Polymers behave unlike metals in ways that catch designers out. They creep at room temperature, their stiffness can drop by a factor of a thousand over a few degrees, and the same chemistry can give you a rigid pipe or a rubber band. All of it comes back to long chains and how free they are to move.

What this week is really asking
How much freedom do these chains have at the temperature this part will actually work at?

One question runs through the whole week: where is the service temperature relative to \(T_g\)? Answer that and you can predict stiffness, ductility, impact behaviour and creep. Any polymer discussion that does not state the service temperature is incomplete.

Where this sits. This is card 9a of the core map, a branch rather than a foundation. It rests on secondary bonding from Week 1 and inherits creep from Week 5, where polymers are the materials that creep in a warm room. Textbook sections: Callister Ch14 §14.1–14.12, Ch15 §15.1–15.16.

The words and symbols, in plain English

Everything this week uses, spelled out before it is used. Come back here whenever a symbol stops making sense.

Short formWhat it stands forWhat it actually means
Chains
Monomer—The single small molecule that gets repeated. Ethylene is the monomer for polyethylene.
Polymer—The long chain built by joining thousands of monomers end to end.
Repeat unit—One link of the chain. Its mass is the \(m\) in the degree of polymerisation.
\(DP_n\)degree of polymerisationHow many repeat units are in an average chain. Bigger means longer chains.
\(M_n\)number-average molecular weightThe average mass of one chain, in grams per mole. Polymers have a spread of chain lengths, so this is an average rather than a fixed value.
Entanglement—Long chains physically tangling like cooked spaghetti. It is what gives a polymer strength without any chemical bonding between chains.
Architecture
Linear—Straight chains, free to slide past each other. Generally flexible and meltable.
Branched—Side chains sticking out, preventing tight packing. Lowers density and crystallinity.
Cross-linked—Chains chemically tied to each other, so they cannot slide at all.
Thermoplastic—Not cross-linked, so it can be melted and reshaped over and over. Most recyclable plastics.
Thermoset—Cross-linked, so it can never be re-melted — heat it enough and it degrades instead. Epoxy, vulcanised rubber.
Crystallinity, \(X_c\)—The fraction of the polymer where chains have packed into an orderly arrangement. More crystallinity means stiffer, stronger, denser and less transparent.
Temperatures
\(T_g\)glass transition temperatureWhere a polymer changes from hard and glassy to soft and rubbery. It is still solid on both sides — this is not melting.
\(T_m\)melting temperatureWhere the crystalline regions actually turn liquid. Only crystalline polymers have a real one.
Viscoelastic—Behaving partly like a solid and partly like a thick liquid, so the response depends on how long and how fast you load it.
Stress relaxation—Hold a polymer at fixed stretch and the stress needed to hold it slowly falls. The mirror image of creep.

The ideas, one card at a time

1 Chain length and why it matters

Polymer properties depend heavily on how long the chains are, and chain length is the one thing you can quantify simply.

\[DP_n=\frac{M_n}{m}\]

\(DP_n\) is the degree of polymerisation, \(M_n\) the average molecular weight of a chain in g/mol, and \(m\) the molecular weight of one repeat unit.

Longer chains tangle more, and entanglement is what stops chains from simply sliding apart under load. So longer chains generally mean higher strength and much higher toughness.

The catch is processing. The same entanglement that gives toughness makes the melt thick and difficult to force through a mould. Polymer engineering lives on this trade-off, and a question asking why you would not simply use the longest chains available is asking about melt viscosity.

Feeds: the processing discussion, and any question about why a grade was chosen for injection moulding rather than strength alone.
Say it cold: "Degree of polymerisation is chain molecular weight over repeat-unit weight; longer chains tangle more, giving toughness but a thicker melt."

2 Glass transition — the property that dominates everything

Cross \(T_g\) and the material changes character completely. Stiffness can fall by three orders of magnitude over a narrow temperature range, and nothing about the chemistry has changed.

Below \(T_g\) the chains are frozen in place. The polymer is glassy: stiff, strong and brittle. Above \(T_g\) the chains can wriggle past each other. The polymer becomes rubbery: compliant, extensible and tough.

This is not melting. Both sides of \(T_g\) are solid. Melting at \(T_m\) is a separate, higher event affecting the crystalline regions, and a fully amorphous polymer has no real \(T_m\) at all.

The comparison that makes it concrete
  • Polystyrene has \(T_g\approx100\) °C. At room temperature it is well below \(T_g\), so a CD case is rigid and cracks rather than bends.
  • Natural rubber has \(T_g\approx-70\) °C. At room temperature it is far above \(T_g\), so it is floppy and stretchy.
  • Same room, opposite behaviour, and the only thing that differs is which side of \(T_g\) the room happens to be on.

This also explains cold-weather failures. Drop a polymer part below its \(T_g\) and something that was tough all summer becomes brittle, which has caused real engineering accidents.

Feeds: every polymer selection decision, and the impact-resistance discussion in Weeks 8 and 13.
Say it cold: "Below T-g the chains are frozen so it is glassy and brittle; above T-g they can move so it is rubbery and tough, and it is still solid on both sides."

3 Architecture and crystallinity

How the chains are arranged relative to each other matters as much as what they are made of. Two samples of identical chemistry can be a flexible film or a rigid solid.

  • Linear chains slide past each other, so the polymer is flexible and can be melted and reshaped.
  • Branched chains cannot pack tightly, which lowers crystallinity and density. This is exactly the difference between low- and high-density polyethylene.
  • Cross-linked chains are chemically tied together and cannot slide at all. This gives a thermoset, which cannot be re-melted at any temperature.
\[X_c=\frac{\rho_c(\rho_s-\rho_a)}{\rho_s(\rho_c-\rho_a)}\]

Crystallinity from density: \(\rho_s\) is the measured sample density, \(\rho_c\) the density of fully crystalline material and \(\rho_a\) of fully amorphous. It works because crystalline regions pack more tightly and are therefore denser.

More crystallinity means stiffer, stronger, denser and less transparent — the last because crystal regions scatter light, which is why a milky bottle is more crystalline than a clear one of the same plastic.

Feeds: Week 11, where the polymer matrix in a composite is chosen on exactly these grounds.
Say it cold: "Linear slides and melts, branched packs poorly, cross-linked cannot melt at all; more crystallinity means stiffer, denser and cloudier."

4 Viscoelasticity — time is a variable

A polymer response depends on how long you load it and how fast. A metal does not care; a polymer very much does.

  • Creep — under constant load, a polymer keeps stretching. Unlike metals this happens at room temperature, over weeks, at ordinary stresses.
  • Stress relaxation — held at constant stretch, the stress needed to hold it decays away. This is why a plastic clip loses its grip and a bolted plastic joint loosens itself.
  • Rate sensitivity — load it fast and it behaves stiff and brittle; load it slowly and it behaves soft and ductile. The same part, the same temperature, different answer.

The design consequence is that a single stiffness value is not enough for a polymer. You need to know the load duration. A shelf sized on the short-term modulus will be visibly sagging in a year.

Feeds: Week 8, where any polymer under sustained load needs creep checked explicitly rather than assumed away.
Say it cold: "Polymers creep under constant load and relax under constant stretch, both at room temperature, so load duration is part of the design."

Worked examples, every step shown

Units on every line. Predict the direction of the answer before you calculate, then check the result against that prediction.

Worked example 1 — degree of polymerisation

Given: \(M_n=120{,}000\) g/mol, repeat unit \(m=100\) g/mol.
  1. \(DP_n=M_n/m=120{,}000/100=1200\).
  2. So an average chain is about 1200 repeat units long.
  3. That is long enough for substantial entanglement, so expect good toughness.
  4. And expect a viscous melt that needs higher temperature or pressure to mould.
What it means: The number itself is one line. The marks are in the interpretation — what 1200 units implies for both properties and processing.

Worked example 2 — the service temperature argument

Given: Polymer A has \(T_g=80\) °C. Polymer B has \(T_g=-20\) °C. Both operate at 25 °C.
  1. Polymer A: service temperature is 55 °C below \(T_g\), so the chains are frozen. Glassy — stiff, strong, brittle on impact.
  2. Polymer B: service temperature is 45 °C above \(T_g\), so the chains are mobile. Rubbery — compliant, extensible, tough.
  3. One comparison, and you have predicted stiffness, ductility and impact behaviour for both.
  4. Now check the extremes. If the part might see −30 °C, polymer B drops below its own \(T_g\) and turns brittle exactly when it is coldest.
What it means: This single comparison is the highest-value move in any polymer question. Always state the service temperature relative to \(T_g\), and always check the temperature extremes rather than just the nominal.

Worked example 3 — why a plastic bracket sags

A polymer bracket is sized using the published modulus and passes the deflection check. A year later it is visibly drooping. What was missed?

  1. The published modulus is normally a short-term value, measured in a test lasting minutes.
  2. Polymers are viscoelastic, so under sustained load they creep at room temperature.
  3. The effective modulus after a year of loading is far lower than the short-term figure.
  4. The correct approach is a creep or apparent modulus for the intended service duration, not the headline number.
What it means: This is the classic polymer design error and a favourite exam scenario. For a metal the short-term modulus would have been fine; for a polymer, load duration has to be part of the calculation.

Where marks get lost

  • Discussing a polymer without stating the service temperature relative to \(T_g\). Nothing else can be predicted until that is fixed.
  • Treating \(T_g\) as melting. The polymer is solid on both sides of \(T_g\). Melting is \(T_m\), and it is higher.
  • Using a short-term modulus for a long-term load. Polymers creep at room temperature; a metal-style calculation will under-predict deflection badly.
  • Saying a thermoset can be recycled by melting. Cross-links make that impossible — it degrades rather than melts.
  • Confusing branching with cross-linking. Branches stick out from one chain; cross-links tie separate chains together. Only the second prevents melting.

Exam practice — 10 questions

Four multiple choice, two true/false, and four short calculations or explanations. Answer before opening the worked solution.

Q1 · Multiple choiceQuick

Which statement about the glass transition temperature \(T_g\) of a polymer is correct?

  1. It is the temperature at which the polymer turns from solid into liquid.
  2. The polymer is solid on both sides of it; below it the chains are frozen and above it they can move past each other.
  3. It is always higher than the melting temperature \(T_m\).
  4. It applies only to fully crystalline polymers.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. \(T_g\), the glass transition temperature, is where a polymer changes from hard and glassy to soft and rubbery. Below it, chain motion is frozen out and the material is stiff, strong and brittle; above it the chains can wriggle past one another and it becomes compliant, extensible and tough. Crucially it is still solid on both sides — this is not melting. A and C confuse it with \(T_m\), the melting temperature, which is a separate and higher event affecting the crystalline regions. D is backwards: a fully amorphous polymer has a \(T_g\) but no real \(T_m\). Design meaning: stiffness can fall by around three orders of magnitude across \(T_g\) with no change in chemistry, so the service temperature relative to \(T_g\) is the first thing to establish about any polymer part.
Q2 · Multiple choiceQuick

Low-density polyethylene (LDPE) and high-density polyethylene (HDPE) have the same repeat unit, yet LDPE is less dense, less crystalline and more flexible. Which structural difference explains this?

  1. LDPE chains are cross-linked to each other, so they cannot pack.
  2. LDPE has side branches sticking out from the chains, which prevent tight packing.
  3. LDPE chains are much shorter, so there is less entanglement.
  4. LDPE contains a different monomer with a larger molecular weight.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: B. Branching is the difference. Side chains sticking out of the backbone get in the way of neighbouring chains, so the chains cannot fold into ordered, closely packed crystalline regions. Lower crystallinity means lower density and lower stiffness, and more transparency, because it is the crystalline regions that scatter light. A is the standard confusion to avoid: branches stick out from a single chain, whereas cross-links chemically tie separate chains together — only cross-linking prevents melting and makes a thermoset. C and D are wrong because both grades share the same monomer and both have chains long enough to entangle. Design meaning: the same chemistry can be processed into a squeezable film or a rigid pipe, so quoting "polyethylene" without the grade does not specify a material.
Q3 · Multiple choiceOne step

A grade has a degree of polymerisation \(DP_n=2{,}500\) and a repeat unit of molecular weight \(m=62\) g/mol. What is its number-average molecular weight \(M_n\)?

  1. 40.3 g/mol
  2. 2,562 g/mol
  3. 155,000 g/mol
  4. 1,550 g/mol
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: C. The relationship is \(DP_n=M_n/m\), where \(DP_n\) is the degree of polymerisation (how many repeat units in an average chain) and \(M_n\) the number-average molecular weight (the average mass of one chain, in g/mol). Rearranged: \(M_n=DP_n\times m=2{,}500\times62\text{ g/mol}=155{,}000\) g/mol. Option A divides instead of multiplying. Sanity check: \(M_n\) must be far larger than the repeat unit mass, since a chain is thousands of units long — an answer of the same order as \(m\) means the formula was inverted. Design meaning: 2,500 units is a long chain, so expect heavy entanglement and good toughness, paid for with a viscous melt that is harder to injection mould.
Q4 · Multiple choiceOne step

For an amorphous thermoplastic, stiffness is plotted on a logarithmic vertical axis against temperature on the horizontal axis. The curve shows a high, nearly flat plateau, then a fall of about three orders of magnitude over a narrow temperature band, then a much lower plateau, and finally a steep drop to nothing. What does the narrow band of steep fall correspond to?

  1. The glass transition, \(T_g\).
  2. Melting of the crystalline regions, \(T_m\).
  3. Thermal degradation of the chains.
  4. The onset of cross-linking.
Show answer and reasoning
Answer: A. Read the plot as three behaviours separated by two events. The upper plateau is glassy behaviour with frozen chains. The narrow, very steep fall is the glass transition, where chain mobility switches on and the stiffness collapses by roughly a factor of a thousand — the signature magnitude for \(T_g\). The lower plateau is rubbery behaviour, and the final drop to zero stiffness is where the material flows outright. B is wrong because this polymer is amorphous, so it has no crystalline regions to melt, and in any case that event would sit above the rubbery plateau rather than at the first fall. Design meaning: a single quoted modulus is meaningless without the temperature it was measured at, because on this curve the value depends entirely on which side of \(T_g\) the measurement was taken.
Q5 · True/falseTrap

True or false: The same polymer part, at the same temperature, behaves stiffer and more brittle when the load is applied quickly than when it is applied slowly.

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: True. This is rate sensitivity, one face of viscoelasticity — behaving partly like a solid and partly like a thick liquid. Deforming by chain motion takes time. Load the part fast and the chains cannot rearrange within the loading time, so the response is glassy: stiff, strong and brittle. Load it slowly and the chains have time to move, so it responds softer and more ductile. Nothing about the material or the temperature has changed; only the time available has. A metal is essentially indifferent to this, which is why the behaviour catches designers out. Design meaning: a polymer part must be checked against both the slow load case, for deflection and creep, and the fast one, for impact — a single test at one rate cannot cover both.
Q6 · True/falseQuick

True or false: A fully amorphous polymer has a well-defined melting temperature \(T_m\).

Show answer and reasoning
Answer: False. \(T_m\) is the temperature at which ordered, crystalline regions lose their order and turn liquid. A fully amorphous polymer has no crystalline regions, so there is nothing to melt at a definite temperature — it simply softens progressively above its \(T_g\) and eventually flows over a range. Only crystalline and semi-crystalline polymers have a real \(T_m\). Sanity framing: the two temperatures answer different questions — \(T_g\) is about chain mobility in the disordered regions and every polymer has one, while \(T_m\) is about crystalline order and only some do. Design meaning: for an amorphous grade the useful upper service limit is set by \(T_g\), not by a melting point, because the stiffness has already collapsed well before anything flows.
Q7 · Short calculationWritten

A polyethylene sample has measured density \(\rho_s=0.955\) g/cm³. Fully crystalline material has \(\rho_c=1.000\) g/cm³ and fully amorphous material \(\rho_a=0.870\) g/cm³. Using \(X_c=\dfrac{\rho_c(\rho_s-\rho_a)}{\rho_s(\rho_c-\rho_a)}\), find the percentage crystallinity, check it is believable, and state two property consequences.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: \(X_c\approx0.68\), that is about 68% crystalline.
Step 1 — numerator. \(\rho_c(\rho_s-\rho_a)=1.000(0.955-0.870)=1.000\times0.085=0.0850\) (g/cm³)².
Step 2 — denominator. \(\rho_s(\rho_c-\rho_a)=0.955(1.000-0.870)=0.955\times0.130=0.1242\) (g/cm³)².
Step 3 — divide. \(X_c=0.0850/0.1242=0.684\), so about 68%. The units cancel, as they must for a fraction.
Sanity check. The result has to lie between 0 and 1, and it should lean the way the density does. The sample at 0.955 g/cm³ sits much closer to the crystalline end (1.000) than to the amorphous end (0.870): it is \(0.085/0.130=65\%\) of the way up on a simple linear reading, which is close to the 68% the formula gives. Agreement between the rough linear estimate and the formula confirms no arithmetic slip. A result above 1 or below 0 would mean \(\rho_s\) was placed outside the two reference densities.
Two property consequences. Higher crystallinity means the chains are tightly and regularly packed, so the material is stiffer and stronger, and it resists creep better because ordered regions restrict chain motion. It is also less transparent, because the crystalline regions scatter light — so this grade would look milky rather than clear.
Design meaning: crystallinity works because crystalline regions pack more densely, which is exactly why a simple density measurement is enough to infer it. If the part must be both clear and stiff, those two requirements pull crystallinity in opposite directions and one has to give.
Self-mark: one point for correct substitution into the formula; one for the result as a percentage; one for a check that the value lies between 0 and 1 and leans with the density; one for two consequences, at least one of which links opacity to the same cause as stiffness.
Q8 · Short calculationWritten

A polymer shelf is sized using the datasheet short-term modulus \(E=2.4\) GPa and its predicted deflection is 3.0 mm, inside a 5.0 mm allowable. Under sustained load the apparent (creep) modulus after one year falls to 0.8 GPa. Deflection is inversely proportional to modulus for a fixed geometry and load. (a) Find the deflection after one year. (b) Say whether the design passes. (c) Give two ways to fix it.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: about 9.0 mm after a year, which fails the 5.0 mm limit by a wide margin.
(a) With deflection \(\delta\propto1/E\) at fixed geometry and load, \(\dfrac{\delta_{1\text{ yr}}}{\delta_{\text{short}}}=\dfrac{E_{\text{short}}}{E_{1\text{ yr}}}=\dfrac{2.4\text{ GPa}}{0.8\text{ GPa}}=3.0\). So \(\delta_{1\text{ yr}}=3.0\times3.0\text{ mm}=9.0\) mm.
(b) 9.0 mm against a 5.0 mm allowable — the design fails, and by a factor of 1.8 rather than marginally. Note that the original calculation was not wrong arithmetically; it used a modulus measured in a test lasting minutes to predict behaviour over a year.
(c) Two fixes. First, size the part on an apparent modulus quoted for the intended load duration rather than the short-term value — the honest calculation from the start. Second, recover the lost stiffness geometrically: since deflection falls as the second moment of area rises, adding ribs or increasing the depth so that the second moment triples restores the 3.0 mm figure at the one-year modulus. A third route is a stiffer, more creep-resistant grade — higher crystallinity, cross-linked, or fibre-filled.
Sanity check: the modulus dropped by a factor of 3, so with a linear relationship the deflection must rise by exactly the same factor of 3. If the answer had come out less than 3.0 mm, the ratio was inverted.
Design meaning: this is the classic polymer error. Load duration is part of the specification, not a detail — for a metal the short-term modulus would have been perfectly adequate.
Self-mark: one point for the modulus ratio applied the right way round; one for the 9.0 mm result compared with the limit; one for identifying the use of a short-term modulus as the cause; one for two genuine fixes, at least one of which is geometric or material rather than simply "use a stronger plastic".
Q9 · Diagram interpretationWritten

A datasheet plots creep strain (vertical axis) against time on a logarithmic horizontal axis, for one polymer at one fixed stress, with one curve measured at 20 °C and another at 60 °C. The polymer has \(T_g=75\) °C. Describe how the two curves differ, explain the difference in terms of chain mobility, and state what it means for a bracket that will sit in a 60 °C enclosure.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: the 60 °C curve sits above the 20 °C curve at every time and rises more steeply, because the chains are far closer to the temperature at which they become mobile.
Reading the plot. Both curves start at a small instantaneous strain and climb continuously with no flat portion — creep never stops while the load is applied. The 60 °C curve is displaced upward throughout and its slope on the log-time axis is steeper, so the gap between the curves widens as time goes on. Equivalently, read it horizontally: the strain the part reaches after years at 20 °C is reached in a small fraction of that time at 60 °C.
Why, in terms of chain mobility. Creep in a polymer is chains slowly sliding and uncoiling past one another. Both temperatures are below \(T_g=75\) °C, so both are nominally glassy, but 60 °C is only 15 °C below the transition while 20 °C is 55 °C below it. Nearer \(T_g\) the chains have much more freedom to rearrange, so the same stress produces the same strain far sooner. This is the same physics as the loading-rate effect, seen through temperature instead of time.
What it means for the bracket. A 60 °C enclosure is a hostile environment for this grade even though it never reaches \(T_g\) or melts. The design must use creep data read at 60 °C and at the full service duration, not room-temperature data, and the deflection allowance must be checked against the strain the 60 °C curve reaches at the end of life. If that fails, the options are a grade with a higher \(T_g\), a stiffer or more crystalline grade, a lower design stress through a larger section, or cooling the enclosure.
Trend check: the hotter curve must be the upper one. If a plot appears to show less creep at higher temperature, the curve labels have been swapped.
Self-mark: one point for the hotter curve being higher and steeper with a widening gap; one for the explanation via proximity to \(T_g\) and chain mobility; one for insisting on creep data at the service temperature and duration; one for a stated design response.
Q10 · Short explanationWritten

An outdoor equipment housing must work between −20 °C and +40 °C and survive being dropped. Polymer P has \(T_g=105\) °C; polymer Q has \(T_g=-45\) °C. A third proposal is a cross-linked epoxy, argued for on the grounds that offcuts could be re-melted and reused like the thermoplastic ones. Recommend a polymer with reasons, and comment on the recycling claim.

Show worked solution and self-mark guide
Answer: neither P nor Q is comfortable on its own — P is glassy and impact-prone across the whole range, Q is rubbery throughout — and the epoxy recycling claim is simply false.
Polymer P. The service range runs from 125 °C to 65 °C below its \(T_g\), so P is deeply glassy at every service temperature. It will be stiff and strong, which suits a housing, but the chains are frozen and it has no mechanism to absorb impact energy by deforming, so a drop is likely to crack it — worst at −20 °C, the coldest and most glassy condition.
Polymer Q. The range runs from 25 °C to 85 °C above its \(T_g\), so Q is rubbery throughout. It is tough and will survive being dropped, but it is far too compliant to hold the shape and stiffness a housing needs.
The recommendation. Neither extreme is right, and the useful answer is a material whose \(T_g\) sits well above +40 °C for stiffness, but which is toughened so it can still absorb impact — a rubber-toughened or impact-modified grade, where a dispersed rubbery phase with a very low \(T_g\) provides energy absorption inside a glassy matrix. Whatever the choice, verify the impact performance at −20 °C rather than at room temperature, because that is where the material is most brittle.
The recycling claim. Cross-links are chemical bonds tying separate chains to each other, so the chains cannot slide at all. That makes the epoxy a thermoset: it cannot be re-melted at any temperature. Heat it enough and it degrades — chars and breaks down chemically — rather than flowing. Offcuts from a thermoset cannot be reprocessed the way thermoplastic offcuts can, so the claim is wrong. Note the distinction from branching: branches stick out from one chain and hinder packing, but only cross-links between chains prevent melting.
Design meaning: every polymer discussion has to state the service temperature relative to \(T_g\) before anything else, and the temperature extremes matter more than the nominal — a housing that is tough in summer and shatters in winter has still failed.
Self-mark: one point for positioning both service extremes relative to each \(T_g\); one for the resulting stiffness and impact predictions for P and Q; one for a recommendation that resolves the stiffness-versus-toughness conflict and specifies testing at −20 °C; one for correctly rejecting the thermoset recycling claim with the cross-link reason.
